# Food

**Earth research anchors** for medieval / early Renaissance tables. Use when writing common inns, estate meals or feast scenes. Map flavor to Caldris; do not paste Earth month names or trade cities into prose as if they are local.

**Caldris calendar:** **13 months × 28 days** (`Economy.md`). Earth month labels below (e.g. November / Martinmas) are anchors only.

**Caldris seasoning lock:** common tables = **salt** (+ local herbs/onion). **Pepper** is middle-class / merchant luxury (thin use can still show up and raise a restaurant bill). **Sugar** is rare spice / medicine / feast (nobility); honey does ordinary sweetening. Do not write commoner kitchens as “mostly sugar, pepper and salt.”

## Peasant / common food

- Daily staple was **pottage**: a thick stew of grains, peas, beans and whatever vegetables were on hand (onions, leeks, cabbage, turnips, parsnips), simmered for hours over the hearth.
- Bread was dark and dense, usually rye, barley or **maslin** (mixed grain). White wheat bread cost more to mill and bake.
- Meat was occasional rather than daily. Salt pork and bacon were the most common forms. Fresh meat showed up at holidays or when an animal was slaughtered.
- Fish (usually salted or dried herring or cod) filled in on the many fast days the church calendar demanded, roughly a third of the year, when meat was forbidden. *(Earth church-fast rhythm; map to Solaria / local faith fasting if a scene needs it.)*
- Cheese, butter and eggs supplied most of the protein between slaughters.
- Ale (weak, low-alcohol **small beer**) was the everyday drink for the whole household including children, since it was safer than most water sources.

## Middle class (craftsmen, merchants, yeomen)

- Noticeably more meat variety: beef, mutton, poultry, pork in rotation rather than salt pork alone.
- White wheat bread became affordable more often, though still a step below the finest **manchet** loaves of the wealthy.
- Wine appeared alongside ale and pepper or other cheaper spices showed up in cooking.

## Nobility and the wealthy

- Feasts centered on roasted meats: venison, boar, swan, peacock and other game showing off hunting rights and status.
- Spices were the real luxury: pepper, cinnamon, saffron, cloves and ginger, imported overland and by sea at enormous markup. *(Earth anchor: Venetian spice trade; in Caldris use long caravan / sea routes through merchant cities, not Venice.)* Heavy spicing signaled wealth more than flavor preference.
- Sugar functioned as a rare spice and medicine rather than an everyday sweetener. Honey did the ordinary sweetening.
- Sauces were thickened with breadcrumbs and sharpened with **verjuice** (juice of unripe grapes or apples), vinegar or wine. Medieval taste favored sweet-sour combinations over the sweet/savory separation common today.
- **Trenchers** (thick stale bread slabs used as plates) soaked up drippings and sauce, then went to servants or the poor after the meal.
- Banquets ran to many courses with sweet and savory dishes mixed through the sequence rather than dessert coming last, sometimes finished with elaborate sugar or marzipan **subtleties** sculpted into castles, animals or heraldic scenes.

## Renaissance shift

- *(Earth anchor.)* The medieval and Renaissance periods split roughly around the **Columbian Exchange**. Before it, European kitchens had no potatoes, tomatoes, maize, peppers, turkey or chocolate.
- These New World foods arrived slowly after **1500** and took generations to become kitchen staples rather than curiosities. A Renaissance-era table could plausibly include a few of them in wealthy or coastal households while most of the countryside still cooked medieval-style.
- **Caldris map:** only use those crops if the setting has already imported them (or a local analogue). Do not invent an Earth discovery date on-page.
- Sugar prices began falling as Atlantic plantations expanded, gradually shifting it from luxury medicine toward the sweetener role it holds today. *(Keep sugar rare for common Caldris tables unless a later arc unlocks cheaper supply.)*

## Rhythm of eating

- Most households ate two main meals: **dinner** at midday (the largest meal) and a lighter **supper** in the evening. Breakfast was minimal for adults, more common for children and laborers doing early physical work.
- Late winter and early spring were the leanest stretch, the **hungry gap** before new crops came in, when households lived on whatever had been preserved (salted meat, dried peas, stored grain, hard cheese). Map to the cold / lean stretch of the **13×28** year, not an Earth month name.
- **Martinmas** *(Earth, ~November)* was traditionally when surplus livestock was slaughtered before winter feed ran out, with the meat salted or smoked to last through the cold months. **Caldris equivalent:** a late-autumn / pre-winter slaughter festival or farm custom before the lean months; do not use the Earth saint name unless a church parallel is established.

## Cooking without a pot

Earth technique for trail / camp meals (Roland forest lunches, goblin-camp prey, Ember drills). In Caldris prose: prefer leaves, clay, green sticks and ash over foil; potatoes and other New World crops only if already imported (`Renaissance shift` above).

### Fire setup

- **Cook on coals, not flames.** Burn hardwood (oak, hickory, maple, birch, fruitwood) for 30 to 45 minutes until it glows under a layer of white ash. Resinous softwoods leave soot and a bitter taste.
- **Build two zones.** Rake deep coals to one side for searing and leave a thin layer on the other for gentle cooking.
- **Hand test at 6 inches above the coals:** 2 to 3 seconds is high heat, 4 to 5 is medium and 6 to 8 is low.
- **Revive coals** by fanning with a hat or blowing through a hollow stick.

### Roast potatoes

- **Ash-baked:** Rake a pocket into the coals, bury whole potatoes and cover with coals and ash. Bake 30 to 60 minutes depending on size and turn once by digging them up. They are done when a stick slides in with no resistance. Brush off the ash and split them open. Sweet potatoes, beets, onions, garlic bulbs and squash halves work the same way.
- **Clay-wrapped:** Coat each potato in a finger-thick layer of wet clay or mud before burying. The clay bakes into a shell that steams the inside and peels away cleanly.
- **Sliced:** Thread thick slices onto a stick and roast beside the coals, or sear them on a hot flat rock with a smear of fat.
- **Finish** by splitting them and mashing fat and salt into the hot flesh.

### Greens

- **Leaf packet:** Wrap chopped greens with fat, garlic and salt in a large leaf or foil. Set it at the edge of the coals for 5 to 10 minutes so the greens steam in their own moisture.
- **Direct char:** Hold hardy greens (kale, collards, cabbage wedges, bok choy, ramps, wild onions) on a stick or grate over medium coals for 1 to 3 minutes until the edges blacken and wilt. Dress with oil, acid and salt.
- **Whole cabbage:** Bury a whole head in coals for 30 to 40 minutes and peel off the charred outer layers to reach the tender interior.
- **Hot stone:** Wilt tender greens on a heated flat rock with a little fat.

### Roasted meat

- **Spit roast:** Set two forked sticks on either side of the fire with a peeled green spit across them. Keep the meat 18 to 24 inches from the coals and rotate every few minutes. A small bird or rabbit takes 1 to 2 hours.
- **Tripod hang:** Suspend a bird or joint from a tripod on cord or wire over the coals. Twist it periodically so it turns.
- **Clay or ash crust:** Encase birds, fish or small game in clay (feathers and scales can stay on) and bury in coals for 45 to 90 minutes. Crack the shell open and the skin comes away with it.
- **Coal-buried:** Wrap meat in wet leaves or foil and bury it in the coal bed with more coals on top.
- **Doneness:** 165°F (74°C) for poultry and 160°F (71°C) for pork and boar. Fish is done at 145°F (63°C).

### Grilled meat

- **Green-stick grate:** Lay thick green sticks side by side across two logs or rocks. Green wood chars slowly and holds a full cook.
- **Flat rock griddle:** Heat a flat, dry, dense stone in the fire for 30 to 45 minutes. Use it to sear thin steaks, sliced meat, eggs and flatbread. Avoid wet river rock because trapped water can make it burst.
- **Directly on the coals:** Fan the ash off glowing hardwood coals and lay the steak straight on them for 2 to 4 minutes per side. Brush off the ash before serving. This gives the deepest crust.
- **Fat management:** Fatty cuts go at the edge of the hot zone to avoid flare-ups. Thin cuts go over the hottest coals.
- **Split-stick clamp:** Split a green stick partway and sandwich a fish or thin cut in it for easy turning.

### Skewers

- **Sticks:** Peel green hazel, willow, birch or maple to about a pencil's thickness and sharpen one end. Green wood needs no soaking. Avoid conifer and laurel.
- **Threading:** Cut meat into 1.5 to 2 cm cubes and alternate lean pieces with fat. Pack them snugly. A flat stick or two parallel sticks keeps the meat from spinning.
- **Hands-free cooking:** Push the skewer into the ground at an angle leaning over the coals, or rest it across two rocks. Turn every 30 to 45 seconds.
- **Cumin-mala method:** Carry a small tin of toasted cumin, dried chili, ground Sichuan pepper and salt. Dust twice, once when the fat starts sizzling and again just before pulling.
- **Dough twist:** Wind a strip of bread dough around a peeled stick and rotate over low coals for 8 to 12 minutes.

### BBQ and slow cooking

- **Earth oven (pit BBQ):**
  1. Dig a pit about 2 feet deep and line it with dry stones.
  2. Burn hardwood in it for 2 to 3 hours.
  3. Rake the coals level and lay wet leaves or green branches over them.
  4. Add meat wrapped in leaves or foil, then another layer of leaves.
  5. Cover with 6 or more inches of soil.
  6. Cook 4 to 8 hours and dig it up. Shoulders, ribs and whole small animals come out fall-apart tender.
- **Smoke rack:** Build a green-stick platform about 3 feet above a low coal bed. Add damp hardwood for smoke and hold 225 to 250°F (107 to 121°C) for 3 to 5 hours.
- **Jerky:** Slice meat 1/4 inch thick, salt it and hang it on the rack in thin smoke for 4 to 8 hours until it bends and cracks without snapping.
- **Mopping:** Baste with vinegar, salted water or melted fat using a bundle of rosemary or a leafy twig.

### Extras

- **Eggs:** Bury in warm ash for 10 to 15 minutes or fill a hollowed orange half and set it on coals.
- **Salt-crust fish:** Pack a whole fish in a thick coat of wet salt and coals for 15 to 20 minutes.
- **Bamboo tubes:** Fill green bamboo with rice or vegetables and water, plug the end and lean it over coals.
- **Minimal kit:** Salt, a spice tin, cooking fat, foil, a sharp knife and cord cover nearly every method above.

## Steam-era spirits and liqueurs

Caldris has magic steam trains and noble airships so copper stills and boiler craft are common. Tavern prices live in `Economy.md`. Process anchors below are Earth/steam technique, not brand names for Caldris shelves.

**Everything comes down to fermenting a sugar source and distilling it in a copper still.** Steam-era gear makes it faster and cleaner.

### Rum (for the coffee liqueur)

- Dilute molasses or fresh cane juice with water.
- Add yeast (skimmings from the sugar boiling or wild yeast) and ferment 1 to 2 weeks.
- Distill in a copper pot still and discard the first harsh runnings.
- Age in oak for smoothness or use it young.

### Whiskey (for the Irish-style cream)

- Soak barley until it sprouts (malting) and dry it in a kiln.
- Mash with hot water to convert the starch to sugar.
- Ferment with yeast for 2 to 3 days.
- Distill in copper pot stills. Irish style runs it three times for a lighter spirit.
- Age in oak barrels. Even a few months of charred oak rounds off the harshness.

### Sweet fortified wine (for the Country Cream style)

- Crush grapes and start fermentation.
- Add grape brandy partway through. The alcohol kills the yeast and leaves the remaining sugar in the wine (the port method).
- Let it rest a few weeks. Aging improves it but is optional.

### Steam-tech upgrades

- The Coffey column still (1830) turns out strong clean spirit continuously and is steam-heated. *(Earth date; use as tech level, not a Caldris patent year.)*
- Steam engines drive crushers and pumps and heat mash tuns.
- Pasteur's yeast work (1860s) explains why fermentations succeed or fail. Earlier brewers relied on saved yeast from a good batch.

Grain and cane spirits are the easiest of the three. A neutral Coffey-still spirit can also stand in for rum or whiskey in the liqueurs by adding sugar and flavor.

**All three are easy in the steam era.** Freshness is the only real obstacle and it is off the table.

### Coffee liqueur

Cold-brew strong coffee (or steep roasted grounds in rum for a few days). Mix with sugar syrup and a vanilla pod. Strain through cloth.

### Irish-style cream liqueur

Whisk fresh cream into whiskey with sugar and a little cocoa and vanilla. Add the cream slowly to the spirit while stirring. Drink the same day or next.

### Country Cream style

Use a sweet fortified wine (port or a sweet Madeira) in place of whiskey. Chill both first and pour the wine into the cream in a thin stream while whisking. Sweet high-sugar wines curdle far less than dry ones.

Everything needed was common by the 1850s: cane sugar, cream, brandy, whiskey, rum and roasted coffee. A homogenizer only matters for shelf life and a silky texture. A fresh hand-whisked batch drinks smoothly.

### Why cumin works with gamey meat

Cumin works with gamey meat because its flavor comes from the same earthy, musky, slightly bitter range as the gaminess itself. Instead of masking the meat, it lines up with those notes so they read as deliberate depth rather than an off flavor.

In lamb, mutton and goat the gaminess comes largely from branched-chain fatty acids such as 4-methyloctanoic and 4-methylnonanoic acid held in the fat. Venison and other wild game add iron-rich, blood-like myoglobin notes plus terpenes picked up from forage. Cumin's main aroma compounds (cuminaldehyde, p-cymene and related terpenes) are earthy and pungent enough to sit in that same space.

Several other factors help:

- **Fat solubility:** Cumin's aromatics dissolve into rendered fat, which is where the gamey compounds concentrate, so the spice flavors the exact part of the meat that needs it.
- **Toasting:** Heating cumin creates roasty, nutty pyrazine-type notes that pair with the browning on seared or roasted meat.
- **Classic partners:** Cumin is usually paired with acid (yogurt, lemon, vinegar) and with garlic, chili and coriander, which cut the richness and keep the gaminess balanced.
- **Culinary history:** Regions that cook a lot of lamb and goat (North Africa, the Middle East, India, Xinjiang) built spice blends around cumin for this reason. *(Earth anchor; in Caldris map to cultures that eat a lot of sheep/goat.)*

### Young boar

Young boar needs cumin less than lamb or mature game does. The strong gamey notes come from mature males, whose fat carries androstenone and skatole (boar taint), plus heavy myoglobin and forage flavors. Young boar has little of that, so the meat tastes like a lean, slightly sweet, nutty pork with a mild wild edge.

Cumin still works, but its role changes from balancing strong flavor to adding warmth and depth.

- **Use a lighter hand:** Heavy cumin overwhelms the delicate flavor. A moderate rub or a small amount in a braise is enough.
- **Add fat:** Young boar is very lean. Cumin's aromatics carry best in fat, so larding, bacon or a lard-based braise improves both moisture and spice flavor.
- **Best applications:** Slow-braised shoulder in a barbacoa or carnitas style with orange, lime, garlic, chili and oregano. Cumin-rubbed roast loin also works when it is not overcooked.
- **Better partners for the wild note:** Juniper, sage, fennel seed, rosemary, black pepper and paprika highlight what young boar actually tastes like, so they often suit it better than cumin.
- **If the fat smells off:** Cumin will not fix boar taint. Trimming the fat, brining or soaking in salted water with acid, and cooking with a sharp element like vinegar or wine do the real work.

#### Ranking for skewers (young wild boar / chuan'r style)

1. **Neck/collar (coppa):** Best choice. Heavy intramuscular marbling keeps the cubes juicy over high heat and the flavor holds up to cumin and mala without drying out.
2. **Shoulder (Boston butt):** Nearly as good and much easier to find. Marbled and forgiving, with enough connective tissue to stay tender when cut small.
3. **Belly:** The traditional partner for lean meat on chuan'r. Young boar belly is thin, so cut it into 1 cm pieces and alternate with lean cubes. It renders onto the meat and carries the cumin.
4. **Leg (ham):** Lean but flavorful. Works when cut across the grain, marinated with a little oil or egg white and cornstarch and alternated with fat.
5. **Loin and tenderloin:** Weakest option. Too lean for long grilling, so use only if threaded with backfat and pulled the moment they are cooked through.

**Fat**

Skin-on or trimmed backfat cut into small cubes and threaded every second or third piece is the Xinjiang method. It bastes the lean meat and makes the spices cling. Pick fat from young animals only, since it carries any taint. *(Earth method name; in Caldris just write the technique.)*

**Cutting and marinating**

- Cubes of 1.5 to 2 cm cook evenly and char at the edges before drying out.
- Marinate 2 to 4 hours with salt, ginger, Shaoxing wine, a little soy and a spoonful of oil. Wine and ginger also suppress any residual wild note. *(Earth ingredients; map wine/soy to local rice wine and fermented soy if needed.)*

**Seasoning**

- Toast whole cumin seed and dried chili separately and grind coarse. Add ground Sichuan peppercorn (the mala numbness) and salt.
- Dust the skewers in two rounds, once halfway through grilling and once at the end, so the spices bloom on the hot fat without burning. Fresh-ground Sichuan pepper added last keeps its numbing aroma.

**Cooking**

- Very hot charcoal with turning every 30 to 45 seconds gives the best crust.
- Wild boar should reach 160°F (71°C) internally. Small cubes reach this in a few minutes and the fat keeps them moist past that point.

**Dungeon Spiked Boar** meat is usually skipped (dense / bad; Ch 11). Ch 12 cooks neck/collar and thin belly strips anyway, brushed with Needle Worm blood as cumin seasoning (paralytic spice; party still eats and likes it).

### Cumin skewers (chuan'r, Xinjiang style) — Earth technique

*(Earth anchor. In Caldris write the method without the place name unless a local culture match is established.)*

1. **Prep:** Cut meat into 1.5 to 2 cm cubes and marinate 2 to 4 hours with salt, ginger, wine and a little oil. Thread tightly, alternating lean cubes with small cubes of fat.
2. **Fire:** Burn charcoal down to glowing red coals with a thin ash layer. Set the skewers close to the heat, often over a long narrow trough grill, with a fan or blower available to boost the fire.
3. **Sear:** Lay the skewers across the grill and turn every 30 to 45 seconds. The fat renders and drips onto the coals, causing flare-ups that add smoke and char.
4. **First seasoning:** Once the surface is set and the fat is sizzling, dust with salt, coarse-ground toasted cumin, chili and ground Sichuan pepper. Turn so the spices toast against the hot meat for 20 to 30 seconds per side.
5. **Second seasoning:** Repeat the dusting just before pulling the skewers so the cumin stays fragrant and the mala numbness stays sharp.
6. **Finish:** Total grill time runs about 6 to 10 minutes. Serve immediately, often with scallion or sesame, since the crust softens as it sits.

The goal is an aggressive, smoky, charred exterior with loud spice flavor carried by rendered fat.

### Yakitori — Earth technique

*(Earth anchor. In Caldris write the method without the place name unless a local culture match is established.)*

1. **Prep:** Cut chicken (thigh, skin, liver, hearts, tsukune) into uniform bite-size pieces and thread them onto thin bamboo skewers so they lie flat. Pieces are usually unmarinated.
2. **Fire:** Use binchotan white charcoal, which burns hot with little smoke or flame. The grill is narrow so the meat sits over the coals while the skewer handles hang clear. Skewers are moved between hotter and cooler zones to control cooking.
3. **Shio (salt) method:** Salt the skewers right before grilling. Turn frequently and cook until the skin is browned and the meat is just cooked through, about 5 to 8 minutes total.
4. **Tare method:** Grill until roughly 70 to 80 percent cooked. Dip in tare (a reduction of soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar) and return to the fire so the glaze caramelizes. Repeat two or three times to build a lacquered coating without burning.
5. **Finish:** Chicken should reach 165°F (74°C) internally. Add sansho or shichimi at the end if wanted and serve immediately.

The goal is clean, precise cooking that highlights the chicken itself with gentle char and a light seasoning layer.
