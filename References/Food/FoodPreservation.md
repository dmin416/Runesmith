# 1. How Food Spoils and How Preservation Works

## 1.1 Causes of Spoilage

| Cause | What Happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | Multiply and produce acids, gases, slime, odors and toxins | Sour milk, slimy meat, botulism, salmonella |
| **Yeasts** | Ferment sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide | Fizzy jam, fermented juice |
| **Molds** | Grow fuzzy colonies; some produce mycotoxins | Moldy bread, cheese, fruit |
| **Enzymes** | The food's own enzymes keep breaking it down after harvest | Browning apples, mushy frozen vegetables, overripe fruit |
| **Oxidation** | Oxygen reacts with fats, pigments and vitamins | Rancid nuts, faded color, stale flavors |
| **Moisture changes** | Gaining or losing water | Soggy crackers, freezer burn, wilted greens |
| **Pests** | Insects and rodents | Weevils in flour, pantry moths |

## 1.2 What Microbes Need

Every preservation method works by removing one or more of these:

| Requirement | How Preservation Removes It |
|---|---|
| **Warmth** (most grow fastest at 21 to 49°C / 70 to 120°F) | Refrigeration, freezing, cold storage |
| **Water** | Drying, salting, sugaring, freezing (ties water up as ice) |
| **Neutral pH** (most pathogens need pH above 4.6) | Pickling, fermentation, acidification |
| **Oxygen** (for molds and many bacteria) | Canning, vacuum sealing, fat sealing, oxygen absorbers |
| **Nutrients and time** | Fast processing |
| **Absence of inhibitors** | Salt, sugar, smoke, alcohol, spices, nitrites, chemical preservatives |

Heat (cooking, pasteurizing, canning) kills microbes and destroys enzymes directly. Combining several factors (called **hurdle technology**) lets each one be milder; for example, salami relies on salt, nitrite, acid from fermentation and drying all at once.

## 1.3 The Temperature Danger Zone

- **4 to 60°C (40 to 140°F):** Bacteria multiply rapidly; some double every 20 minutes.
- Perishable food should spend no more than **2 hours** total in this zone (1 hour above 32°C / 90°F).
- **Below 4°C (40°F):** Growth slows greatly; some bacteria (listeria) still grow slowly.
- **At -18°C (0°F) and below:** Growth stops; microbes survive and resume after thawing.
- **Above 74°C (165°F):** Most vegetative bacteria die quickly.
- **121°C (250°F):** Required to destroy botulism spores in reasonable time; achievable only under pressure.

## 1.4 Water Activity

**Water activity (aw)** measures water available to microbes, from 0 (bone dry) to 1.0 (pure water). It is different from moisture content; salt and sugar bind water and lower aw.

| aw | What Can Grow | Typical Foods |
|---|---|---|
| 0.95 to 1.0 | Nearly all bacteria, yeasts, molds | Fresh meat, fish, milk, fruit, vegetables |
| 0.91 to 0.95 | Most bacteria including botulism (lower limit about 0.94) | Bread, cured ham, some cheeses |
| 0.87 to 0.91 | Most yeasts; staphylococcus | Dry salami, aged cheese |
| 0.80 to 0.87 | Most molds | Jams, syrups, fruitcake |
| 0.75 to 0.80 | Salt-tolerant bacteria | Heavily salted fish, marzipan |
| 0.65 to 0.75 | Dry-tolerant molds | Dried fruit, rolled oats |
| 0.60 to 0.65 | Sugar-tolerant yeasts | Honey, dried fruit |
| Below 0.60 | No microbial growth | Crackers, pasta, powdered milk, spices, dry grains |

Shelf-stable jerky must be at or below **0.85**.

## 1.5 Acidity (pH)

| Category | pH | Examples | Home Canning Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High acid** | 4.6 and below | Most fruits, pickles, sauerkraut, jams, acidified tomatoes | Boiling water bath or steam canner |
| **Low acid** | Above 4.6 | All meats, fish, poultry, milk, most vegetables, beans, soups | Pressure canner only |

Tomatoes (pH 4.2 to 4.9), figs and some Asian pears sit near the boundary and must be acidified before water bath canning.

## 1.6 Botulism

*Clostridium botulinum* is the central concern in home preservation.

- Lives as spores in soil and on all produce; spores survive boiling.
- Grows and produces toxin in conditions that are: **low oxygen, low acid (above pH 4.6), moist (aw above 0.94) and between about 3 and 50°C**.
- Sealed jars, oil-covered foods, vacuum packs, foil-wrapped baked potatoes, garlic in oil and improperly canned vegetables and meats match those conditions.
- The toxin cannot be seen, smelled or tasted reliably.
- Prevention: pressure canning low-acid foods at 116 to 121°C, acidifying to pH 4.6 or below, salting or curing properly, keeping water activity low, refrigerating or freezing.
- The toxin itself is destroyed by boiling for 10 minutes, which is why some guidance recommends boiling home-canned low-acid foods before eating.

# 2. Refrigeration

## 2.1 Settings

- Refrigerator: **1 to 4°C (34 to 40°F)**; 3°C (37°F) is a good target.
- Freezer: **-18°C (0°F)** or colder.
- Use an appliance thermometer; built-in dials are often inaccurate.

## 2.2 Refrigerator Zones

| Zone | Temperature | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom shelf (back) | Coldest | Raw meat, poultry, fish (also prevents drips onto other foods) |
| Middle shelves | Steady | Dairy, eggs, leftovers |
| Top shelf | Slightly warmer | Ready-to-eat foods, drinks, herbs |
| Crisper drawers (high humidity) | Moist | Leafy greens, herbs, carrots, broccoli, cucumbers |
| Crisper drawers (low humidity) | Drier, vented | Apples, pears, stone fruit, avocados (ethylene producers) |
| Door | Warmest, fluctuating | Condiments, juices; not milk or eggs |

## 2.3 Refrigerator Storage Times

| Food | Time |
|---|---|
| Raw ground meat, ground poultry, sausage | 1 to 2 days |
| Raw poultry pieces or whole | 1 to 2 days |
| Raw steaks, chops, roasts | 3 to 5 days |
| Raw fish and shellfish | 1 to 2 days |
| Bacon (opened) | 7 days |
| Hot dogs, lunch meat (opened) | 3 to 5 days |
| Cooked leftovers, soups, stews | 3 to 4 days |
| Cooked poultry and meat | 3 to 4 days |
| Hard-cooked eggs | 7 days |
| Raw eggs in shell | 3 to 5 weeks |
| Milk (opened) | About 7 days |
| Hard cheese (opened) | 3 to 4 weeks |
| Soft cheese | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Butter | 1 to 3 months |
| Refrigerator pickles | 1 to 3 months |
| Opened jams | 6 to 12 months |

## 2.4 Keeping Produce Fresh Longer

- **Ethylene producers** (apples, pears, bananas, avocados, tomatoes, stone fruit, melons) ripen nearby produce faster. Store them apart from ethylene-sensitive items (leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, cucumbers, potatoes).
- **Herbs:** Stand soft herbs (parsley, cilantro, mint) in a glass of water like flowers, loosely bagged, in the fridge. Basil keeps better at room temperature in water.
- **Greens:** Wash, spin dry, wrap in a dry towel and bag.
- **Carrots, celery, radishes:** Store submerged in water in a container, changing water every few days; they stay crisp for weeks.
- **Mushrooms:** Paper bag, not plastic.
- **Asparagus:** Stand in an inch of water.
- **Do not refrigerate:** Potatoes (starch turns to sugar), onions, garlic, winter squash, sweet potatoes, whole tomatoes for best flavor, bananas, bread (stales faster), honey.

## 2.5 Cooling Hot Food Safely

- Cool from 60 to 21°C (140 to 70°F) within 2 hours, then to 4°C (40°F) within 4 more hours.
- Divide large batches into shallow containers no more than 5 cm (2 inches) deep.
- Use an ice bath or ice wand for stocks and soups.

# 3. Freezing

## 3.1 How Freezing Works

Freezing stops microbial growth and slows enzymes but does not kill most microbes or stop enzymes completely. Quality slowly declines from enzyme activity, oxidation and ice crystal growth.

**Fast freezing** makes small ice crystals that damage cells less. Slow freezing forms large crystals that puncture cell walls, causing mushy texture and drip loss on thawing.

## 3.2 General Rules

- Freezer at **-18°C (0°F)** or below.
- Freeze food at peak freshness; freezing does not improve quality.
- Do not overload; add no more than about 1 kg of unfrozen food per 28 liters (2 to 3 lb per cubic foot) of freezer space in 24 hours.
- Spread items in a single layer near the walls or on a tray for fastest freezing.
- **Remove air** to prevent freezer burn (dry, discolored patches caused by sublimation and oxidation).
- Leave headspace in rigid containers for liquids: about 1 to 2.5 cm (0.5 to 1 inch) since liquids expand about 9%.
- Label with contents and date.

## 3.3 Packaging

| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| Vacuum sealing | Best protection; extends quality life 2 to 5 times |
| Freezer bags | Press or suck air out; water displacement method (submerge open bag to push air out) |
| Rigid containers | Good for liquids, soups, berries |
| Freezer paper | Plastic-coated paper wrapped tightly around meat, then taped; traditional for butcher cuts |
| Plastic wrap plus foil | Double wrap for meats |
| Glass jars | Use straight-sided freezer-safe jars with headspace; tapered shoulders can crack |
| Ice glazing | Dip frozen fish in ice water repeatedly to build a protective ice coat |

## 3.4 Tray Freezing (IQF)

For berries, sliced fruit, vegetables, meatballs, dumplings and cookie dough:

1. Spread pieces in a single layer on a parchment-lined tray.
2. Freeze until solid (1 to 4 hours).
3. Transfer to bags. Pieces stay separate and pour freely.

## 3.5 Blanching Vegetables

Blanching (brief boiling or steaming, then rapid cooling) stops enzymes that cause off-flavors, color loss and toughness during freezing.

**Method:**

1. Bring at least 4 liters (1 gallon) of water per 500 g (1 lb) of vegetables to a rolling boil.
2. Add vegetables; start timing when water returns to a boil.
3. Transfer immediately to ice water for the same amount of time.
4. Drain thoroughly and pat dry.
5. Package and freeze.

Steam blanching takes about 1.5 times as long as boiling.

| Vegetable | Boiling Blanch Time |
|---|---|
| Asparagus | 2 to 4 minutes (by thickness) |
| Green and wax beans | 3 minutes |
| Lima beans | 2 to 4 minutes |
| Broccoli florets | 3 minutes |
| Brussels sprouts | 3 to 5 minutes |
| Cabbage (shredded) | 1.5 minutes |
| Carrots (sliced or diced) | 2 minutes |
| Carrots (small whole) | 5 minutes |
| Cauliflower florets | 3 minutes |
| Corn on the cob | 7 to 11 minutes |
| Corn kernels | Blanch cobs 4 minutes, then cut |
| Collards | 3 minutes |
| Kale, chard, spinach, other greens | 2 minutes |
| Kohlrabi (cubed) | 1 minute |
| Okra | 3 to 4 minutes |
| Parsnips (cubed) | 2 minutes |
| Peas (shelled) | 1.5 minutes |
| Snow and snap peas | 1.5 to 3 minutes |
| Summer squash, zucchini (slices) | 3 minutes |
| Turnips (cubed) | 2 minutes |

**No blanching needed:** Bell and hot peppers, onions, herbs, tomatoes (freeze whole; skins slip off when thawed), shredded zucchini for baking.

**Cook fully before freezing:** Beets, winter squash, pumpkin, sweet potatoes, potatoes (raw potatoes turn grey and grainy).

## 3.6 Freezing Fruit

| Method | How | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Dry pack | Freeze plain, tray-frozen | Berries, cherries, rhubarb, cranberries |
| Sugar pack | Toss with sugar (about 1 part sugar to 4 to 5 parts fruit) until juice forms | Sliced peaches, strawberries |
| Syrup pack | Cover with cold sugar syrup (30 to 40%) | Peaches, apricots, pears, plums |
| Purée | Purée with or without sugar | Very ripe fruit, baby food |
| Juice pack | Cover with fruit juice | Low-sugar option |

**Prevent browning** (apples, peaches, pears, apricots, bananas): Ascorbic acid (about 1/2 teaspoon pure ascorbic acid powder per cup of syrup or per 4 liters of water for a dip), commercial fruit fresh mixes or lemon juice.

## 3.7 Freezing Meat, Poultry and Fish

- Freeze in meal-size portions.
- Remove from store trays; rewrap tightly or vacuum seal.
- Separate burgers, chops and steaks with parchment.
- Fatty fish and fatty meats (pork, sausage, bacon) keep the shortest time because fat oxidizes.
- **Fish for raw eating (sushi, ceviche, cold-smoked):** Freezing kills parasites at -20°C (-4°F) for 7 days or -35°C (-31°F) for 15 hours (commercial blast freezing). Home freezers often do not reach -20°C reliably.

| Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel) | 2 to 3 months |
| Shrimp, scallops | 3 to 6 months |
| Cooked meat, casseroles, soups | 2 to 6 months |
| Stock and broth | 4 to 6 months |
| Blanched vegetables | 8 to 12 months |
| Fruit | 8 to 12 months |
| Bread | 3 months |

## 3.9 Freezing Other Foods

| Food | Method |
|---|---|
| **Eggs** | Crack and beat; freeze in portions. Yolks alone gel when frozen unless mixed with 1/8 teaspoon salt or 1.5 teaspoons sugar per 1/4 cup yolks. Whites freeze plain perfectly |
| **Milk** | Freezes well for cooking; separates slightly. Leave headspace |
| **Butter** | Freezes very well |
| **Cheese** | Hard and semi-hard cheeses freeze best shredded; texture turns crumbly |
| **Cream** | Heavy cream freezes for cooking; whips poorly. Freeze whipped cream in dollops |
| **Bread** | Freeze sliced; toast from frozen |
| **Cooked rice and grains** | Freeze flat in bags |
| **Herbs** | Chop and freeze in ice cube trays with water, oil or melted butter; or freeze whole leaves of hardy herbs |
| **Citrus** | Zest and juice separately; freeze juice in cubes |
| **Tomato paste** | Freeze tablespoon dollops |
| **Ginger** | Freeze whole; grate from frozen |
| **Bananas** | Peel, slice and freeze for baking and smoothies |
| **Avocado** | Purée with lime juice |
| **Stock** | Reduce to concentrate, freeze in cubes |
| **Do not freeze well** | Lettuce, cucumbers, radishes, raw potatoes, mayonnaise, custards, cream sauces, fried foods (soggy), hard-cooked egg whites (rubbery), sour cream and yogurt (texture changes) |

## 3.10 Thawing

- **Refrigerator:** Safest; allow about 24 hours per 2 to 2.5 kg (4 to 5 lb). Food can be refrozen.
- **Cold water:** Sealed package submerged in cold water, changed every 30 minutes; about 30 minutes per 500 g. Cook immediately.
- **Microwave:** Cook immediately afterward.
- **Cooking from frozen:** Takes about 50% longer.
- **Never** thaw on the counter.

## 3.11 Power Outages

- A full freezer keeps food frozen about 48 hours closed (24 hours if half full).
- A refrigerator keeps food cold about 4 hours unopened.
- Food that still has ice crystals or reads 4°C (40°F) or below can be refrozen.
- Dry ice: about 11 kg (25 lb) keeps a full 0.5 cubic meter (18 cubic foot) freezer frozen for 2 days. Handle with gloves; ventilate.

# 4. Root Cellaring and Cold Storage Without Power

## 4.1 Root Cellar Basics

A root cellar uses the earth's stable temperature (about 10 to 13°C / 50 to 55°F at depth in temperate climates, colder in winter near the surface) plus humidity and ventilation to keep produce alive and dormant for months.

**Key factors:**

- **Temperature:** Ideal is 0 to 4°C (32 to 40°F) for most crops.
- **Humidity:** 85 to 95% for roots; dry air for onions and squash.
- **Ventilation:** Low intake vent and high exhaust vent create airflow that removes ethylene, heat and excess moisture. Opening vents on cold nights cools the space.
- **Darkness:** Prevents sprouting and greening of potatoes.
- **Floor:** Earth or gravel floors help humidity; dampen with water if too dry.

**Types:** Dug-out hillside cellars, basement corners (insulated from the furnace, with an outside vent), buried barrels or trash cans, buried chest freezers (unplugged), garages, unheated porches, under-stair spaces, crawl spaces.

## 4.2 Storage Conditions by Crop

| Condition | Temp | Humidity | Crops | Storage Life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold and very moist** | 0 to 4°C (32 to 40°F) | 90 to 95% | Carrots, beets, parsnips, turnips, rutabagas, celeriac, kohlrabi, winter radishes, horseradish, salsify, Jerusalem artichokes | 2 to 6 months |
| | | | Cabbage, Chinese cabbage, Brussels sprouts (on stalk), leeks, collards | 1 to 4 months |
| | | | Apples, pears | 2 to 7 months (variety dependent) |
| **Cool and moist** | 4 to 10°C (40 to 50°F) | 85 to 95% | Potatoes | 4 to 9 months |
| **Cool and dry** | 0 to 10°C (32 to 50°F) | 60 to 70% | Onions, garlic, shallots | 4 to 8 months (garlic up to 9) |
| | | | Dry beans, dried peppers, grains | 1 year+ |
| **Warm and dry** | 10 to 15°C (50 to 60°F) | 60 to 75% | Winter squash, pumpkins | 2 to 6 months |
| | 13 to 16°C (55 to 60°F) | 75 to 85% | Sweet potatoes | 4 to 7 months |
| **Cool, moderate** | 10 to 15°C (50 to 60°F) | 80 to 90% | Green tomatoes for ripening | 1 to 2 months |

## 4.3 Curing Before Storage

Curing heals skins and toughens them for long storage.

| Crop | Curing Conditions |
|---|---|
| Potatoes | 10 to 14 days at 10 to 15°C (50 to 60°F), high humidity, dark |
| Onions | 2 to 4 weeks warm, dry and airy until necks are dry and skins papery |
| Garlic | 2 to 4 weeks hung or spread in shade with airflow |
| Winter squash, pumpkins | 10 to 14 days at 27 to 29°C (80 to 85°F); acorn squash is not cured |
| Sweet potatoes | 7 to 10 days at 27 to 32°C (80 to 90°F), 85 to 90% humidity; flavor sweetens over the following weeks |

## 4.4 Preparing Produce

- Harvest in dry weather; handle gently. Bruised or damaged items go to the kitchen first.
- **Roots:** Do not wash; brush off excess soil. Cut tops to about 2.5 cm (1 inch). Leave taproots intact.
- **Potatoes:** Do not wash; store in breathable bins or burlap in the dark.
- **Cabbage:** Pull with roots and hang upside down, or trim and wrap in newspaper. Strong odors spread; store away from fruit or in a separate space.
- **Apples:** Wrap individually in newspaper; check monthly. "One bad apple" rots neighbors.
- **Squash:** Leave 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 inches) of stem; wipe skins with diluted vinegar or bleach solution to reduce rot.

## 4.5 Packing Methods

- **Damp sand, sawdust, peat or wood shavings:** Layer carrots, beets, parsnips and other roots in boxes or buckets so they do not touch. Keeps humidity high.
- **Bins and crates:** Slatted crates allow airflow for potatoes and apples.
- **Hanging:** Mesh bags or braids for onions and garlic; whole plants of cabbage.
- **Shelves:** Single layers for squash with space between.

## 4.6 Ethylene and Odor Separation

- Apples and pears release ethylene, which makes potatoes sprout and carrots bitter.
- Potatoes take on off-flavors from apples; apples take on flavors from potatoes, cabbage and turnips.
- Store fruit in a separate room or high and near the exhaust vent.

## 4.7 Other Unpowered Cold Storage

| Method | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **In-ground storage** | Leave carrots, parsnips, leeks, kale, Jerusalem artichokes and salsify in the garden under 30 to 45 cm (12 to 18 inches) of straw or leaf mulch; harvest through winter. Parsnips and carrots sweeten after frost |
| **Clamp (pit)** | Pile roots on a bed of straw, cover with thick straw then 15 to 30 cm of soil, leaving a straw chimney for ventilation; dig a drainage trench around it |
| **Buried container** | A barrel, trash can or old refrigerator buried on its side or upright, filled with produce in layers of straw, covered with soil and straw |
| **Cold frame storage** | Insulated cold frame filled with roots in straw |
| **Spring house** | A small building over a spring or stream; cold running water keeps crocks of milk and butter cool |
| **Ice house** | Lake ice cut in winter, packed in sawdust in an insulated building, lasts into late summer |
| **Evaporative cooler (zeer pot)** | A clay pot inside a larger clay pot with wet sand between them; evaporation cools the inner pot 10 to 15°C below air temperature in dry climates |
| **Window box / porch** | Cold-weather outdoor storage in an insulated cooler |
| **Snow and ice** | Food buried in snow or in a cooler packed with ice |
| **Well or stream** | Sealed containers lowered into cold water |

# 5. Drying and Dehydrating

## 5.1 Principles

Drying removes enough water that microbes and enzymes cannot work. Most dried fruits end at 15 to 20% moisture; vegetables at 5 to 10%; jerky at aw 0.85 or below.

Good drying needs **low humidity, steady warm temperature and moving air**. Too much heat too fast hardens the surface (**case hardening**), trapping moisture inside.

## 5.2 Drying Methods

| Method | Temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Electric dehydrator** | 35 to 74°C (95 to 165°F) adjustable | Most reliable; fan and thermostat |
| **Oven** | Lowest setting, door propped 5 to 10 cm open | Uses more energy; many ovens run too hot |
| **Sun drying** | Needs 30°C (86°F)+ and humidity below 60% | Fruits mainly; cover with screens; bring in at night |
| **Solar dehydrator** | 40 to 65°C (100 to 150°F) | Box with a glazed collector and screened trays; faster and cleaner than open sun |
| **Air drying** | Room temperature | Herbs, chilies (ristras), beans, mushrooms (strung), corn |
| **Microwave** | Short bursts | Herbs only |
| **Smokehouse / over fire** | Low heat with smoke | Traditional for meat and fish |
| **Wind and cold drying** | Freezing wind | Stockfish, Inuit dried fish and meat |

## 5.3 Pretreatments for Fruit

| Pretreatment | Method | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Ascorbic acid dip | 1 teaspoon pure ascorbic acid per cup of water, 3 to 5 minutes | Prevents browning |
| Citric acid / lemon juice dip | 1 teaspoon citric acid per liter of water, or equal parts lemon juice and water | Prevents browning (less effective) |
| Honey dip | 1 part honey, 1 part sugar, 3 parts hot water | Sweet, glossy finish |
| Syrup blanching | Simmer in light syrup 10 minutes | Softer, sweeter, brighter |
| Steam blanching | 3 to 5 minutes | Preserves color; speeds drying |
| Checking (cracking skins) | Dip in boiling water 30 to 60 seconds, then cold water | Lets moisture escape from waxy skins (grapes, plums, blueberries, cherries, cranberries) |
| Sulfuring | Exposing fruit to burning sulfur fumes outdoors, or soaking in sodium metabisulfite solution | Commercial golden apricots; best color retention; some people are sulfite-sensitive |

## 5.4 Fruit Drying Guide

| Fruit | Preparation | Dryness Test |
|---|---|---|
| Apples | Peel optional; core; slice 6 mm (1/4 inch); pretreat | Leathery to crisp |
| Apricots | Halve, pit, pretreat; turn inside out (pop backs) | Pliable, no moisture when squeezed |
| Bananas | Slice 6 mm; lemon dip | Leathery or crisp |
| Berries | Check skins; whole or halved | Hard, rattle |
| Cherries | Pit, halve | Leathery, slightly sticky |
| Citrus peel | Thin strips | Crisp |
| Coconut | Grate or shred | Crisp |
| Cranberries | Check skins; sweeten with syrup first | Leathery |
| Figs | Halve or whole if small | Leathery, pliable |
| Grapes (raisins) | Check skins | Pliable, wrinkled |
| Mangoes | Slice 6 to 10 mm | Pliable |
| Peaches, nectarines | Peel, halve or slice, pretreat | Leathery |
| Pears | Peel, slice, pretreat | Leathery, springy |
| Persimmons | Slice firm Fuyu; or hang whole Hachiya (hoshigaki) | Leathery |
| Pineapple | Slice 1 cm | Leathery, not sticky |
| Plums (prunes) | Halve and pit, or check skins whole | Pliable, leathery |
| Strawberries | Slice 6 mm | Crisp or leathery |
| Tomatoes | Halve or slice; salt lightly | Leathery to crisp |

**Typical fruit drying temperature:** 57 to 63°C (135 to 145°F), 6 to 36 hours depending on fruit and thickness.

**Conditioning:** Dried fruit pieces vary in moisture. Pack loosely in a sealed jar two-thirds full for 7 to 10 days, shaking daily. Condensation in the jar means it needs more drying.

**Hoshigaki (Japanese hand-massaged persimmons):** Peel firm Hachiya persimmons, hang by the stem in airy shade, gently massage every few days for 4 to 6 weeks until a white sugar bloom forms.

## 5.5 Vegetable Drying Guide

Most vegetables are blanched before drying (same times as freezing in Section 3.5) to stop enzymes and preserve color.

| Vegetable | Preparation | Finished Texture |
|---|---|---|
| Beans (green) | Blanch, cut | Brittle |
| Beets | Cook, peel, slice | Leathery, dark |
| Broccoli | Blanch, small florets | Brittle |
| Cabbage | Shred, blanch | Crisp |
| Carrots | Blanch, slice or shred | Leathery to brittle |
| Celery | Slice, blanch optional | Brittle |
| Corn | Blanch on cob, cut kernels | Brittle |
| Garlic | Peel, slice | Crisp; grind for powder |
| Greens (kale, spinach) | Remove stems; no blanch needed for kale chips | Crisp, crumbly |
| Mushrooms | Slice, no blanch | Leathery to brittle |
| Okra | Slice | Brittle |
| Onions | Slice or dice, no blanch | Brittle |
| Peas | Blanch | Shriveled, hard |
| Peppers (bell, hot) | Slice or dice, no blanch | Leathery to brittle |
| Potatoes | Slice or shred, cook until just tender | Brittle |
| Pumpkin, winter squash | Peel, slice, blanch or cook | Brittle |
| Tomatoes | Slice or halve | Leathery to crisp |
| Zucchini | Slice 6 mm | Brittle (chips) |

**Typical vegetable drying temperature:** 52 to 57°C (125 to 135°F), 4 to 14 hours.

**Vegetable powders:** Grind fully dried tomatoes, onions, garlic, mushrooms, peppers, greens or mixed vegetables in a blender or spice grinder. Store airtight with a desiccant packet; powders absorb moisture and clump.

## 5.6 Fruit and Vegetable Leathers

1. Purée fruit (cooked or raw), adding sweetener and lemon juice to taste.
2. Spread 3 to 6 mm (1/8 to 1/4 inch) thick on lined trays, thinner in the center.
3. Dry at 57 to 63°C (135 to 145°F) for 6 to 10 hours until no longer sticky.
4. Roll in parchment and store airtight.

Applesauce added to tart or watery purées improves texture. Tomato leather becomes instant sauce or paste.

## 5.7 Herbs

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| Hang drying | Tie small bundles, hang upside down in warm, dark, airy space, 1 to 3 weeks; paper bag over bundles catches falling leaves |
| Screen drying | Spread leaves on screens |
| Dehydrator | 35 to 46°C (95 to 115°F) to keep aromatic oils |
| Oven | Lowest setting with door open, watched closely |
| Microwave | Between paper towels in 20 to 30 second bursts |

Herbs are dry when leaves crumble and stems snap. Store whole leaves and crush when using. Dried herbs keep best flavor 6 to 12 months.

## 5.8 Jerky

**Meat choice:** Lean cuts (beef eye of round, top round, flank; venison; lean pork; turkey breast). Fat turns rancid.

**Slicing:** Partially freeze for easier slicing. Cut 3 to 6 mm (1/8 to 1/4 inch) thick. With the grain gives chewy jerky; across the grain gives tender, brittle jerky.

**Marinade:** Salt (soy sauce), sugar, spices, acid (vinegar, citrus). 12 to 24 hours in the refrigerator.

**Safety step (USDA):** Heat meat to **71°C (160°F)** internal (poultry 74°C / 165°F) before or after drying because dehydrators alone may not reach temperatures that kill pathogens before the meat dries out.

- **Before drying:** Simmer strips in marinade until they reach temperature, or steam/roast them.
- **After drying:** Heat dried strips in a 135°C (275°F) oven for 10 minutes.

**Drying:** 63 to 68°C (145 to 155°F) for 4 to 12 hours.

**Doneness:** Bends and cracks but does not snap; no moist spots inside.

**Curing salt option:** Adding Cure #1 (1 level teaspoon per 2.3 kg / 5 lb of meat) adds color, flavor and protection.

**Ground meat jerky:** Use a jerky gun; requires the same heat step.

**Storage:** 1 to 2 months at room temperature in airtight containers; 6 months refrigerated; 1 year frozen. Oxygen absorbers extend shelf life.

## 5.9 Biltong, Charqui and Other Dried Meats

| Product | Origin | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Biltong** | South Africa | Thick strips (2 to 3 cm) of beef or game cut with the grain; dipped in vinegar; coated in coarse salt, toasted coriander, black pepper and sometimes sugar; marinated a few hours; hung in a dry, airy space at room temperature (often with a fan and a low-watt bulb) for 4 to 10 days until 50 to 60% weight is lost |
| **Droëwors** | South Africa | Thin dried sausage, spiced like biltong |
| **Charqui** | Andes | Thin meat strips (originally llama and alpaca) salted and dried in sun and cold mountain air; origin of the word "jerky" |
| **Carne seca / machaca** | Mexico | Salted, dried beef, shredded and rehydrated with eggs or in stews |
| **Pemmican** | North America | Dried meat pounded to powder mixed with rendered fat (Section 13.4) |
| **Bresaola** | Italy | Salted, air-dried beef eye of round (Section 7) |
| **Bakkwa** | China / Southeast Asia | Sweet, thin sheets of minced or sliced pork, marinated, dried and grilled |
| **Kilishi** | West Africa | Thin meat sheets dried, coated in peanut and spice paste, dried again and roasted |
| **Pastırma** | Turkey, Armenia | Salted, pressed, air-dried beef coated in fenugreek-garlic-paprika paste (çemen) |
| **Stockfish** | Norway | Unsalted cod air-dried on wooden racks in cold wind for months (Section 19) |

## 5.10 Drying Fish

1. Clean and split fish (butterfly) or fillet thin.
2. Salt lightly (brine at 10 to 15% for 30 minutes to a few hours, or dry salt).
3. Dry in sun and wind on racks, in a smokehouse or in a dehydrator.
4. Fully dried fish is brittle; partially dried fish needs refrigeration.

## 5.11 Drying Beans, Grains, Seeds and Corn

- **Dry beans:** Leave pods on the plant until rattling dry; pull plants and hang to finish; thresh by stomping in a bag or beating on a tarp; winnow chaff in wind or fan.
- **Corn:** Leave on stalk until hard, or hang ears with husks pulled back.
- **Grains:** Cut when kernels are hard; dry in shocks or stooks; thresh and winnow.
- **Seeds and nuts:** Dry to below 10% moisture before storage.
- **Pest treatment:** Freeze dried beans and grains at -18°C (0°F) for 3 to 7 days to kill insect eggs.

## 5.12 Storing Dried Foods

- Airtight glass jars, vacuum bags or mylar with oxygen absorbers.
- Cool, dark, dry place.
- Check weekly for the first month for moisture or mold.
- **Typical shelf life:** Dried fruits 6 to 12 months; dried vegetables 6 to 12 months (up to several years in oxygen-free packaging); herbs 6 to 12 months for best flavor.

## 5.13 Rehydrating

- Soak in cold water (fruits 1 to 8 hours; vegetables 20 minutes to 2 hours) or simmer.
- Use the soaking liquid in soups and sauces.
- About 1 cup of dried vegetables rehydrates to 1.5 to 2 cups.

# 6. Freeze-Drying

## 6.1 How It Works

1. Food is frozen solid (to about -30 to -40°C).
2. A vacuum pump lowers pressure in the chamber.
3. Gentle heat makes ice **sublimate** (turn directly from solid to vapor) without melting.
4. A cold condenser collects the vapor as ice.
5. Final secondary drying removes bound water to about 1 to 4% moisture.

Food keeps its shape, color, flavor and most nutrients and rehydrates quickly.

## 6.2 Home Freeze Dryers

- Cycles take about 20 to 40 hours (longer for dense or high-sugar foods).
- Pieces should be no thicker than about 1.5 to 2 cm (0.5 to 0.75 inch).
- Foods with very high fat (butter, pure oils, peanut butter) or very high sugar (honey, syrup) do not freeze-dry well.
- Works for raw or cooked meats, whole meals, dairy (milk, yogurt, cheese, ice cream), eggs (raw beaten eggs dried to powder), fruits, vegetables, herbs, candy.

## 6.3 Packaging

- Mylar bags (7 mil or thicker) with oxygen absorbers, heat-sealed.
- Glass jars with oxygen absorbers or vacuum sealing.
- Freeze-dried food reabsorbs moisture from air within hours; package immediately.

## 6.4 Shelf Life

- 15 to 25 years for many foods in oxygen-free, moisture-proof packaging at moderate temperatures.
- Raw meats freeze-dried at home keep their raw status and must be cooked after rehydration.

## 6.5 Natural Freeze-Drying

- **Chuño (Andes):** Potatoes left outdoors to freeze on cold nights at high altitude and thaw in the day sun, then trampled underfoot to squeeze out water; repeated for several days. Keeps for years.
- **Stockfish and Inuit dried fish** partly freeze-dry in cold, dry winter wind.

# 7. Salting and Curing

## 7.1 How Salt Preserves

- Draws water out of food and microbes by osmosis.
- Lowers water activity.
- Inhibits enzymes and bacteria.
- Favors harmless salt-tolerant and lactic acid bacteria.

## 7.2 Salt Types

| Salt | Use |
|---|---|
| **Pickling / canning salt** | Pure NaCl without iodine or anti-caking agents; dissolves clearly |
| **Kosher salt** | Coarse; brands differ greatly in density (weigh it) |
| **Sea salt (non-iodized)** | Fine for curing; minerals may darken some foods |
| **Iodized table salt** | Iodine can darken pickles; anti-caking agents cloud brine |
| **Cure #1 (Prague Powder #1, pink curing salt)** | 6.25% sodium nitrite, 93.75% salt, dyed pink so it is not mistaken for table salt; for cooked, smoked or short-cure products (bacon, ham, sausages, jerky, corned beef) |
| **Cure #2 (Prague Powder #2)** | 6.25% sodium nitrite plus 4% sodium nitrate; for long dry cures (salami, prosciutto, coppa) where nitrate slowly converts to nitrite over weeks |
| **Saltpeter (potassium nitrate)** | Historic curing agent; less predictable; largely replaced |
| **Celery powder / juice** | Natural nitrate source used in "uncured" products; converted to nitrite by bacteria |

**Nitrite** prevents botulism in cured meats, creates the pink cured color and adds characteristic cured flavor. It is toxic in large amounts, so curing salts are measured precisely by weight.

**Standard Cure #1 rate:** 1 level teaspoon (about 5.5 to 6 g) per 2.3 kg (5 lb) of meat, or 0.25% of meat weight, giving about 156 ppm nitrite.

**Standard Cure #2 rate:** 0.25% of meat weight for dry-cured products.

## 7.3 Curing Methods

### Dry Cure (Traditional Salt Box)

Meat is packed or rubbed generously with salt (plus sugar and spices) and turned and re-salted periodically. Excess salt is rinsed off afterward. Produces salty, long-keeping meat (country ham, salt pork).

**Time rule (traditional):** About 1.5 to 2 days per 500 g (1 lb) for hams.

### Equilibrium Cure (Modern, Precise)

Salt, sugar and cure are measured as a percentage of meat weight; the meat is sealed in a bag with the mixture and refrigerated until salt distributes evenly. Cannot become too salty.

| Product | Salt | Sugar | Cure #1 / #2 | Time (refrigerated) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bacon | 2 to 2.5% | 1 to 2% | 0.25% Cure #1 | 7 to 10 days |
| Pancetta | 2.75 to 3% | 1% | 0.25% Cure #1 or #2 | 10 to 14 days, then hang |
| Guanciale | 3% | 1 to 2% | 0.25% Cure #2 | 7 to 14 days, then hang |
| Coppa / capicola | 2.75 to 3% | 0.5 to 1% | 0.25% Cure #2 | 10 to 14 days, then hang |
| Lonza (pork loin) | 2.75 to 3% | 0.5 to 1% | 0.25% Cure #2 | 7 to 14 days, then hang |
| Bresaola | 2.75 to 3% | 1 to 2% | 0.25% Cure #2 | 10 to 14 days, then hang |
| Duck prosciutto | 3% | 0 to 1% | 0.25% Cure #2 (optional) | 5 to 7 days, then hang |

**Rule of thumb for equilibrium curing time:** About 1 day per 6 mm (1/4 inch) of thickness, plus 2 days.

### Wet Cure (Brine Cure)

Meat is submerged in or injected with a salt-sugar-cure brine.

**Typical ham or corned beef brine (per 4 liters / 1 gallon of water):**

- 225 to 340 g (1 to 1.5 cups) kosher salt
- 100 to 200 g (0.5 to 1 cup) sugar
- 40 g (8 level teaspoons / about 1.4 oz) Cure #1
- Spices: peppercorns, bay, mustard seed, garlic, coriander, juniper

**Time:** About 1 day per 500 g (1 lb), or 1 day per 2.5 cm (1 inch) of thickness; inject large hams along the bone with brine (about 10% of meat weight) to cure from the inside.

**Products:** Corned beef (brisket), wet-cured ham, pastrami (corned then smoked), Canadian bacon, pickled tongue, brined turkey (salt only, cooked soon).

### Brining Without Cure (Flavor and Moisture)

Short soaks to season and keep meat juicy, not to preserve.

- 5 to 6% brine (about 50 to 60 g salt per liter) for poultry and pork.
- 1 hour per 500 g of meat, up to 24 hours for a whole turkey.
- Dry brining: about 1% salt rubbed on meat 1 to 3 days ahead.

## 7.4 Dry-Cured Whole Muscles (Charcuterie)

After curing, these are hung to dry and age.

**Drying chamber conditions:**

- **Temperature:** 10 to 15°C (50 to 59°F)
- **Humidity:** 70 to 80%, starting higher and dropping slightly
- **Airflow:** Gentle and steady
- **Target weight loss:** 30 to 40% of starting weight

Home chambers are commonly a converted refrigerator or wine cooler with a temperature controller, humidifier and dehumidifier.

| Product | Cut | Notes | Aging Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prosciutto / Parma ham** | Pork hind leg | Salt only (no nitrite in Parma); fat surface protected with lard-flour paste (sugna) | 12 to 36 months |
| **Jamón serrano / ibérico** | Pork hind leg | Salted, washed, aged in mountain air | 12 to 48 months |
| **Country ham (US)** | Pork hind leg | Heavy salt cure, often smoked, aged through summer | 3 to 24 months |
| **Speck** | Pork leg | Cured, cold smoked, air-dried | 5 to 6 months |
| **Lonza** | Pork loin | | 3 to 6 weeks |
| **Coppa** | Pork neck/shoulder muscle | Cased or wrapped | 2 to 4 months |
| **Bresaola** | Beef eye of round | Cased or netted | 1 to 3 months |
| **Culatello** | Pork thigh muscle | Cased in bladder; aged in humid river-valley cellars | 12+ months |
| **Lardo** | Pork back fat | Cured with salt, herbs and garlic in marble boxes | 6 to 12 months |
| **Guanciale** | Pork jowl | | 3 to 6 weeks |
| **Pancetta** | Pork belly | Flat or rolled | 2 to 4 weeks (rolled) |
| **Duck breast prosciutto** | Duck breast | Good beginner project | 1 to 3 weeks |
| **Lomo** | Pork loin | Spanish; coated in paprika | 2 to 3 months |

**Mold:** White powdery mold (often *Penicillium nalgiovense*, sometimes added deliberately) is beneficial and protects the surface. Green or blue fuzzy spots are wiped with vinegar. Black, pink, yellow or hairy mold means discard.

**Case hardening:** Surface dries too fast when humidity is too low, trapping moisture inside. Raise humidity or wrap in plastic for a few days to equalize.

## 7.5 Fermented Dry Sausages (Salami)

1. **Grind** chilled pork (and/or beef) and back fat (about 70% lean, 30% fat). Keep everything near freezing.
2. **Mix** with 2.5 to 3% salt, 0.25% Cure #2, 0.3 to 1% dextrose (food for bacteria), spices and a **starter culture** (selected lactic acid bacteria and staphylococci).
3. **Stuff** tightly into natural or collagen casings; prick air pockets.
4. **Ferment** at 18 to 24°C (65 to 75°F) and 85 to 90% humidity for 1 to 3 days until pH drops below **5.3** (ideally 4.8 to 5.2). This acid step protects against pathogens before drying.
5. **Dry** at 10 to 15°C and 70 to 80% humidity until 30 to 40% weight loss (3 weeks to 3 months).

**Examples:** Genoa salami, soppressata, chorizo (Spanish dry), saucisson sec, finocchiona, pepperoni, landjäger (pressed, smoked, dried), summer sausage (fermented then cooked or smoked), nham / nem chua (Thai and Vietnamese fermented pork with rice and garlic, eaten within days).

A pH meter is the key tool for safe fermented sausage.

## 7.6 Salt Pork, Salt Beef and Barrel Meats

- **Salt pork:** Fatty pork belly or back packed in salt and brine in crocks or barrels; kept for months; soaked before cooking. Used in beans and chowders.
- **Salt beef / bully beef:** Historically ship provisions; beef in heavy brine in barrels.
- **Petit salé:** French lightly salted pork.

## 7.7 Salt Fish

| Product | Method |
|---|---|
| **Salt cod (bacalao, baccalà, bacalhau)** | Cod split, heavily salted in stacks for 2 to 3 weeks, then air-dried; soaked 24 to 72 hours in several changes of cold water before cooking |
| **Salted herring** | Gutted herring packed in barrels with salt; brine forms naturally |
| **Matjes herring** | Young herring lightly salted; enzymes from the gut ripen it |
| **Anchovies (salt-packed)** | Layered in salt under weight for 2 to 6 months; filleted and stored in oil or salt |
| **Gravlax** | Salmon fillet covered with equal parts salt and sugar plus dill (about 2 to 3 tablespoons each per 500 g), weighted, refrigerated 2 to 3 days, turned twice daily; use previously frozen fish for parasite safety |
| **Lox** | Salmon belly cured in salt brine (not smoked) |
| **Bottarga** | Salted, pressed, dried mullet or tuna roe |
| **Salted mackerel, pollock** | Similar to cod |
| **Dried salted shrimp** | Asia; salted, boiled and sun-dried |

## 7.8 Salt-Preserved Vegetables and Others

| Product | Method |
|---|---|
| **Salted green beans** | Layered with about 1 part salt to 4 to 6 parts beans in crocks; rinsed and soaked before use; historic European method |
| **Herbes salées (Quebec)** | Chopped herbs, green onions and vegetables layered with salt; refrigerated for months |
| **Preserved lemons** | Lemons quartered (not cut through), packed with salt (about 2 tablespoons per lemon), pressed into a jar and topped with juice; ready in 3 to 4 weeks, keep a year refrigerated |
| **Salted herbs and greens** | Chopped, mixed with 20% salt by weight, packed in jars |
| **Umeboshi** | Ume fruit salted at 18 to 20% for weeks, sun-dried 3 days, often with red shiso (Section 10.7) |
| **Salted egg yolks** | Yolks buried in equal parts salt and sugar for 4 to 7 days, rinsed and dried; grated like cheese |
| **Salted duck eggs** | Section 16 |
| **Salted cabbage / suan cai** | Salted and fermented (Section 11) |
| **Salted capers** | Capers layered in coarse salt; rinse before use |

# 8. Smoking

## 8.1 How Smoke Preserves

- **Phenols and other compounds** in smoke are antimicrobial and antioxidant.
- **Surface drying** creates a protective skin (pellicle).
- **Heat** in hot smoking cooks and pasteurizes.
- Smoke alone preserves weakly; it is combined with salting, curing and drying for long keeping.

## 8.2 Cold Smoking vs. Hot Smoking

| | Cold Smoking | Hot Smoking |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Below 30°C (86°F), ideally below 25°C (77°F) | 52 to 107°C (125 to 225°F) |
| **Duration** | Hours to weeks (often in sessions) | Hours |
| **Cooks food?** | No | Yes |
| **Required prep** | Must be cured first (salt, nitrite) | Curing optional |
| **Examples** | Smoked salmon (lox-style), prosciutto-style speck, cold-smoked bacon, cheese, butter, salt, nuts, country ham | Smoked salmon (kippered), barbecue, smoked sausage, smoked turkey, hot-smoked trout |
| **Storage** | Depends on cure and drying | Refrigerate; freeze for long term |

Cold smoking is done in cold weather or with a smoke generator outside the smoking chamber so heat dissipates.

## 8.3 Pellicle

Before smoking, cured meat or fish is rinsed, patted dry and air-dried on a rack in the refrigerator or a cool breezy place for 1 to 12 hours until the surface feels tacky. The pellicle helps smoke adhere and gives an even color.

## 8.4 Woods

| Wood | Flavor | Best With |
|---|---|---|
| Alder | Light, delicate | Fish, especially salmon |
| Apple | Mild, sweet, fruity | Pork, poultry, cheese |
| Cherry | Mild, sweet; reddish color | Pork, poultry, beef |
| Pecan | Mild, nutty | Pork, poultry |
| Maple | Mild, sweet | Bacon, ham, cheese, vegetables |
| Hickory | Strong, bacon-like | Pork, bacon, ham, beef |
| Oak | Medium; versatile | Beef, sausage, all-purpose |
| Mesquite | Very strong, earthy | Beef (short cooks) |
| Beech | Mild | European hams and sausages, fish |
| Juniper (branches/berries) | Aromatic | Northern European meats and fish |
| Peat | Earthy | Fish (traditional in Scotland, Ireland) |
| Tea, rice and sugar | Aromatic | Chinese tea-smoked duck and eggs |

**Avoid:** Pine, spruce, fir, cedar (resinous, soot), painted, treated or pressure-treated wood, plywood, unknown wood, moldy wood.

**Forms:** Chunks (charcoal smokers), chips (short sessions, electric smokers), pellets (pellet grills, smoke tubes), sawdust (cold smoke generators), logs (offset smokers).

**Good smoke:** Thin, pale blue, nearly invisible. Thick white or grey billowing smoke deposits bitter creosote.

## 8.5 Smoker Types

- **Smokehouse:** Building with a firebox or separate fire pit connected by an underground pipe or trench (keeps food cool for cold smoking).
- **Offset smoker:** Firebox beside the cooking chamber.
- **Kettle and kamado grills:** Indirect heat with wood chunks.
- **Vertical water smoker:** Water pan moderates heat.
- **Electric and pellet smokers:** Thermostatic control.
- **Cold smoke generator / smoke tube:** Smoldering sawdust or pellets in a maze tray inside a cool chamber.
- **Cardboard box or barrel smoker:** Improvised cold smoker with an external smoke source.

## 8.6 Smoked Meats

| Product | Method | Internal Temp |
|---|---|---|
| Bacon (hot smoked) | Cured belly smoked at 70 to 90°C | 66°C (150°F) |
| Ham (smoked) | Cured, smoked at 60 to 80°C | 66 to 68°C (150 to 155°F) for cooked ham |
| Smoked sausage | Cured sausage smoked then cooked | 66 to 71°C (150 to 160°F); 74°C (165°F) for poultry |
| Pastrami | Corned beef, spice-crusted, smoked | 74 to 93°C (165 to 200°F) |
| Brisket / pulled pork | Barbecue at 107 to 135°C | 90 to 96°C (195 to 205°F) for tenderness |
| Smoked turkey | Brined or cured, smoked | 74°C (165°F) |
| Jerky (smoked) | Strips smoked at 60 to 70°C | 71°C (160°F) |
| Andouille, kielbasa, tasso | Cured, smoked | As above |

## 8.7 Smoked Fish

1. **Brine:** 5 to 10% salt brine (often with brown sugar) for 30 minutes to 12 hours depending on thickness, or dry cure.
2. **Rinse and form a pellicle.**
3. **Smoke.**

| Style | Method |
|---|---|
| **Hot-smoked salmon / trout** | Smoke gradually rising from about 50°C to 80°C until fish reaches **63°C (145°F) internal for at least 30 minutes**; flaky texture |
| **Cold-smoked salmon** | Dry cure 12 to 24 hours, rinse, pellicle, cold smoke below 27°C for 6 to 24 hours; silky texture; previously frozen fish recommended for parasite safety |
| **Kippers** | Split herring, brined and cold smoked |
| **Finnan haddie** | Haddock brined and cold smoked (Scotland) |
| **Arbroath smokies** | Whole haddock hot smoked over hardwood |
| **Smoked mackerel** | Brined and hot smoked |
| **Smoked oysters, mussels** | Steamed open, brined, smoked |
| **Katsuobushi** | Skipjack tuna simmered, then smoked and dried repeatedly over weeks, then molded with a beneficial fungus; becomes wood-hard; shaved into flakes (Section 19) |

**Storage:** Hot-smoked fish keeps about 2 weeks refrigerated, 2 to 3 months frozen. Vacuum-packed smoked fish must be kept refrigerated or frozen.

## 8.8 Smoking Other Foods

| Food | Method |
|---|---|
| Cheese | Cold smoke 1 to 4 hours below 27°C; rest wrapped in the fridge 1 to 2 weeks for flavor to mellow |
| Butter | Cold smoke chilled butter 1 to 2 hours |
| Salt | Spread thin; smoke 12 to 48 hours |
| Nuts | Toss with oil and salt; hot smoke 1 to 2 hours |
| Garlic, onions | Whole heads, hot or cold smoke |
| Peppers (chipotle) | Ripe red jalapeños smoked and dried for days until leathery |
| Paprika (pimentón) | Peppers smoke-dried over oak, then ground |
| Tea (lapsang souchong) | Leaves smoked over pine fires |
| Eggs | Hard-cooked, peeled, smoked 30 to 60 minutes |
| Tomatoes | Halved, smoked, then dried or canned as sauce |
| Water / ice | Smoked water for cocktails |

# 9. Canning

## 9.1 How Canning Works

Food is sealed in jars and heated long enough to destroy spoilage organisms (and, for low-acid foods, botulism spores). Cooling forms a vacuum seal that keeps new microbes out.

**Two safe home methods:**

| Method | Temperature | For |
|---|---|---|
| **Boiling water bath** (or atmospheric steam canner) | 100°C (212°F) at sea level | High-acid foods (pH 4.6 or below) |
| **Pressure canning** | 116 to 121°C (240 to 250°F) | Low-acid foods (above pH 4.6) |

Only **tested recipes** from reliable sources (USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, National Center for Home Food Preservation, Ball Blue Book, university extension services) give safe times. Density, acidity and jar size all change heat penetration.

## 9.2 Equipment

- **Mason jars:** Half-pint (250 mL), pint (500 mL), quart (1 L); tempered glass made for canning. Reusable if not chipped.
- **Two-piece lids:** Flat lids with sealing compound (single use) and screw bands (reusable). Reusable lids (rubber ring and plastic or glass lid) also exist.
- **Water bath canner:** Large pot with a rack; deep enough for 2.5 cm (1 inch) of water above jars.
- **Pressure canner:** Heavy pot with locking lid, vent, and dial gauge or weighted gauge. A pressure cooker (Instant Pot type) is not a canner unless specifically approved; most cannot verify temperature.
- **Steam canner:** Shallow base with a domed lid; approved for high-acid foods with processing times of 45 minutes or less.
- **Tools:** Jar lifter, wide-mouth funnel, headspace tool / bubble remover, magnetic lid wand, clean towels, timer, ladle.

## 9.3 Headspace

The space between food and the jar rim allows expansion and vacuum formation.

| Food | Headspace |
|---|---|
| Jams, jellies, fruit juices | 6 mm (1/4 inch) |
| Fruits, tomatoes, pickles, salsas, sauces | 1.25 cm (1/2 inch) |
| Low-acid vegetables | 2.5 cm (1 inch) |
| Meats, poultry, fish, dry beans | 2.5 to 3 cm (1 to 1.25 inches) |

## 9.4 Packing Methods

- **Raw pack (cold pack):** Raw food packed into jars and covered with hot liquid. Faster but food may float and shrink.
- **Hot pack:** Food heated in liquid before packing. Removes air, shrinks food (more fits), less floating, better color.

## 9.5 Water Bath Canning Procedure

1. **Inspect** jars for chips and cracks. Wash jars, lids and bands in hot soapy water.
2. **Keep jars hot** (in the canner, dishwasher or oven at low heat) to prevent thermal shock. Sterilize jars (boil 10 minutes) only for recipes processed less than 10 minutes.
3. **Heat water** in the canner to about 60°C (140°F) for raw pack or 82°C (180°F) for hot pack.
4. **Fill** hot jars with food, leaving correct headspace.
5. **Remove air bubbles** by sliding a non-metallic spatula around the inside.
6. **Wipe rims** with a clean damp cloth (vinegar helps for greasy foods).
7. **Apply lids and bands** fingertip-tight (not cranked down; air must escape).
8. **Load** jars onto the rack in the canner. Water must cover jars by at least 2.5 cm (1 inch).
9. **Bring to a full rolling boil**, then start timing. Keep boiling the entire time; if boiling stops, restart timing.
10. **Turn off heat**, remove the lid and let jars sit 5 minutes.
11. **Lift jars out** straight up without tilting and set on a towel, 2.5 cm apart, away from drafts.
12. **Cool 12 to 24 hours** undisturbed. Lids "ping" as they seal.
13. **Check seals:** Center of lid should be concave and not flex when pressed. Unsealed jars go in the fridge or are reprocessed within 24 hours with a new lid.
14. **Remove bands**, wash jars, label with contents and date, store.

## 9.6 Pressure Canning Procedure

1. Prepare jars and food as above.
2. Put the rack and 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 inches) of hot water in the canner (or the amount the manufacturer specifies).
3. Load jars. Lock the lid with the vent open (weight off).
4. Heat on high until steam flows steadily from the vent. **Vent for 10 minutes** to drive out air (air pockets lower the temperature).
5. Place the weight or close the petcock.
6. Bring to the required pressure, then **start timing**.
7. Hold pressure steady for the full time. If pressure drops below the target, bring it back up and restart timing from zero.
8. Turn off heat. **Let pressure drop naturally** to zero; do not force-cool, which causes siphoning (liquid loss) and unsafe processing.
9. Wait 10 more minutes after the gauge reads zero, then remove the weight and open the lid away from the face.
10. Let jars sit 10 minutes in the canner, then remove and cool as with water bath.

**Dial gauges** should be tested for accuracy yearly (many extension offices test them).

## 9.7 Altitude Adjustments

Water boils at lower temperatures at altitude, so processing changes.

**Water bath / steam canning (add to processing time):**

| Altitude | Add |
|---|---|

## 9.10 Foods Not Safe for Home Canning

- **Purées of pumpkin or winter squash** (too dense for heat penetration; can cubes instead and purée when opening)
- **Dairy** (milk, cream, butter, cheese)
- **Flour, cornstarch, rice, pasta, bread or other thickeners** in canned foods (thicken when serving); Clear Jel (modified cornstarch) is the only thickener approved for tested pie filling recipes
- **Eggs** (pickled or otherwise)
- **Oils and lard** in large amounts (fat shields bacteria from heat)
- **Mashed or refried foods** (beans, potatoes)
- **Cake or bread in jars**
- **Chocolate sauce, nut butters**
- **Pesto**
- **Untested or creative recipes** in low-acid categories

**Unsafe methods:** Oven canning, dishwasher canning, microwave canning, open kettle canning (pouring hot food into jars without processing), inversion sealing, paraffin wax seals on jams, aspirin as a preservative, solar canning, electric multicookers not approved for canning.

## 9.11 Canned Goods Storage

- Store at 10 to 21°C (50 to 70°F) in a dark, dry place.
- Remove bands so failed seals are visible.
- Best quality within 12 to 18 months; safe as long as the seal holds, though color, texture and nutrients decline.
- Avoid stacking more than two jars high (pressure on lids can mask failed seals).

## 9.12 Signs of Spoilage

Discard without tasting if:

- Lid bulges, is loose or unsealed
- Liquid spurts when opened
- Mold, cloudiness (not explained by starch or minerals), foam or bubbles rising
- Off odor or unusual smell
- Mushy, slimy or discolored food

Dispose of suspect low-acid foods carefully: keep sealed, bag them and discard; or detoxify by boiling jar and contents covered in water for 30 minutes before disposal. Wash surfaces with a bleach solution (1 part bleach to 5 parts water).

# 10. Pickling

## 10.1 Types of Pickles

| Type | Preservation Agent | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| **Fresh-pack (quick process) pickles** | Vinegar brine | Water bath canned; shelf-stable |
| **Refrigerator pickles** | Vinegar brine | Refrigerated, 1 to 3 months |
| **Fermented pickles** | Lactic acid from fermentation in salt brine (Section 11) | Cool storage, refrigerated or canned |
| **Oil pickles (achaar)** | Salt, acid, oil and spices | Room temperature (traditional) |
| **Salt pickles** | Heavy salt | Room temperature |
| **Sweet pickles** | Vinegar and sugar | Canned |
| **Soy, miso or rice bran pickles** | Salt and fermentation | Refrigerated or cool |

## 10.2 Vinegar Pickle Fundamentals

- **Vinegar:** Use commercial vinegar of known **5% acidity** for shelf-stable pickles. Homemade vinegar acidity varies and is only for refrigerator pickles unless tested.
- **Ratio:** Tested shelf-stable recipes generally use at least equal parts vinegar and water, often more vinegar. Never dilute a tested recipe's vinegar.
- **Salt:** Canning or pickling salt.
- **Water:** Soft water; hard water minerals cloud brine and discolor pickles.
- **Produce:** Very fresh, firm, unwaxed. Pickling cucumbers (Kirby type) within 24 hours of harvest. Cut 1.5 mm (1/16 inch) off the **blossom end**, which contains softening enzymes.
- **Crispness:** Soak in ice water 4 to 5 hours before pickling; add calcium chloride (Pickle Crisp, about 1/8 teaspoon per pint) or grape, oak, horseradish or black tea leaves (tannins). Pickling lime soaks are also used but require thorough rinsing.
- **Spices:** Fresh whole spices. Ground spices cloud brine.
- **Garlic** sometimes turns blue or green from a harmless reaction with acid and trace copper or sulfur compounds.

## 10.3 Basic Refrigerator Pickle Brine

- 1 cup vinegar (white, cider or rice), 1 cup water, 1 tablespoon salt, 0 to 2 tablespoons sugar.
- Heat to dissolve; pour over vegetables and spices in jars.
- Refrigerate; ready in 24 hours to 1 week; keeps 1 to 3 months.

**Good refrigerator pickle candidates:** Cucumbers, red onions, carrots, radishes, jalapeños, green beans, cauliflower, beets (cooked), cabbage, fennel, asparagus, turnips, daikon, okra, garlic scapes, ramps, watermelon rind, grapes, cherries, peaches.

## 10.6 Oil Pickles (Achaar and Similar)

Indian and South Asian pickles preserve with salt, acid (from mango, lime or vinegar), mustard oil and spices, often sun-matured.

**General method (mango achaar):**

1. Cut unripe green mango into pieces; toss with salt (about 10% by weight) and turmeric.
2. Sun-dry or rest 1 to 3 days to draw out water.
3. Mix with spice blend (mustard seed, fenugreek, nigella, fennel, chili, asafoetida).
4. Heat mustard oil to smoking, cool, pour over to cover completely.
5. Keep in a sterilized glass or ceramic jar in the sun 1 to 2 weeks, stirring with a dry spoon.
6. Keep fruit submerged under oil. Always use a dry spoon; water introduces mold.

Other achaars: lime (nimbu), green chili, garlic, ginger, carrot, gooseberry (amla), lotus stem. The combination of high salt, acid and spice provides the preservation; the oil seals out air.

# 11. Fermentation

## 11.1 How Fermentation Preserves

Beneficial microbes (mainly lactic acid bacteria, yeasts and molds) consume sugars and produce acids, alcohol or other compounds that prevent spoilage and pathogens while transforming flavor. Lactic acid fermentation drops pH below 4.6, usually to 3.3 to 4.0.

## 11.2 Lacto-Fermented Vegetables

### Salt Ratios

| Method | Salt | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Dry salting** (vegetable makes its own brine) | 2 to 2.5% of vegetable weight | Sauerkraut, shredded vegetables, kimchi (after brining) |
| **Brine** | 2 to 3.5% of water weight for most vegetables; 3.5 to 5% for cucumbers (firmer) | Whole or chopped vegetables |
| **Hot sauce mash** | 2 to 5% of pepper weight | Chili mash |

**Brine math:** 20 g salt per liter = 2% brine. 35 g per liter = 3.5%. 50 g per liter = 5%.

### General Method

1. Use clean equipment: glass jars, ceramic crocks or food-grade plastic. Avoid reactive metals.
2. Prepare vegetables (shred, slice, chop or whole).
3. Add salt and spices; massage (kraut) or cover with brine.
4. **Keep vegetables submerged** under brine with a weight (glass weight, small jar, clean stone, cabbage leaf, zip bag of brine).
5. Cover with an airlock lid, loose lid or cloth; burp sealed jars daily.
6. Ferment at **18 to 22°C (64 to 72°F)** away from direct sun. Warmer is faster but softer; cooler is slower with better flavor.
7. Taste after a few days. When sour enough, refrigerate or move to cold storage to slow fermentation.

### Fermentation Times

| Food | Time at 18 to 22°C |
|---|---|
| Sauerkraut | 3 to 6 weeks (can be eaten after 1 week) |
| Kimchi | 1 to 5 days, then refrigerated |
| Dill pickles (fermented) | 1 to 4 weeks |
| Carrots, green beans, cauliflower | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Hot sauce mash | 1 week to several months |
| Beets (kvass) | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Garlic | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Radishes, turnips | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Salsa, relishes | 2 to 5 days |

### Signs and Troubleshooting

| Observation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bubbles, cloudy brine, sour smell | Normal active fermentation |
| White sediment at the bottom | Dead bacteria and yeast; normal |
| Flat white film on the surface (**kahm yeast**) | Harmless but can add off-flavor; skim it off |
| Fuzzy colored mold (blue, green, black, pink) | Mold; discard if it penetrates below the surface or covers food; surface spots on brine above submerged vegetables are often skimmed but caution is advised |
| Soft, mushy vegetables | Too warm, too little salt, blossom ends left on, or overfermented |
| Slimy brine | Early stage of some ferments (often clears) or too warm |
| Pink kraut | Too little salt or uneven salt; discard |
| Rotten smell | Discard |

### Sauerkraut

1. Remove outer leaves; core and finely shred cabbage.
2. Weigh. Add 2% salt (20 g per kg); add caraway or juniper if desired.
3. Massage or pound 5 to 10 minutes until juicy.
4. Pack tightly into a crock or jar; brine should rise above the cabbage. Top up with 2% brine if needed.
5. Weight down; ferment 3 to 6 weeks.
6. Refrigerate up to 6 months or water-bath can (Section 9.8).

### Kimchi (Napa Cabbage, Baechu)

1. Quarter Napa cabbage lengthwise; salt between leaves or soak in about 10% brine for 6 to 8 hours, turning occasionally, until leaves bend without snapping.
2. Rinse thoroughly 3 times; drain 1 to 2 hours.
3. Make paste: rice flour porridge, gochugaru (Korean chili flakes), garlic, ginger, fish sauce or salted shrimp, sugar; mix in sliced radish, green onions and chives.
4. Rub paste between every leaf.
5. Pack tightly into jars, pressing out air.
6. Ferment 1 to 3 days at room temperature, then refrigerate. Flavor deepens for weeks; old sour kimchi is used in stews.

**Other kimchi:** Kkakdugi (cubed radish), oi sobagi (stuffed cucumber), dongchimi (water kimchi with radish), baek kimchi (white, no chili), chonggak (ponytail radish), gat (mustard leaf).

### Fermented Hot Sauce

1. Stem chilies; chop with garlic, onion or fruit.
2. Mix with 2 to 3% salt by weight or cover with 3 to 5% brine.
3. Ferment 1 to 8 weeks.
4. Blend with some brine and vinegar to taste; strain if desired.
5. Adding vinegar to lower pH below 4.0 and refrigerating stabilizes the sauce.

# 12. Sugar Preservation: Jams, Jellies and Preserves

## 12.1 How Sugar Preserves

High sugar concentrations bind water and lower water activity. Jams and jellies reach about **65% sugar** in the finished product. Sugar also helps pectin gel, preserves color and adds flavor.

## 12.2 Types of Sweet Preserves

| Product | Description |
|---|---|
| **Jelly** | Clear fruit juice set with pectin; holds its shape |
| **Jam** | Crushed or chopped fruit cooked with sugar until it sets |
| **Preserves** | Whole or large pieces of fruit in thick syrup or soft jelly |
| **Conserve** | Jam with mixed fruits, often with nuts and dried fruit |
| **Marmalade** | Soft jelly with suspended citrus peel |
| **Fruit butter** | Fruit pulp cooked slowly to a thick, smooth spread (apple, pear, pumpkin; pumpkin butter is not safe to can) |
| **Fruit curd** | Fruit juice with eggs and butter; refrigerate or freeze only |
| **Fruit cheese / paste** | Very thick fruit purée cooked until it slices (quince paste / membrillo, guava paste, damson cheese) |
| **Compote** | Fruit cooked in syrup; short-keeping unless canned |
| **Spoon sweets (glyka tou koutaliou)** | Greek fruit preserved whole in heavy syrup |
| **Candied fruit** | Fruit saturated with sugar syrup until translucent, then dried |
| **Crystallized fruit** | Candied fruit rolled in sugar |
| **Glacé fruit** | Candied fruit with a glossy syrup coating |
| **Fruit syrups** | Strained juice cooked with sugar |
| **Pepper jelly** | Peppers in vinegar-pectin-sugar jelly |
| **Herb and flower jellies** | Infused liquid set with pectin (mint, lavender, rose petal, violet, dandelion) |

## 12.3 The Four Elements of a Gel

| Element | Role |
|---|---|
| **Fruit** | Flavor; supplies some pectin and acid |
| **Pectin** | Fiber that forms the gel network |
| **Acid** | Needed for pectin to set (pH about 2.8 to 3.4); brightens flavor |
| **Sugar** | Pulls water away so pectin chains bond; preserves |

### Natural Pectin and Acid in Fruit

| Pectin and Acid | Fruits |
|---|---|
| **High pectin, high acid** | Tart apples, crabapples, quince, cranberries, currants, gooseberries, sour plums, lemons, limes, sour oranges, grapes (Concord) |
| **High pectin, low acid** | Sweet apples, ripe quince, sour cherries (moderate) |
| **Low pectin, high acid** | Apricots, sour cherries, pineapple, rhubarb, strawberries (moderate acid) |
| **Low pectin, low acid** | Blueberries, peaches, pears, sweet cherries, figs, raspberries, guava (varies), elderberries |

Slightly underripe fruit has more pectin; ripe fruit has more flavor. Mixing about 1/4 underripe with 3/4 ripe fruit balances both.

### Pectin Types

| Pectin | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Homemade apple pectin** | Simmer tart apple peels and cores (or crabapples) in water, strain; adds pectin to low-pectin fruits |
| **Regular (high methoxyl) powdered pectin** | Requires full sugar; added to fruit before sugar |
| **Liquid pectin** | Added after sugar at the end of cooking |
| **Low or no sugar pectin (low methoxyl)** | Sets with calcium instead of sugar (Pomona's type); allows honey or little sweetener |
| **Citrus pith and seeds** | Tied in cheesecloth and cooked with marmalade |

## 12.4 Jam Without Added Pectin

1. Prepare fruit; measure.
2. Combine with sugar (commonly 3/4 to 1 part sugar per part fruit by weight) and lemon juice.
3. Optionally macerate overnight to draw out juice.
4. Bring to a boil, stirring; boil rapidly.
5. Test for set:
   - **Temperature:** 104.5°C (220°F) at sea level, or 4.5°C (8°F) above the boiling point of water at the current altitude.
   - **Cold plate (wrinkle) test:** Drop jam on a chilled plate, return to the freezer 1 minute; push with a finger. Set jam wrinkles.
   - **Sheet test:** Jelly drips from a cold metal spoon in a sheet rather than separate drops.
6. Skim foam (a small pat of butter reduces foaming).
7. Ladle into hot jars, 6 mm headspace, process (Section 9.8).

## 12.5 Jelly

1. **Extract juice:** Simmer fruit with water until soft. Drip through a dampened jelly bag or several layers of cheesecloth for several hours. Squeezing gives more juice but a cloudy jelly.
2. **Pectin test (optional):** Mix 1 teaspoon cooled juice with 1 tablespoon rubbing alcohol (do not taste). A single firm clot means high pectin; small pieces mean add pectin.
3. **Cook:** Usually 3/4 to 1 cup sugar per cup of juice for high-pectin juice. Boil to the gel point.
4. Skim, jar and process.

Make jelly in small batches (4 to 6 cups juice); larger batches cook too long and set poorly.

## 12.6 Marmalade

1. Slice citrus (Seville oranges are traditional for their bitterness and pectin) thinly or shred peel.
2. Soak overnight in water to soften peel.
3. Simmer until peel is very tender (1 to 2 hours); peel will not soften after sugar is added.
4. Add sugar (often equal weight to the cooked mixture); boil rapidly to the set point.
5. Rest 10 minutes before jarring so peel stays suspended.

## 12.7 Fruit Butter

1. Cook fruit until soft; purée and strain.
2. Add sugar (optional, about 1/4 to 1/2 cup per cup of purée) and spices.
3. Cook slowly, stirring frequently, until thick enough to mound on a spoon with no liquid separating (2 to 6 hours on stovetop; slow cooker or oven methods prevent scorching).
4. Jar and process.

## 12.8 Fruit in Syrup

| Syrup | Sugar per Water | Approx. Sugar % | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extra light | 1.25 cups per 5.5 cups | About 20% | Sweet fruits, low sugar |
| Light | 2.25 cups per 5.25 cups | About 30% | Sweet apples, cherries, grapes |
| Medium | 3.75 cups per 5 cups | About 40% | Apricots, peaches, pears, sour apples |
| Heavy | 5.25 cups per 4.25 cups | About 50% | Very sour fruit |

Fruit can also be canned in water or juice; sugar improves color and texture but is not needed for safety in water bath canning of fruit.

## 12.9 Candied Fruit and Peel

1. **Prepare:** Slice citrus peel into strips (pith left or partly removed), cut fruit into pieces or keep small fruits whole (cherries, kumquats).
2. **Blanch peel** 2 to 3 times in fresh boiling water to remove bitterness.
3. **Syrup:** Start with about 1 part sugar to 1 part water. Simmer fruit gently until translucent (30 to 90 minutes). Traditional long methods increase syrup strength gradually over 1 to 2 weeks, letting fruit soak overnight between boilings so it stays plump instead of shriveling.
4. **Dry** on a rack 1 to 3 days until tacky.
5. **Coat** in sugar (crystallized) or dip in chocolate.
6. Store airtight at room temperature for months.

**Candied ginger:** Peel and slice ginger; simmer in water until tender; simmer in equal parts sugar and water until syrup thickens; drain, roll in sugar and dry. The leftover syrup is ginger syrup.

## 12.10 Sugar Storage of Fresh Fruit

- **Sugar-packed berries:** Layered with sugar in the freezer.
- **Fruit in sugar (Russian varenye-style "raw jam"):** Mashed berries with equal or double weight of sugar, refrigerated; preserves raw flavor.
- **Rumtopf:** Section 14.

## 12.11 Low-Sugar Preserves

- Use low or no-sugar pectin.
- Refrigerator and freezer jams keep 3 weeks refrigerated, a year frozen.
- Sweeten with honey, fruit juice concentrate or non-nutritive sweeteners in recipes designed for them.
- Low-sugar jams have shorter opened shelf lives.

## 12.12 Jam Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Runny | Undercooked, too little pectin or acid, batch too large | Re-cook with added pectin or lemon juice |
| Too stiff | Overcooked, too much pectin | Next batch, cook less; warm and loosen with juice |
| Crystals | Too much sugar, undissolved sugar, overcooking (grape jelly forms tartrate crystals) | Dissolve sugar fully; let grape juice settle overnight and strain before making jelly |
| Weeping | Too much acid or storage too warm | Adjust acid |
| Floating fruit | Fruit not cooked enough or jam too thin | Rest jam 5 to 10 minutes before jarring, stir |
| Mold | Poor seal, unprocessed jars, low sugar | Process in water bath; discard moldy jam |
| Dark color | Overcooking, warm storage, light exposure | Shorter boiling; store cool and dark |

# 13. Preserving in Fat and Oil

## 13.1 How Fat Preserves

A layer of fat seals out air and moisture. Fat preservation works best with food that is salted, cooked and stored cold. Fat alone does not prevent botulism, so cold storage is essential.

## 13.2 Confit

**Duck or goose confit:**

1. Salt duck legs (about 1.5 to 2% salt for modern taste, or traditional heavier salting) with garlic, thyme, bay and pepper; refrigerate 12 to 36 hours.
2. Rinse and dry.
3. Submerge in rendered duck fat in a pot or oven dish.
4. Cook gently at 80 to 95°C (175 to 200°F) fat temperature for 2 to 4 hours until meat is very tender.
5. Pack legs in a clean crock or jar; strain the fat and pour over, making sure meat is covered by at least 2.5 cm (1 inch) of fat with no meat juices at the bottom (juices spoil; drain them first).
6. Refrigerate. Keeps about 1 month refrigerated in the fat (traditionally several months in a cold cellar when heavily salted); freezes for a year.

**Other confits:** Pork belly, pork shoulder, gizzards, chicken thighs, rabbit, turkey legs, garlic confit (refrigerate, use within about a week; or freeze), onions, tomatoes (refrigerate).

## 13.3 Potted Meats and Fish

Cooked, seasoned meat or fish packed tightly into pots and sealed with clarified butter or fat.

| Product | Description |
|---|---|
| **Rillettes** | Pork (or duck, rabbit, salmon) slowly cooked in fat, shredded, mixed with fat and packed under a fat seal |
| **Potted shrimp** | Small brown shrimp in spiced butter (England) |
| **Potted beef, ham, game, cheese** | Ground cooked meat with butter and spices |
| **Pâté and terrines** | Covered with fat or aspic |
| **Foie gras au torchon** | Wrapped and poached, sealed |

Refrigerate; under an unbroken fat seal these keep 1 to 4 weeks refrigerated.

## 13.4 Pemmican

A high-energy trail and winter food developed by Indigenous peoples of North America.

1. Dry lean meat (bison, venison, beef, elk) until brittle.
2. Pound or grind into a coarse powder.
3. Render tallow or other hard fat.
4. Mix equal weights of meat powder and warm fat (traditional ratios range from about 1:1 to slightly more meat).
5. Optional: Add dried berries (saskatoons, chokecherries, cranberries, blueberries).
6. Press into bars or pack into containers or rawhide bags.

Properly made pemmican keeps for years at cool temperatures. Rancidity of the fat is the main limit.

## 13.5 Lard Crocks and Fat-Packed Meats

- **Sausages and pork in lard:** Cooked sausages, pork chops or meatballs layered in crocks and covered in hot lard; stored in cold cellars through winter (rural European and American tradition).
- **Qawarma / kavurma:** Lamb or mutton cooked in its own tail fat with salt, packed into jars and sealed by the fat (Lebanon, Turkey, Central Asia); kept through winter.
- **Smen:** Aged salted butter in North Africa (Section 17).

## 13.6 Vegetables Under Oil (Sott'olio)

Italian method for vegetables, mushrooms, artichokes and peppers.

1. Blanch or briefly cook vegetables in **vinegar** (or a vinegar-water mix) to acidify them; this is the key safety step.
2. Drain and dry thoroughly (hours to overnight on towels).
3. Pack into sterilized jars with herbs, garlic, chili.
4. Cover completely with oil, pressing out air bubbles.
5. Refrigerate. Modern guidance treats these as refrigerated foods, used within weeks.

**Oil-packed foods at room temperature** (fresh garlic in oil, fresh herbs in oil, roasted peppers in oil) can produce botulism toxin. Refrigerate and use within about 4 days, freeze, or acidify first. Volatile-oil and fresh-herb-in-oil notes stay in `EssentialOils.md`.

**Dried tomatoes in oil:** Fully dried tomatoes rehydrated in vinegar, dried, then covered with oil and refrigerated.

**Oil-cured olives:** Ripe black olives packed in coarse salt for 4 to 6 weeks (stirred and drained of bitter liquid regularly) until shriveled and no longer bitter, then rubbed with oil.

**Other olive cures:** Water curing (soaking cracked olives in fresh water changed daily for 1 to 3 weeks, then storing in brine), brine curing (8 to 10% brine for 2 to 6 months) and lye curing (commercial-style rapid debittering with food-grade lye, followed by extensive rinsing and brining).

## 13.7 Butter and Wax Seals

- **Clarified butter seal:** Poured over potted foods.
- **Wax-dipped cheese:** Section 17.
- **Paraffin on jam:** Historical; no longer recommended because seals fail and mold grows beneath.

# 14. Preserving in Alcohol

## 14.1 How Alcohol Preserves

Alcohol at about 20% or more ABV inhibits most microbes; above 40% essentially nothing grows. Alcohol also extracts flavors.

## 14.2 Methods

| Product | Method |
|---|---|
| **Rumtopf (rum pot)** | Throughout summer, layer fruits as they ripen in a stoneware crock with about half their weight in sugar; cover each addition with high-proof rum (at least 50 to 54% ABV). Keep fruit submerged and the pot cool and dark. Ready 4 to 6 weeks after the last addition; traditionally opened at Christmas |
| **Brandied cherries / peaches / pears** | Fruit packed in jars with sugar syrup and brandy (often equal parts syrup and brandy); refrigerate or water bath process per tested recipe |
| **Cherries in maraschino or bourbon** | Pitted cherries in liqueur or bourbon with sugar |
| **Fruit liqueurs** | Fruit steeped in vodka, brandy or grain spirit with sugar for weeks to months (sloe gin, limoncello, cassis, cherry bounce, nocino from green walnuts) |
| **Fruitcake** | Dense cake of dried and candied fruit; wrapped in brandy- or rum-soaked cheesecloth and "fed" with spirits weekly; keeps months to years |
| **Christmas pudding** | Steamed suet pudding with dried fruit and spirits; ages for months |
| **Mincemeat** | Dried fruit, suet, spices, sugar and brandy; matures for weeks; modern tested canning recipes exist |
| **Eggnog (aged)** | High-alcohol eggnog aged weeks in the refrigerator |
| **Tinctures** | Herbs steeped in alcohol for weeks, then strained. Volatile-oil tinctures stay in `EssentialOils.md` |
| **Vanilla extract** | Beans in vodka or rum |
| **Pickled in wine** | Some fruits and meats marinated in wine for flavor; not a long-term method |
| **Drunken shrimp / crab** | Chinese dishes marinated in Shaoxing wine; short-term |

# 15. Preserving in Honey

Honey's very low water activity (about 0.5 to 0.6), acidity (pH 3.2 to 4.5) and natural hydrogen peroxide make it a strong preservative.

| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Fruit in honey** | Sliced fruit covered in honey; refrigerate. Fruit juice dilutes the honey and allows fermentation over time |
| **Fermented honey garlic** | Peeled garlic covered in raw honey; burp daily; ferments 1 month or more; garlic juice dilutes honey enough for yeasts to work. Keep garlic submerged and monitor pH (should drop below 4.6 within days); some guidance recommends refrigeration |
| **Honey-preserved nuts** | Nuts submerged in honey |
| **Honey-cured meats** | Honey as a sweetener in cures (honey ham) |
| **Honey as a jam sweetener** | Use low-methoxyl pectin |
| **Comb honey** | Kept in the comb |
| **Mead** | Honey fermented to wine. Drink process stays in `FoodDesign.md` |
| **Ancient uses** | Meat and fruits were stored in honey in antiquity; honey is found preserved in Egyptian tombs |

Raw honey should not be given to infants under 12 months because of botulism spores.

# 16. Egg Preservation

## 16.1 Unwashed Eggs

Fresh eggs have a natural coating (**bloom** or cuticle) that seals pores. Unwashed eggs keep several weeks at room temperature and months refrigerated. Washed eggs (standard in the US) must be refrigerated.

## 16.2 Methods

| Method | How | Shelf Life |
|---|---|---|
| **Refrigeration** | 4°C or below, pointy end down | 3 to 5 weeks (store-bought), up to 3 months (farm fresh) |
| **Water glassing (lime water)** | Clean, unwashed, uncracked eggs placed pointy end down in a solution of **28 g (1 oz by weight) pickling lime (calcium hydroxide) per liter (1 quart) of water**; container covered, kept cool and dark | 8 to 18 months |
| **Sodium silicate (water glass)** | Historic version using sodium silicate solution | Similar; lime is more common today |
| **Mineral oil coating** | Rub unwashed eggs with food-grade mineral oil; store cool, pointy end down; turn monthly | 6 to 9 months |
| **Salt packing** | Eggs buried in salt in a crock | Months; whites may thicken |
| **Lard or wax coating** | Historic sealing methods | Months |
| **Freezing** | Cracked, beaten (Section 3.9) | 12 months |
| **Dehydrating** | Scrambled-cooked eggs dried and powdered, or raw eggs dried at higher temperatures (commercial pasteurized drying is safer) | Months to years |
| **Freeze-drying** | Raw beaten eggs | 10 to 25 years |
| **Pickling** | Hard-cooked eggs in vinegar brine, refrigerated | 3 to 4 months |
| **Salted duck eggs** | Raw duck eggs submerged in strong brine (about 20 to 25% salt, often with Shaoxing wine or star anise) for 3 to 5 weeks, or coated in a salt-clay or salt-ash paste | Weeks to months; yolks become firm, orange and rich |
| **Century eggs (pidan)** | Duck, chicken or quail eggs coated in an alkaline mixture of clay, lime, wood ash, salt and tea, often rolled in rice husks, for several weeks to months (modern method uses an alkaline brine). pH rises to about 12; whites become dark amber jelly, yolks dark green and creamy | Months |
| **Tea eggs** | Hard-cooked, cracked and simmered in soy-tea-spice liquid | Refrigerated, about a week |
| **Soy-marinated eggs** | Soft-boiled eggs in soy-mirin marinade (ramen eggs) | Refrigerated, 3 to 5 days |

**Using preserved eggs:** Rinse lime or oil-stored eggs; crack each into a separate bowl first. Water-glassed eggs have slightly runny whites and do not whip as well; pierce the shell before boiling as it can crack.

## 16.3 Egg Freshness Test

Place in water: fresh eggs sink and lie flat; older eggs stand on end; eggs that float should be discarded.

# 17. Dairy Preservation

## 17.1 Butter

| Form | Method | Shelf Life |
|---|---|---|
| **Salted butter** | 1.5 to 3% salt worked into washed butter | Weeks at cool room temp; months refrigerated |
| **Brined butter** | Butter balls submerged in strong brine in crocks (historical) | Months |
| **Cultured butter** | Cream fermented with lactic cultures before churning; acid improves keeping | Like regular butter |
| **Clarified butter** | Melted; milk solids and water removed | 3 months at room temperature; longer refrigerated |
| **Ghee** | Simmered until water evaporates and solids brown, then strained | 3 to 6+ months at room temperature in an airtight jar; a year refrigerated |
| **Smen / samna** | Salted butter, sometimes herbed, aged in clay pots or buried for months to years (North Africa, Middle East) | Years; strong cheese-like flavor |
| **Butter bell / crock** | Butter packed into an inverted cup sealed by water | 2 to 4 weeks at cool room temperature |
| **Freezing** | Wrapped | 6 to 9 months |
| **Canned butter** | Not a tested or recommended home method |

**Washing butter:** After churning, rinse butter in cold water and knead until water runs clear. Leftover buttermilk causes rancidity.

## 17.2 Cheese

Cheese is milk preserved by acid, salt, water removal and aging.

| Category | Examples | Keeping |
|---|---|---|
| **Fresh** | Ricotta, cottage, cream cheese, paneer, queso fresco, chèvre | Days to 2 weeks refrigerated |
| **Brined** | Feta, halloumi, brined mozzarella | Months in brine |
| **Soft ripened** | Brie, Camembert | Weeks |
| **Semi-hard** | Gouda, Cheddar (young), Monterey Jack, Havarti | Months |
| **Hard aged** | Parmigiano-Reggiano, aged Cheddar, Gruyère, Pecorino, Manchego | 1 to 3+ years |
| **Dried cheese** | Kurut (Central Asia), chhurpi (Himalayas, yak milk, rock-hard), jameed (Jordan) | Years |
| **Smoked** | Scamorza, smoked Gouda | Months |

**Home preservation methods:**

- **Waxing:** Hard cheeses dipped 2 to 3 times in cheese wax (not paraffin, which cracks) at about 90°C to seal; aged at 10 to 13°C and 80 to 85% humidity.
- **Bandaging:** Cheddar wrapped in cloth rubbed with lard.
- **Vacuum sealing:** Aged cheese.
- **Brining:** Feta and halloumi stored in 8 to 10% brine.
- **Oil marinating:** Small fresh cheeses (labneh balls, goat cheese) in oil with herbs, refrigerated.
- **Cheese caves:** Converted refrigerators or wine coolers with humidity control.
- **Drying:** Grating hard cheese and drying it; or making dried cheese balls.

**Mold on cheese:** Hard cheeses: cut off at least 2.5 cm (1 inch) around and below mold. Soft and shredded cheeses with mold: discard.

## 17.3 Fermented Milk

| Product | Method | Keeping |
|---|---|---|
| **Yogurt** | Milk heated to 82 to 85°C, cooled to 43°C, inoculated with culture, held at 40 to 45°C for 4 to 12 hours | 1 to 3 weeks refrigerated |
| **Greek yogurt / labneh** | Yogurt strained through cloth; labneh salted and strained further, rolled into balls and stored under oil | Labneh balls in oil: weeks to months refrigerated |
| **Kefir** | Milk fermented at room temperature with kefir grains 12 to 48 hours | 1 to 3 weeks refrigerated |
| **Cultured buttermilk** | Milk with buttermilk culture at room temperature | 2 to 3 weeks |
| **Sour cream, crème fraîche** | Cream with culture | 2 to 4 weeks |
| **Skyr, filmjölk, viili** | Nordic cultured milks | |
| **Kashk / kurut / qurut** | Strained yogurt or buttermilk salted, formed into balls and sun-dried | Years |
| **Tarhana** | Yogurt and grain or flour fermented, then sun-dried and crumbled into a soup base (Turkey, Greece, Balkans) | 1 year+ |

## 17.4 Milk

| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Pasteurization** | 63°C (145°F) for 30 minutes (vat) or 72°C (161°F) for 15 seconds (HTST) |
| **Ultra-pasteurization / UHT** | 135 to 150°C for 2 to 5 seconds; UHT in aseptic packaging is shelf stable for months |
| **Evaporated milk** | Milk with about 60% of water removed, canned and sterilized commercially |
| **Sweetened condensed milk** | Evaporated milk with about 40 to 45% sugar; sugar preserves it. Homemade versions are refrigerated |
| **Dulce de leche / cajeta** | Milk and sugar cooked down to a thick caramel |
| **Powdered milk** | Commercially spray-dried; nonfat powder keeps 20+ years in oxygen-free packaging; whole milk powder only 6 to 12 months due to fat |
| **Freeze-dried milk** | Home freeze dryer |
| **Khoa / mawa** | Indian milk solids made by simmering milk until thick; used in sweets |
| **Freezing** | Section 3.9 |

# 18. Dry Goods and Long-Term Pantry Storage

## 18.1 Enemies of Stored Food

**Heat, moisture, oxygen, light and pests.** Each 10°C (18°F) increase in storage temperature roughly halves shelf life.

## 18.2 Containers

| Container | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Mylar bags** | Metallized polyester; blocks oxygen, light and moisture; heat seal with an iron or impulse sealer; 5 to 7 mil thickness recommended |
| **Food-grade buckets (HDPE #2)** | Protect mylar bags from pests and puncture; gamma seal lids allow easy opening |
| **#10 cans** | Commercially sealed or via home can sealers at some community canneries |
| **Glass jars** | With oxygen absorbers or vacuum sealing (jar attachment) |
| **PETE bottles (#1)** | Clean, dry soda bottles work for small amounts of grains and beans with oxygen absorbers |
| **Vacuum-seal bags** | Medium term; slightly oxygen permeable over years |

## 18.3 Oxygen Absorbers

- Packets of iron powder that react with oxygen; reduce oxygen to below 0.1%.
- Kill insects and prevent oxidation.
- **Typical amounts:** 300 to 500 cc per 4-liter (1-gallon) bag; 2,000 to 2,500 cc per 19-liter (5-gallon) bucket or bag. Use more for powdery foods (less air) and less precision is needed than often thought; extra is harmless.
- Open the package and use quickly; store unused absorbers in a small sealed jar.
- **Use only with dry foods** (moisture below about 10%). Low-oxygen packaging of moist foods creates botulism risk.
- Do not use with sugar (it turns into a hard brick) or salt (unnecessary).

## 18.4 Other Treatments

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Dry ice (CO2)** | About 60 to 85 g (2 to 3 oz) per 19-liter bucket; place at the bottom under grain, set the lid loosely until it sublimates (30 to 60 minutes), then seal |
| **Freezing** | Kills insects and eggs: -18°C (0°F) for 3 to 7 days |
| **Diatomaceous earth (food grade)** | Mixed with grain (about 1 cup per 25 kg / 50 lb) to deter insects; washes off |
| **Bay leaves** | Traditional insect deterrent in flour bins (modest effect) |
| **Nitrogen flushing** | Commercial |
| **Desiccant packets** | Silica gel for moisture control in dry goods; not oxygen absorbers |

| **Bread crumbs, croutons** | Dried stale bread |
| **Portable soup** | Historic: meat stock boiled down to a hard gelatinous cake that kept for years on ships |

- **First in, first out:** Place new items at the back.
- **Store what is eaten** and eat what is stored.
- Label everything with contents and date.
- Inventory every 6 to 12 months.

| Method | How It Works | Examples |

| **Commercial canning (retort)** | Sealed cans or pouches sterilized in large pressure vessels | Canned goods, retort pouches (MREs, tuna pouches) |
| **Aseptic processing** | Food sterilized separately, filled into sterile packages in a sterile environment | Shelf-stable milk, juice boxes, broth cartons |
| **Pasteurization** | Mild heat kills pathogens | Milk, juice, eggs, beer |
| **High-pressure processing (HPP)** | Sealed food subjected to extremely high water pressure (about 6,000 bar); kills microbes without heat | Cold-pressed juice, guacamole, deli meats |
| **Irradiation** | Gamma rays, electron beams or X-rays kill microbes and insects | Spices, some meats, imported fruit; marked with the Radura symbol |
| **Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP)** | Air replaced with nitrogen, carbon dioxide or a blend | Salad bags, fresh meat, coffee, chips |
| **Vacuum packaging** | Air removed | Meats, cheese, coffee; must stay refrigerated unless the food is shelf stable |
| **Controlled atmosphere storage** | Low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, cold storage rooms | Apples sold year-round |
| **Sous vide pasteurization** | Vacuum-sealed food held at precise temperatures to pasteurize | Restaurant prep; refrigerated or frozen afterward |
| **Spray drying** | Liquid sprayed into hot air, drying instantly | Powdered milk, instant coffee, egg powder |
| **Drum drying** | Thin film dried on heated drums | Instant potato flakes, baby cereal |
| **Pulsed electric field** | Electric pulses damage microbial cells | Juices |
| **UV treatment** | Ultraviolet light on surfaces and clear liquids | Water, juice |
| **Edible coatings** | Wax or plant-based coatings slow moisture loss | Apples, citrus, cucumbers |
| **Ethylene control** | Ethylene absorbers and inhibitors (1-MCP) | Fruit storage |
| **Blast freezing / IQF** | Very fast freezing | Frozen vegetables, seafood |
| **Cryogenic freezing** | Liquid nitrogen | Seafood, berries |
| **Fermentation with selected cultures** | Controlled strains | Yogurt, salami, sauerkraut |
| **Bacteriophages and protective cultures** | Targeted microbes | Listeria control in deli meats |

## 22.5 Nuts and Seeds

- Nuts contain oils that go rancid. Store shelled nuts airtight in the refrigerator (6 to 12 months) or freezer (1 to 2 years).
- In-shell nuts keep longer than shelled.
- Dry fresh nuts (walnuts, chestnuts, hickory) to below 8% moisture before storage.
- **Chestnuts:** High moisture; refrigerate, freeze, dry or make into flour.
- **Acorns:** Leached of tannins with water, then dried for flour.
- Nut butters: refrigerate after opening for longer life.

# 23. Safety Reference

## 23.1 Key Temperatures

| Temperature | Significance |
|---|---|
| -18°C (0°F) | Freezer storage |
| 1 to 4°C (34 to 40°F) | Refrigerator |
| 4 to 60°C (40 to 140°F) | Danger zone |
| 63°C (145°F) | Whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, veal (with 3-minute rest); fish |
| 71°C (160°F) | Ground meats; jerky |
| 74°C (165°F) | Poultry; leftovers reheated |
| 100°C (212°F) | Boiling at sea level; water bath canning |
| 104.5°C (220°F) | Jam set point at sea level |
| 116°C (240°F) | Pressure canning at 10 psi |
| 121°C (250°F) | Pressure canning at 15 psi; kills botulism spores |

## 23.2 Pathogens to Know

| Organism | Typical Sources | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| *Clostridium botulinum* | Low-acid canned foods, oil infusions, vacuum packs, fermented fish, improperly cured meats | Pressure canning, acidification, nitrite, refrigeration, salt |
| *Listeria monocytogenes* | Deli meats, soft cheese, smoked fish, raw milk; grows in the fridge | Cooking, pasteurization, refrigeration timelines |
| *Salmonella* | Poultry, eggs, raw meat, produce | Cooking, hygiene |
| *E. coli* O157:H7 | Ground beef, raw produce, unpasteurized juice | Cooking ground beef to 71°C, washing produce |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | Handled foods left warm; toxin survives cooking | Hygiene, refrigeration, fast fermentation in sausage |
| *Clostridium perfringens* | Large batches of cooked meat cooling slowly | Rapid cooling |
| *Bacillus cereus* | Cooked rice left warm | Refrigerate rice promptly |
| *Vibrio* | Raw shellfish | Cooking |
| Parasites (*Trichinella*, *Anisakis*, tapeworms) | Wild game, pork (rare now), raw fish | Cooking or proper freezing |
| Mycotoxins | Moldy grains, nuts, fruit | Dry storage, discard moldy food |

## 23.3 General Rules

- Use tested recipes for canning and curing.
- Measure salt, cure and acid by weight where possible.
- Keep everything clean; sanitize equipment.
- Label and date everything.
- When a home-preserved food shows signs of spoilage, discard it without tasting.
