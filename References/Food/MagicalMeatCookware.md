## Best choice: silver-lined copper

Real historical cookware (French kitchens used tin- or silver-lined copper). Fits the setting's mana feel.

- **Heat:** Silver has the highest thermal conductivity of any metal (about 429 W/m·K). Copper is close behind (about 400). Heat spreads evenly with no hot spots. Mana-dense meat cooks through without scorched patches.
- **Food safety:** Silver barely reacts with food. Copper alone leaches into acidic dishes so the silver lining covers the cooking surface while the thick copper body moves the heat.
- **Mana:** For this proposal only, silver does not strip potency the way orichalcum does. The pan and meat form a closed loop where mana circulates and stays in the dish. Does not change `Energy.md`.
- **Drawback:** Silver tarnishes from sulfur in eggs, onions and some meats. Relining costs money and the pan is expensive overall. That makes it a noble, guild-chef or high-end tavern item.

## The grounding problem

A conductive pan only holds mana if the mana has nowhere to go. If it sits on a mana-stone stove or a conductive iron grate the meat's mana could bleed into the heat source. Fixes that fit the world:

- Cook over a plain wood or charcoal fire.
- Put a poor mana conductor between the pan and a mana-stone burner.
- Give the pan a wooden or bone handle so the cook's own body does not pull mana from it.

## Runesmith upgrade

A rune inscribed into the copper base could seal the loop or push mana back into the meat. That makes a potency-preserving pan a believable runesmith product with real market value.

## Alternatives by tier

| Material | Mana behavior | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Cast iron / carbon steel | Neutral: neither drains nor holds | Hunters, commoners, field cooking. Great sear. Seasoned with animal fat. |
| Silver-lined copper | Conducts and retains in a closed loop | Nobles, guild chefs, premium taverns |
| Solid silver | Same as above with better heat | Royal kitchens only. Too soft and costly for daily use. |
| Stoneware / clay | Neutral | Slow braises and stews. Mana loss is low because cooking is gentle. |

## Cooking tip (in-world)

Blood conducts mana. Basting or making a pan sauce with the beast's own blood keeps potency in the dish. Blood sausage or black pudding from edible monster meat would be prized for this reason. **Not goblins, ghouls, zombies, liches or other undead.** Those are never eaten.

## Smooth cast iron / top pans

> Vacuum pans / meat cookware culture. Vacuum weld craft: `../World/Science/Metallurgy/Vacuum.md`. Production methods: `KitchenCraft.md`.

### Smooth cast iron stages

Smoothness comes from five stages. The first four are about the casting and finish. The fifth is surface treatment.

1. **Fine mold sand.** Sand is sieved to a very small grain size and mixed with clay binder. Grain size sets the surface texture of the casting. Coarse sand gives the pebbly look of typical Western pans.
2. **Smooth pattern and mold face.** The pattern is polished. The mold face gets a thin wash of fine charcoal or clay slip and is dried or baked before pouring. This keeps molten iron from burning into the sand.
3. **Hot, fluid iron.** Iron is melted very hot with low impurities and poured fast. That lets thin walls fill cleanly with no cold shuts or rough spots.
4. **Machining after casting.** The cooking surface is turned on a lathe and then polished with abrasives. This step does most of the work. Western pans are often sold close to as-cast.
5. **Heat-formed oxide layer.** The pan is fired at high temperature to form a hard black iron oxide skin. Some makers (Nambu ironware, for example) then bake on urushi lacquer or oil. The result is rust-resistant and slick.

**Story notes:**
- Every step works without electricity.
- A water-powered lathe cuts the surface.
- Sandstone and charcoal powder polish it.
- A sieve set controls sand grade.
- Charcoal firing makes the oxide layer.
- A mage could replace the lathe step with a shaping or smoothing spell and keep the rest.

### Top five pans (vacuum welding)

### Vacuum welding in-world
1. Polish both faces to a mirror finish.
2. Scrub off the oxide layer so bare metal is exposed.
3. Remove the air with a mage's void spell or a piston pump with leather valves.
4. Press the faces together in a screw press.
5. Clean metals bond atom to atom with no solder or heat.
6. Lacquer goes on only after bonding because it cannot survive the process.

See also `Vacuum.md` (Void-Weld).

### 1. Hex-Lattice Clad Iron
- A smooth cast iron body with a corrosion-resistant hex mesh (mythril; not stainless) bonded to the cooking face.
- Sear happens on the ridges. Oil pools in the pockets.
- **Strength:** iron heat retention with a scratch-proof hard surface.
- **Weakness:** heavy. The pockets need careful cleaning.

### 2. Ceramic-Pocket Hex Pan
- A hex lattice (mythril) bonded to iron with glazed ceramic fired into each cell first.
- The ridges shield the ceramic from spoons and tongs.
- **Strength:** near-nonstick with metal durability.
- **Weakness:** ceramic can chip under hard impact. Fire the ceramic before bonding.

### 3. Urushi-Sealed Nambu Pan
- A fine-sand cast, lathe-turned, mirror-polished iron pan with a fired oxide skin and baked lacquer.
- **Strength:** the smoothest surface here. Rust-resistant and easy to maintain.
- **Weakness:** lacquer dislikes high dry heat and metal utensils.

### 4. Vacuum-Bonded Tri-Ply Pan
- Corrosion-resistant faces (mythril), a copper core, and matching face bonded with no solder layers.
- **Strength:** fast and even heat. It will not delaminate like soldered clad.
- **Weakness:** copper is expensive and the bonding step is demanding.

### 5. Rune-Core Iron Pan
- A cast iron pan with an enchanted rune disc vacuum-bonded into the base.
- The rune holds an even temperature or boosts heat spread.
- **Strength:** it fixes hot spots and cuts fuel use.
- **Weakness:** a cracked disc kills the enchantment. It costs mana and skill to repair.
- **Disc metal:** copper is the cheap mid conductivity host. Mythril or star steel suits a luxury version (cooler under flow). Plain iron is the worst common host. Prefer steel or better.

### Best overall for the character
- Pan 1 or 2 as a signature piece.
- Pan 5 as a luxury export.
- Pan 3 as the traditional benchmark the others get compared against.

## Uniform heat (whole animal)

**Proposal / rough idea. Not law.** Instant uniform heating of a carcass (magic), not ordinary fire.

Mostly juicy, with a few major catches depending on the target temperature.

### Why it would be juicier than normal cooking

Ordinary cooking moves heat from the outside in. The exterior overshoots while the center catches up, which leaves that band of gray, squeezed-out meat near the surface. That band is where most juice loss happens. Raising every part of the animal to the same temperature at the same moment gives edge-to-edge doneness, like sous vide with no waiting.

### Juiciness depends on the temperature chosen

- **50 to 55°C (122 to 131°F):** Myosin sets and the meat firms up while holding nearly all its water. Rare to medium rare throughout. Extremely juicy.
- **60 to 65°C (140 to 149°F):** Moderate shrinkage. Still juicy.
- **Above roughly 68°C (155°F):** Actin denatures and the muscle fibers clamp down hard, wringing out moisture. Instant well-done gives uniformly dry meat with no pink center to offset it.
- **Above 100°C (212°F):** Internal water flashes to steam all at once. Steam pressure tears the tissue from inside, the skin splits and the carcass could rupture or burst like popcorn. Dry, shredded and messy.

### Problems with doing a whole animal in one shot

- **No crust.** Browning needs a dry surface at around 140 to 165°C. Uniform moderate heat produces a pale, boiled-looking exterior with none of the roasted flavor.
- **Tough cuts stay tough.** Collagen converts to gelatin over hours at 70°C or higher. Instant heat cannot replace that time, so shoulder, shank and brisket stay chewy even when perfectly cooked. Loin and tenderloin come out excellent.
- **Different parts want different temperatures.** Chicken breast is best around 60 to 65°C while thighs need 70 to 80°C to loosen up. A single temperature is a compromise.
- **Fat stays unrendered.** Fat caps and skin need time and high surface heat to render and crisp. Instant uniform heat leaves them rubbery.
- **Food safety needs time at lower temperatures.** Poultry at 74°C kills salmonella almost instantly. At 60°C the same kill takes several minutes of holding. Whole-muscle beef interiors are nearly sterile, so this matters mostly for poultry and pork.
- **Guts cook too.** An ungutted animal cooks its intestinal contents right alongside the meat. The flavor spreads into everything nearby.

### The ideal magic method

Gut the animal. Heat it uniformly to about 55 to 60°C and hold it there for a few minutes (longer for poultry). Then hit only the outer few millimeters with a brief pulse to 200°C or more for crust and crisp skin. For tender cuts the result would beat any real-world technique. Tough cuts would still need magic that speeds up time or a slow braise.
