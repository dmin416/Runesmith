# 1. Salt

## 1.1 What Salt Is

"Salt" in everyday use means **sodium chloride (NaCl)**. Chemically, a salt is any compound formed from a positive ion and a negative ion, so many other "salts" exist and some of them appear alongside table salt in nature.

| Salt | Formula | Common Name / Use |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium chloride | NaCl | Table salt, rock salt, halite |
| Potassium chloride | KCl | Salt substitute, fertilizer (sylvite) |
| Magnesium chloride | MgCl2 | Nigari (tofu coagulant), de-icer |
| Magnesium sulfate | MgSO4·7H2O | Epsom salt |
| Calcium sulfate | CaSO4·2H2O | Gypsum, tofu coagulant, plaster |
| Calcium carbonate | CaCO3 | Limestone, chalk |
| Sodium carbonate | Na2CO3 | Washing soda, soda ash, natron, barilla |
| Sodium bicarbonate | NaHCO3 | Baking soda |
| Potassium carbonate | K2CO3 | Potash, pearl ash |
| Sodium borate | Na2B4O7·10H2O | Borax |
| Potassium aluminum sulfate | KAl(SO4)2·12H2O | Alum (pickling, styptic) |

## 1.2 Sources of Salt

| Source | Salt Content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Seawater | About 3.5% dissolved salts (35 g/L); roughly 27 g/L is NaCl | Unlimited supply; contains gypsum and magnesium salts that must be separated |
| Brine springs and wells | 5 to 26% | Historically the main inland source; many towns named "Salina" or ending in "-wich" were brine-boiling centers |
| Salt lakes | Great Salt Lake 5 to 27%; Dead Sea about 34% | Dead Sea brine is high in magnesium and calcium chlorides, making it bitter |
| Rock salt (halite) | 90 to 99% NaCl | Buried deposits left by ancient evaporated seas |
| Salt flats and playas | Crust of nearly pure salt | Salar de Uyuni (Bolivia), Danakil Depression (Ethiopia), Lake Assal (Djibouti) |
| Salt-accumulating plants (halophytes) | Variable | Glasswort / samphire (*Salicornia*), saltwort (*Salsola*), sea purslane |
| Plant ash | Mostly potassium salts | Inland cultures burned specific plants and leached the ash |
| Seaweed | High mineral content | Used to enrich sea salt (Japanese moshio) |
| Desalination brine | Roughly double seawater salinity | Byproduct of producing fresh water |

## 1.3 How Seawater Salts Separate

As seawater evaporates, its salts crystallize in a predictable order. This is the key to making good-tasting salt.

| Stage | Concentration (vs. seawater) | Brine Density (°Baumé) | What Crystallizes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | About 2× | About 7°Bé | Calcium carbonate (small amount) |
| 2 | About 3.5 to 4× | About 14 to 25°Bé | Gypsum (calcium sulfate) |
| 3 | About 10 to 11× | 25.7°Bé (saturation) | Sodium chloride begins |
| 4 | About 30× and beyond | Above 29 to 30°Bé | Magnesium sulfate, magnesium chloride and potassium salts (the **bitterns**) |

**Practical rule:** Harvest sodium chloride between about 25.7 and 29°Bé and drain the remaining liquid. Letting the brine dry completely traps bitterns in the salt, making it bitter, damp and clumpy.

Saturated brine at 20°C holds about 26.4% NaCl by weight, or roughly 357 g of salt per liter of water.

## 1.4 Solar Evaporation (Salt Pans)

The oldest large-scale method and still the source of most sea salt.

**Process:**

1. Seawater flows into a series of shallow **concentrating ponds**. Sun and wind evaporate water while carbonate and gypsum settle out.
2. Brine at or near saturation moves into **crystallizer ponds** lined with clay or compacted earth.
3. Salt crystallizes over days to weeks.
4. Salt is raked into piles, washed in saturated brine to remove clay and bitterns, then drained and dried.
5. The leftover bittern is drained away or processed for magnesium and potassium salts.

**Requirements:** Hot, dry, windy climate with low rainfall during the season. Clay soils that do not leak.

**Specialty salts from salt pans:**

- **Fleur de sel:** Delicate crystals that form as a thin crust on the brine surface on calm, hot days; skimmed by hand. Higher moisture and trace minerals; traditionally from Guérande and Camargue in France.
- **Sel gris (grey salt):** Coarse crystals raked from the clay bottom; grey from clay particles; damp and mineral-rich.

## 1.5 Boiling (Fire Evaporation)

Used where the climate is too cold or wet for solar pans.

**Open pan boiling:**

- Brine is boiled in wide, shallow pans (historically lead, iron or clay; now stainless steel).
- Gypsum forms a hard scale on the pan bottom ("pan scale") and is chipped off between batches.
- Gentle heat just below boiling produces **flake salt**: hollow pyramid crystals that form at the surface and sink when they grow heavy.
- Hard boiling produces fine cubic crystals.

**Vacuum pan evaporation (industrial):**

- Brine boils at reduced pressure in sealed vessels, often in multiple-effect chains where steam from one vessel heats the next.
- Produces very pure, uniform fine salt; the standard method for table salt.

**Historic brine enrichment:**

- **Graduation towers:** Brine trickles over walls of stacked blackthorn twigs; wind evaporates water and the concentrated brine is collected below. Saves firewood.
- **Salted sand (sleeching):** Tidal sand is spread to dry in the sun, collected and leached with seawater to make a strong brine for boiling.

## 1.6 Home Sea Salt Procedure

**Equipment:** Large stainless steel pot, wide shallow stainless pan, fine cloth or coffee filters, slotted spoon or skimmer, baking sheet.

**Collecting water:**

- Collect from clean, open coastline away from harbors, marinas, river mouths, storm drains and sewage outfalls.
- Avoid collecting after heavy rain, which washes runoff into coastal water.
- Check local regulations; some areas regulate seawater collection.

**Steps:**

1. Filter seawater through cloth or coffee filters to remove sand, algae and debris.
2. Boil in a large pot until reduced to about one tenth of the starting volume. Gypsum will form a white haze and settle.
3. Let the concentrate cool and settle for a few hours, then pour off the clear brine, leaving the gypsum sludge behind. Filter again.
4. Pour the clear brine into a wide shallow pan and heat gently (steaming, not boiling hard).
5. Crystals form on the surface and sink. Scoop them out periodically with a slotted spoon.
6. **Stop before the pan goes dry.** When about 10 to 20% of the liquid remains and it feels slightly oily or tastes bitter, discard it; this is the bittern.
7. Spread the salt on a baking sheet and dry at about 90 to 120°C (200 to 250°F) or in the sun.

**Yield:** About 25 to 30 g of salt per liter of seawater. 10 liters gives roughly 250 to 300 g.

**Saving the bittern:** The bitter leftover liquid is **nigari**, the traditional Japanese tofu coagulant (mostly magnesium chloride). It can be dried into crystals and stored.

## 1.7 Rock Salt Mining

**Conventional mining (room and pillar):**

- Shafts reach underground halite beds; salt is drilled, blasted and removed, leaving pillars of salt to hold up the roof.
- Rock salt is crushed and screened by size. Most goes to road de-icing and industry.
- Famous mines: Khewra (Pakistan, source of "Himalayan" pink salt colored by iron oxide), Wieliczka (Poland, historic), Goderich (Canada, one of the largest).

**Solution mining:**

1. Wells are drilled into a salt bed.
2. Fresh water is pumped down to dissolve the salt.
3. Saturated brine is pumped back up.
4. The brine is purified (calcium and magnesium removed chemically) and evaporated in vacuum pans.

Most table salt in North America is made from solution-mined brine.

## 1.8 Purifying Rock Salt or Crude Salt at Home

Crude rock salt, salt flat crust or dirty salt can be refined by recrystallization.

1. Dissolve the salt in warm water until no more dissolves (saturated brine).
2. Let it stand so clay and grit settle.
3. Filter through cloth, then through coffee filters.
4. Evaporate in a shallow pan with gentle heat.
5. Collect crystals before the pan goes dry, leaving impurities in the last bit of liquid.
6. Repeat for higher purity.

**Note:** Road salt and water-softener salt often contain additives (anti-caking agents, colorants, rust inhibitors) and are not food grade.

## 1.9 Salt From Plants

Inland and mountain peoples without access to sea or brine developed ways to extract salt from plants.

| Method | Source | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Ash salt | Burned salt-accumulating plants (swamp grasses, palm parts, sedges, coltsfoot); ash is leached with water, filtered and boiled down | Mostly potassium chloride with some sodium chloride; used in parts of Papua New Guinea, Central Africa and historically by Indigenous North Americans |
| Asin tibuok (Philippines) | Coconut husks soaked in seawater for months, burned; the ash is soaked in seawater, filtered and boiled in clay pots | Hard "salt eggs" |
| Moshio (Japan) | Seaweed (hondawara) soaked in seawater and dried repeatedly, then rinsed with seawater or burned; the enriched brine is boiled | Mineral-rich, tan sea salt |
| Bamboo salt (Korea, jugyeom) | Sea salt packed into bamboo tubes sealed with yellow clay, roasted in a pine fire; repeated up to 9 times | Grey to purple roasted salt with a sulfurous note |
| Green salt | Glasswort / samphire (*Salicornia*) dried and ground | Salty powder used as a lower-sodium seasoning |
| Barilla | Burned saltwort and glasswort | Sodium carbonate (soda ash) for soap and glass; not table salt |

**Ash salt procedure:**

1. Burn the plant material completely to white or grey ash.
2. Place ash in a container with a cloth-lined hole or a cloth funnel.
3. Pour water slowly through the ash and collect the liquid (leachate).
4. Pass the leachate through the ash a second time to concentrate it.
5. Filter and boil down to crystals.

Ash salt is high in potassium and tastes more bitter and metallic than sea salt.

## 1.10 Other Useful Salts and Their Extraction

| Salt | Source | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Potash** (potassium carbonate) | Hardwood ash | Leach ash with water as in Section 1.9; boil the liquid dry. Historically used for soap, glass and baking (pearl ash). The leachate itself is **lye** water |
| **Soda ash** (sodium carbonate) | Trona deposits (Wyoming), natron lakes (Egypt, Kenya), seaweed or saltwort ash | Mining and recrystallization; ancient Egyptians harvested natron from dry lake beds for mummification |
| **Baking soda** | Made from soda ash and carbon dioxide | Industrial carbonation |
| **Epsom salt** | Seawater bitterns, mineral springs (originally Epsom, England), mined epsomite | Crystallize from bitterns after removing sodium chloride |
| **Nigari** (magnesium chloride) | Seawater bitterns | See Section 1.6 |
| **Gypsum** | Seawater evaporation stage 2, gypsum rock | Byproduct of salt making or mined |
| **Potassium chloride** | Sylvite deposits (Saskatchewan), Dead Sea brine | Mining and flotation; solar evaporation |
| **Borax** | Dry lake deposits (California, Turkey) | Mining and recrystallization |
| **Alum** | Alunite rock, alum shale | Historically roasted rock was leached and crystallized |
| **Lithium salts** | Salt flat brines (Chile, Argentina, Bolivia) | Solar evaporation over 12 to 18 months |

## 1.11 Finishing and Additives

- **Iodization:** Potassium iodate or iodide is added at roughly 20 to 40 mg of iodine per kg of salt to prevent iodine deficiency.
- **Anti-caking agents:** Calcium silicate, sodium ferrocyanide or silicon dioxide keep salt free-flowing.
- **Kosher salt:** Large flaky or coarse crystals originally designed for drawing blood from meat.
- **Smoked salt:** Salt smoked over wood for hours or days.
- **Infused salts:** Salt mixed with herbs, citrus zest or wine and dried.

## 1.12 Salt Quality Considerations

- Coastal and lake water can carry heavy metals, agricultural runoff and microplastics; open-ocean sites with good circulation produce cleaner salt.
- Bitterns left in salt cause bitterness and moisture absorption.
- Unrefined salts naturally contain trace minerals but in amounts too small to matter nutritionally.
- Store salt in airtight containers; it absorbs moisture readily.

# 2. Sugar

## 2.1 What Sugar Is

Sugars are carbohydrates made by living things. Rocks and minerals contain essentially no sugar, with one curiosity: trace amounts of ribose and other sugars have been detected in some meteorites.

| Sugar | Type | Main Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Sucrose | Disaccharide (glucose + fructose) | Sugarcane, sugar beet, maple sap, palm sap, sweet sorghum |
| Glucose (dextrose) | Monosaccharide | Fruits, honey, starch breakdown |
| Fructose | Monosaccharide | Fruits, honey, agave, inulin breakdown |
| Maltose | Disaccharide (glucose + glucose) | Malted grains |
| Lactose | Disaccharide (glucose + galactose) | Milk |
| Xylose / xylitol | Sugar / sugar alcohol | Birch wood, corn cobs (hemicellulose) |
| Inulin | Fructose polymer | Agave, chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, yacon |
| Starch | Glucose polymer | Corn, wheat, rice, potatoes, cassava |

|---|---|---|

| Sugar beet (*Beta vulgaris*) | 16 to 20% sucrose | White sugar; beet molasses |
| Sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*) | Sap 1.5 to 3% sucrose | Maple syrup, sugar, cream, candy |
| Other maples, box elder, black walnut, sycamore | Sap 0.5 to 2% | Syrup |

**Non-sugar sweeteners from plants:**

- **Stevia:** Steviol glycosides extracted from leaves with hot water, purified with resins and crystallized.
- **Monk fruit (luo han guo):** Mogrosides extracted from fruit with water.
- **Licorice root:** Glycyrrhizin extracted with water.

## 2.3 Sugarcane Processing

### Industrial Raw Sugar

1. **Harvest:** Cane is cut when sucrose peaks. Cane starts losing sugar within hours of cutting and must be milled within about 24 hours.
2. **Milling:** Cane is shredded and crushed through a series of roller mills. Hot water sprayed on the fiber between mills (imbibition) washes out more sugar. The leftover fiber, **bagasse**, is burned to power the mill.
3. **Clarification:** Juice is heated and mixed with lime (calcium hydroxide) to raise pH to about 7 and prevent sucrose breakdown. Impurities form a sludge that settles out (**mud**, used as fertilizer).
4. **Evaporation:** Clear juice is boiled in multiple-effect evaporators from about 15°Brix to 60 to 70°Brix syrup.
5. **Crystallization:** Syrup is boiled under vacuum (about 65 to 70°C) in vacuum pans. Tiny seed crystals are added and grow as sugar comes out of solution. The crystal-syrup mix is called **massecuite**.
6. **Centrifuging:** Spinning baskets separate crystals from the liquid **molasses**.
7. **Repeated boiling:** The molasses is boiled again two or three times to recover more sugar. Each pass gives darker sugar and darker molasses. The final molasses is **blackstrap**.

**Yield:** About 10 to 12 kg of raw sugar per 100 kg of cane.

### Refining to White Sugar

1. **Affination:** Raw sugar is mixed with syrup to loosen the molasses film on the crystals, then centrifuged.
2. **Melting:** Crystals are dissolved in hot water.
3. **Clarification:** Phosphatation (phosphoric acid and lime) or carbonatation (lime and carbon dioxide) removes fine impurities.
4. **Decolorization:** Liquor passes through activated carbon, ion exchange resins or **bone char** (charred animal bone). Bone char use is why some white cane sugar is not considered vegan.
5. **Recrystallization:** Boiled in vacuum pans, centrifuged and dried.

**Brown sugar** is either partially refined or white sugar with molasses added back.

### Non-Centrifugal Cane Sugars (Jaggery, Panela, Gur, Rapadura, Muscovado)

Whole juice is boiled down without separating molasses, keeping all minerals and flavor.

1. Crush cane and collect juice.
2. Heat juice and skim the scum that rises. Traditional clarifiers include lime, wood ash, okra or hibiscus mucilage, which bind impurities into the scum.
3. Boil in open pans, moving through progressively hotter pans in traditional setups.
4. When the syrup reaches about 118 to 120°C (245 to 248°F), remove from heat.
5. Stir vigorously as it cools to start crystallization.
6. Pour into molds for blocks or keep stirring for granulated sugar.

**Muscovado** is boiled a little further and stirred into soft, moist crystals.

### Historical Clayed Sugar

Before centrifuges, sugar was poured into cone-shaped clay molds with a hole at the bottom. Wet clay was spread over the top. Water slowly seeped from the clay through the sugar, washing molasses out the bottom and whitening the crystals. The result was the "sugarloaf."

### Home Sugarcane Method

1. Peel and cut cane into short pieces.
2. Extract juice with a roller press, a hand-cranked cane mill or a heavy-duty juicer. Without a press, chop finely and simmer in a small amount of water for an hour, then strain and squeeze.
3. Strain through cloth.
4. Simmer, skimming foam constantly.
5. For syrup (cane syrup), stop at about 104 to 106°C (219 to 223°F).
6. For jaggery, continue to 118 to 120°C, then stir as it cools and pour into molds.

## 2.4 Sugar Beet Processing

### Industrial

1. **Washing and slicing:** Beets are cut into thin V-shaped strips called **cossettes**.
2. **Diffusion:** Cossettes move through a long tank against a flow of hot water (about 70°C). Sugar diffuses out into the water, giving **raw juice**. The spent pulp is pressed and dried for animal feed.
3. **Carbonatation:** Lime is added, then carbon dioxide is bubbled through, forming calcium carbonate that traps impurities. The lime and carbon dioxide come from roasting limestone in a kiln at the factory.
4. **Filtration and sulfitation:** Removes precipitate; sulfur dioxide reduces color.
5. **Evaporation, crystallization and centrifuging:** Same as cane. Beet sugar is crystallized directly to white sugar in one factory.

**Yield:** About 14 to 16 kg of sugar per 100 kg of beets.

**Beet molasses** is too strongly flavored for people and goes to animal feed, yeast production and fermentation.

### Home Beet Method

1. Scrub sugar beets (not table beets, which have much less sugar) and slice or shred thinly.
2. Cover with hot water (about 70 to 80°C) and hold for 1 hour.
3. Strain, then press the shreds to extract more liquid. A second soak can follow.
4. Simmer the liquid, skimming the scum.
5. Reduce to syrup. Home beet syrup has an earthy flavor; producing white crystals at home is impractical because the impurities that carry the flavor require industrial purification.

## 2.5 Tree Sap Syrups

### Maple

**Tapping:**

- Season runs late winter to early spring when nights freeze and days thaw (below 0°C at night, above 0°C during the day). Freeze-thaw cycles create pressure that pushes sap out.
- Trees should be at least 25 to 30 cm (10 to 12 inches) in diameter; larger trees can take 2 to 3 taps.
- Drill a 5/16 or 7/16 inch hole about 4 to 5 cm (1.5 to 2 inches) deep at a slight upward angle, on the sunny side, into healthy wood.
- Insert a spile and hang a bucket or connect tubing.
- A tap yields roughly 40 to 80 liters (10 to 20 gallons) of sap per season.
- Season ends when buds swell; sap turns cloudy and "buddy" in flavor.

**Rule of 86:** Gallons of sap needed per gallon of syrup = 86 ÷ sap sugar percentage. At 2% sugar, 43 gallons of sap make 1 gallon of syrup. The commonly cited average is 40:1.

**Boiling:**

1. Keep sap cold and boil within a few days; it spoils like milk.
2. Filter sap through cloth.
3. Boil in a wide, shallow pan for maximum evaporation. Boiling indoors releases enormous amounts of steam.
4. Add fresh sap continuously to keep the depth around 4 to 5 cm (1.5 to 2 inches); never let the pan run dry.
5. Finish in a smaller pot for control.
6. Syrup is done at **66 to 67°Brix**, which occurs at about **4°C (7°F) above the boiling point of water** at the current location: about 104°C (219°F) at sea level. Measure water's boiling point the same day since it changes with altitude and air pressure.
7. Filter hot syrup through wool or synthetic felt to remove **sugar sand** (niter), a gritty mineral deposit mainly of calcium malate.
8. Bottle hot, at 82 to 85°C (180 to 185°F) or above, and seal.

**Density matters:** Syrup below 66°Brix can ferment. Syrup above 67°Brix crystallizes in the bottle.

**Reverse osmosis:** Commercial producers push sap through membranes that remove 75% or more of the water before boiling, cutting fuel use dramatically.

**Maple products (temperatures above water's boiling point):**

| Product | Degrees Above Boiling | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Syrup | 4°C / 7°F | As above |
| Maple taffy (on snow) | 12 to 13°C / 22 to 24°F | Pour on packed snow; roll up with a stick |
| Maple cream (butter) | 12 to 13°C / 22 to 24°F | Cool rapidly to about 10°C (50°F) without stirring, then stir until it turns opaque and spreadable |
| Soft maple candy | 18 to 19°C / 32 to 34°F | Cool slightly, stir until it thickens, pour into molds |
| Granulated maple sugar | 25 to 28°C / 45 to 50°F | Stir continuously off heat until it crumbles into granules |

Grade A colors (golden, amber, dark, very dark) reflect the season: early sap makes lighter, more delicate syrup.

### Birch

- Sap runs after maple season, usually for 2 to 3 weeks.
- Sap is only 0.5 to 1.5% sugar, so it takes **100 to 150 liters of sap per liter of syrup**.
- The sugars are mostly fructose and glucose, which scorch at lower temperatures than sucrose. Boil gently and finish at low heat; many producers finish below a full boil or use reverse osmosis.
- Flavor is tart, mineral and molasses-like rather than sweet like maple.
- Fresh birch sap is also drunk as a beverage in Northern and Eastern Europe.

### Palm Sap

- **Tapping:** Flower stalks are bruised, cut and tied; sap drips into a container hung below. A tree can yield several liters per day for weeks or months.
- **Rapid fermentation:** Palm sap begins fermenting into toddy within hours in tropical heat. Collectors coat the containers with lime (calcium hydroxide) or add bark from certain trees to slow fermentation.
- **Boiling:** Sap is boiled the same day in open pans, skimmed, then concentrated until it sets when dropped in water. It is poured into molds (coconut shells, bamboo tubes) or stirred into granules.

| Palm | Product |
|---|---|
| Coconut | Coconut sugar, gula melaka (Malaysia), gula jawa (Indonesia) |
| Palmyra (*Borassus*) | Palm jaggery (India, Sri Lanka, Cambodia) |
| Date palm | Khejur gur / nolen gur (Bengal), harvested in winter |
| Sugar palm (*Arenga pinnata*) | Gula aren (Indonesia) |
| Nipa palm | Sugar and vinegar (Philippines) |

## 2.6 Sweet Sorghum Syrup

1. Harvest when seeds are in the soft dough stage.
2. Strip leaves and remove seed heads.
3. Crush stalks in a roller mill (traditionally horse- or mule-powered).
4. Let juice settle and strain.
5. Boil in a long shallow pan with baffles, moving the juice from the cold end to the finishing end.
6. Skim the heavy green foam continuously; it carries chlorophyll, starch and proteins that cause off-flavors.
7. Finish at about 108 to 110°C (226 to 230°F).
8. Some producers add amylase enzyme to break down starch, which otherwise causes cloudiness and gelling.

**Ratio:** Roughly 8 to 12 liters of juice per liter of syrup.

## 2.8 Honey

1. **Harvest:** Remove frames when at least 80 to 90% of cells are capped with wax (capping means bees have dried the honey enough).
2. **Uncapping:** Slice off wax caps with a heated or serrated knife or scratch them open with an uncapping fork.
3. **Extraction:** Spin frames in a centrifugal extractor; honey flings out of the cells and drains to the bottom. Frames return to the hive intact.
4. **Crush and strain (no extractor):** Cut comb from frames, crush it and let honey drip through cloth or a fine strainer for 24 hours.
5. **Straining:** Pass through progressively finer mesh to remove wax and debris.
6. **Settling:** Let honey stand 1 to 2 days so air bubbles and fine wax rise; skim.
7. **Moisture:** Honey must be below about 18.6% moisture to resist fermentation; a refractometer measures this.

**Byproducts:** Beeswax from cappings (melted in water, strained, cooled into blocks) and honey-water from rinsing (used for mead).

**Crystallization:** All raw honey eventually crystallizes; high-glucose honeys (rapeseed, clover) set quickly. Gentle warming below 40°C (104°F) re-liquefies it.

## 2.9 Sugar From Starch

Starch is a long chain of glucose. Breaking it apart produces sugar syrups.

### Malting (Natural Enzymes)

Germinating grain produces its own starch-digesting enzymes (amylases).

1. **Steep:** Soak barley (or wheat, rice, sorghum, millet) in water for 1 to 2 days, changing water periodically.
2. **Germinate:** Spread damp grain in a cool place, turning daily, for 4 to 6 days until the rootlets are 1 to 1.5 times the grain length.
3. **Dry (kiln):** Dry at low heat (below about 50°C) to keep enzymes alive.
4. **Grind** the dried malt.

**Mashing:**

1. Mix crushed malt (and optionally cooked starchy food such as rice) with water.
2. Hold at 62 to 68°C (144 to 154°F) for 1 to 8 hours. Enzymes convert starch to maltose and glucose.
3. Strain and squeeze.
4. Boil the sweet liquid (wort) down to syrup.

**Traditional examples:**

- **Malt syrup / barley malt extract:** Malted barley mash reduced to syrup.
- **Jocheong / yeot (Korea):** Cooked rice mixed with malted barley powder, held at about 55 to 60°C for 6 to 8 hours, strained and boiled to syrup (jocheong) or further into taffy (yeot).
- **Mizuame (Japan):** Rice or potato starch converted with malt; a clear glucose-maltose syrup.

### Industrial Enzymatic Conversion

1. **Wet milling:** Corn is steeped and separated into starch, germ (for corn oil), fiber and protein.
2. **Liquefaction:** Starch slurry is heated to about 90 to 105°C with heat-stable alpha-amylase, breaking it into shorter chains.
3. **Saccharification:** Glucoamylase at about 55 to 60°C and pH 4.5 converts chains to glucose.
4. **Refining:** Filtering, carbon treatment and ion exchange.
5. **Evaporation** to glucose syrup, or crystallization to dextrose.
6. **Isomerization:** Glucose isomerase converts part of the glucose to fructose, producing high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS-42; enriched by chromatography to HFCS-55 and HFCS-90).

**Acid hydrolysis:** The older method, boiling starch with dilute acid, works but produces more off-flavors and color.

### Inulin to Fructose

Agave, chicory, Jerusalem artichoke and yacon store inulin (a fructose chain).

- **Agave syrup:** Agave cores (piñas) are cooked in ovens or treated with enzymes to break inulin into fructose; the juice is filtered and concentrated.
- **Yacon syrup:** Roots are juiced and the juice is reduced; much of the sweetness is fructooligosaccharides that the body digests poorly.

## 2.10 Sugar From Milk and Wood

**Lactose:**

1. Whey (left from cheesemaking) contains about 4.5 to 5% lactose.
2. Proteins are removed by heating or ultrafiltration.
3. The liquid is concentrated by evaporation to about 60% solids.
4. Lactose crystallizes as it cools slowly over many hours.
5. Crystals are centrifuged, washed and dried.

**Xylitol:**

1. Hardwood (especially birch) or corn cobs are treated with acid to break hemicellulose into xylose.
2. Xylose is purified and hydrogenated over a catalyst into xylitol.
3. Industrial process only.

**Cellulosic sugar:** Wood, straw and crop residue can be broken into glucose with acid or enzymes. Produced industrially for fuel ethanol and chemicals.

## 2.12 Sugar Storage

- **Syrups:** Must be at the correct density (maple 66 to 67°Brix) to avoid fermentation; refrigerate after opening.
- **Jaggery and palm sugar:** Absorb moisture; store airtight.
- **Crystal sugar:** Keeps indefinitely when dry.

## 3.3 Pre-Treatment

Oil comes out more easily and in larger amounts when the material is prepared properly.

- **Cleaning:** Remove stones, dirt, stems and metal.
- **Dehulling:** Removing hulls (sunflower, cotton, hemp) increases oil yield per press run; leaving some hull helps the press grip.
- **Drying:** Seeds pressed too wet give emulsified, cloudy oil; seeds too dry give poor yield. Most seeds press best at 6 to 10% moisture.
- **Cracking or flaking:** Breaks cells open.
- **Conditioning (cooking):** Heating seeds to about 80 to 110°C with steam breaks down cell walls and lets oil flow, raising yield. Skipped for cold-pressed oil.
- **Roasting:** For flavor (toasted sesame, pumpkin, peanut, walnut oils).

## 3.4 Traditional Water Methods (Flotation)

The oldest methods. Oil is lighter than water and does not mix with it, so crushed oil-rich material is boiled or kneaded in water and the oil is skimmed off.

**General procedure:**

1. Pound or grind the material into a paste.
2. Add hot water and boil, or knead with water.
3. Oil rises to the surface.
4. Skim the oil.
5. Heat the skimmed oil gently to drive off remaining water.

**Examples:**

| Product | Method |
|---|---|
| **Shea butter** | Kernels are boiled, sun-dried, roasted and ground into a paste. The paste is kneaded by hand with water for hours until fat separates as a cream, then boiled to clarify and strained |
| **Coconut oil (traditional)** | Grated fresh coconut is squeezed with water to make coconut milk; the milk is boiled until water evaporates and the oil separates from browned solids |
| **Red palm oil (traditional)** | Fruit is boiled, pounded, mixed with water and the oil skimmed |
| **Hickory and walnut oil (Indigenous North American)** | Nuts were pounded shells and all, boiled; oil and "nut milk" were skimmed while shells sank |
| **Argan oil (traditional)** | Roasted kernels are ground on a stone mill, kneaded with warm water and squeezed |
| **Bear's / animal fats** | Rendered by boiling (see Section 3.9) |

## 3.5 Olive Oil and Fruit Oils

Olive and avocado oils come from fruit flesh, which is mostly water.

### Process

1. **Harvest:** Olives are picked green to purple. Early harvest gives less oil but more flavor and polyphenols. Process within 24 hours to prevent fermentation and defects.
2. **Wash and remove leaves.**
3. **Crush:** Stone mills (traditional) or hammer mills crush whole olives, pits included, into a paste.
4. **Malaxation:** The paste is slowly stirred for 20 to 45 minutes so small oil droplets merge into larger ones. "Cold extracted" means below 27°C (80.6°F).
5. **Separation:**
   - **Traditional press:** Paste is spread on round fiber mats, stacked and pressed hydraulically. Oil and vegetable water run out together and separate by settling.
   - **Decanter centrifuge (modern):** Spins paste to separate oil, water and pomace continuously.
6. **Final separation:** A vertical centrifuge or settling removes the last water.
7. **Filtering or racking:** Oil is filtered or left to settle and decanted off the sediment.

**Yield:** About 5 to 8 kg of olives per liter of oil.

**Grades:**

- **Extra virgin:** Mechanical extraction only, free acidity under 0.8%, no taste defects.
- **Virgin:** Mechanical extraction, free acidity under 2%, minor defects.
- **Refined / "pure" / "light":** Chemically refined, often blended with some virgin oil.
- **Pomace oil:** Solvent extracted from leftover paste, then refined.

### Home Olive Oil

1. Crush pitted or whole olives into paste with a heavy mortar, a food processor or a meat grinder.
2. Stir the paste slowly by hand or mixer at room temperature for 30 to 45 minutes.
3. Spoon paste into cloth bags and press, or add a little warm water, stir and let sit.
4. Collect the liquid and let it settle in a tall container for several hours.
5. Skim or decant the oil from the top; a turkey baster or separatory funnel helps.
6. Let oil rest 1 to 2 weeks and decant off the sediment.

Home yields are lower than commercial yields.

### Avocado Oil

- Ripe, very soft avocados give the best yield.
- Flesh is mashed, malaxed at 45 to 50°C for about an hour, then separated by centrifuge or by pressing and settling.
- Traditional home method: Mash flesh, dry it (sun, dehydrator or low oven) until dark and leathery, then press or squeeze in cloth.

## 3.6 Mechanical Pressing

### Press Types

| Press | How It Works | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Lever or wedge press | Weight or driven wedges squeeze material in cloth or baskets | Historic, small |
| Hydraulic press | Hydraulic ram squeezes material in a cage | Cocoa butter, olive, artisan oils |
| Screw press (expeller) | A rotating screw drives seeds through a tapering barrel; oil drains through slots and compressed press cake exits the end | Home to industrial |
| Ram press | A hand-operated piston forces seed through a cylinder | Small-scale rural |

### Cold Pressing vs. Expeller Pressing

- **Cold pressed:** Press temperature kept below roughly 40 to 50°C (104 to 122°F). Retains more flavor, color and nutrients; lower yield.
- **Expeller pressed:** Friction in a screw press commonly raises temperature to 60 to 100°C. Higher yield; still solvent-free.

### Yields and Residual Oil

- Screw presses leave about 6 to 14% oil in the press cake.
- Pressing twice increases yield slightly.
- Press cake is high-protein animal feed or flour (sunflower, hemp, pumpkin) or fertilizer.

### Home Screw Press Procedure

1. Use a hand-cranked or small electric screw press.
2. Preheat the press head (many come with a small burner or heating band) to about 50 to 70°C so oil flows.
3. Feed clean, dry seeds steadily.
4. Adjust the press-cake outlet; tighter gives more oil but risks jamming.
5. Collect the oil in a jar.
6. Let oil settle 1 to 2 weeks in a cool, dark place; sediment ("foots") drops to the bottom.
7. Decant or filter through coffee filters or cloth.

|---|---|
| Sunflower (black oil type) | 250 to 350 mL |
| Rapeseed | 250 to 350 mL |
| Pumpkin | 200 to 300 mL |
| Hemp | 200 to 250 mL |
| Flax | 250 to 300 mL |
| Sesame | 300 to 400 mL |

## 3.7 Coconut Oil Methods

| Method | Product | Process |
|---|---|---|
| **Copra pressing** | Crude coconut oil | Coconut meat is dried in sun or kilns to copra, then pressed; crude oil is refined (RBD coconut oil) |
| **Hot process virgin** | Virgin coconut oil (toasty flavor) | Grated coconut squeezed with water into milk; milk boiled until oil separates; solids strained out |
| **Fermentation** | Virgin coconut oil (mild) | Coconut milk left 24 to 48 hours at warm room temperature; it separates into cream, oil and water; oil is skimmed and gently heated to dry |
| **Cold separation** | Virgin coconut oil | Coconut milk chilled until cream sets; cream is separated and centrifuged or gently warmed |
| **Direct micro expelling** | Virgin coconut oil | Fresh grated meat dried quickly to about 10% moisture and pressed the same day |

**Home fermentation method:**

1. Grate the meat of mature brown coconuts.
2. Blend with warm water (about 1:1) and squeeze through cloth for milk. Repeat once with fresh water.
3. Pour milk into a glass jar and leave covered at 25 to 35°C for 24 to 48 hours.
4. Three layers form: curd on top, oil in the middle, water at the bottom (layer order varies; the clear layer is oil).
5. Refrigerate briefly so the oil solidifies, then lift or scoop it out.
6. Warm gently and strain to remove remaining water and curd.

**Yield:** Roughly 8 to 12 mature coconuts per liter of virgin oil.

## 3.8 Solvent Extraction (Industrial)

Used for soybeans and for squeezing the last oil from press cake.

1. Seeds are cracked, conditioned and rolled into thin flakes (or pressed first).
2. Flakes pass through a percolation extractor washed with hexane.
3. The oil-solvent mix (**miscella**) is distilled to recover hexane for reuse.
4. The flakes are steamed to drive off remaining hexane (desolventizing) and become meal for feed.
5. Residual oil in meal drops below 1%.

Hexane is extremely flammable and the process requires sealed, explosion-proof equipment; it is not practical at home. Ethanol can extract oil at small scale but also pulls water-soluble compounds, color and chlorophyll.

**Other methods:**

- **Supercritical CO2:** For high-value oils (seed oils for supplements, cocoa butter, wheat germ); no solvent residue.
- **Aqueous enzymatic extraction:** Enzymes break down cell walls in water, releasing oil without solvents; used for some specialty oils.

## 3.9 Animal Fats and Oils

### Rendering

Separating fat from animal tissue by heating.

**Dry rendering:**

1. Trim meat and blood from fat; chill and cut into small cubes or grind.
2. Heat slowly in a heavy pot, oven (about 100 to 120°C / 225 to 250°F) or slow cooker.
3. Stir occasionally as fat melts out over 2 to 6 hours.
4. Stop when the remaining bits (**cracklings**) are golden and sink.
5. Strain through cheesecloth or a fine strainer into jars.

**Wet rendering:**

1. Add about 1 to 2 cm of water to the pot with the fat.
2. Simmer gently; water keeps temperature near 100°C, giving whiter, milder fat.
3. Strain, then chill so fat solidifies on top of the water.
4. Lift off the fat disc, scrape the underside and reheat gently to drive off remaining water.

**Purifying tallow:** For odorless, snow-white tallow (for soap or candles), re-melt with an equal volume of salted water, boil briefly, chill and lift off the fat. Repeat as needed.

| Fat | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf lard | Pork fat around kidneys | Mildest, whitest lard; prized for pastry |
| Back fat lard | Pork back fat | General cooking |
| Tallow | Beef or mutton fat; suet is the hard fat around kidneys | Hard fat; frying, soap, candles |
| Schmaltz | Chicken or goose fat | Often rendered with onions |
| Duck and goose fat | Skin and fat | Confit, roasting |
| Bacon grease | Cooked bacon | Strain while warm |
| Bone marrow fat | Bones roasted or boiled | Historically valued in cold climates |
| Bear grease | Bear fat | Historically used for cooking, lamp oil and waterproofing |

### Butter, Ghee and Milk Fat

- **Butter:** Cream (35 to 40% fat) is churned until fat globules clump into butter grains; buttermilk drains off; butter is washed and kneaded.
- **Clarified butter:** Butter melted gently; milk solids sink and foam rises; pure fat is poured off.
- **Ghee:** Butter simmered until water evaporates and milk solids turn golden brown, adding a nutty flavor; strained. Shelf-stable for months.

### Fish and Marine Oils

1. Fish (often small oily fish like anchovy and menhaden) or fish waste is cooked.
2. Cooked fish is pressed into press cake (fishmeal) and press liquor.
3. Press liquor is centrifuged to separate oil from water.
4. Oil is refined, deodorized and often molecularly distilled to remove contaminants.

**Cod liver oil:** Traditionally made by letting livers rot in barrels so oil separated and floated (dark, strong oil), later by steaming fresh livers.

**Historic whale and seal oil:** Blubber was "tried out" (rendered) in pots on ships or on shore.

### Lanolin (Wool Wax)

1. Raw wool is washed (scoured) in hot water with detergent.
2. The wash water is centrifuged to separate the wool grease.
3. The crude grease is refined into lanolin.

Raw wool contains 5 to 25% wool grease by weight.

### Insect Fats

- Black soldier fly larvae contain about 30 to 40% fat (dry weight); larvae are dried, pressed or rendered.
- Mealworm fat is extracted the same way. Both are growing industries for animal feed.

## 3.10 Refining Plant Oils

Crude oil is refined for neutral flavor, light color and high smoke point. The result is **RBD** (refined, bleached, deodorized) oil.

| Step | Purpose | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Degumming | Removes phospholipids (gums) | Water or dilute acid is added; gums hydrate and are centrifuged out. Soybean gums become **lecithin** |
| Neutralization | Removes free fatty acids | Sodium hydroxide converts free fatty acids into soap (**soapstock**), which is centrifuged out |
| Bleaching | Removes pigments, trace metals and soap | Oil is mixed with activated **bleaching earth** (bentonite or montmorillonite clay) under vacuum and filtered |
| Deodorizing | Removes odors and remaining free fatty acids | Steam is blown through oil at 220 to 260°C under high vacuum |
| Winterization | Removes waxes that cloud oil in the fridge | Oil is chilled slowly; wax crystals are filtered out (sunflower, rice bran, corn) |

**Unrefined (virgin) oils** skip these steps and keep their natural flavor, color and nutrients, with lower smoke points and shorter shelf lives.

## 3.11 Oil Storage and Rancidity

- **Oxidation:** Oxygen attacks unsaturated fatty acids, producing stale, paint-like or fishy flavors.
- **Speed of rancidity:** Flax, hemp, walnut and fish oils (highly polyunsaturated) spoil fastest; coconut, palm, cocoa butter and tallow (saturated) last longest.
- **Store** in dark glass, filled to the top, in a cool place. Refrigerate polyunsaturated oils.
- **Natural antioxidants:** Vitamin E (tocopherols) or rosemary extract slow oxidation.
- **Shelf life:** Flax oil 6 to 8 weeks once opened; olive oil 12 to 18 months; coconut oil 2+ years.
- **Sediment and water:** Water left in homemade oil causes spoilage; settle and decant thoroughly.

## 3.12 Oil From Rocks and the Earth (Petroleum)

### What Petroleum Is

Crude oil and natural gas formed over millions of years from buried marine plankton and algae. Heat and pressure converted their remains first into **kerogen**, then into liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons that migrated into porous rock (sandstone, limestone) and were trapped under impermeable layers.

### Sources

| Source | Description | Extraction |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional crude oil | Liquid oil in porous rock reservoirs | Drilled wells; natural pressure, pumps, water flooding |
| Tight oil / shale oil | Oil trapped in low-permeability shale | Horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing |
| Oil sands (tar sands) | Sand mixed with thick bitumen (Alberta, Venezuela) | Surface mining with hot water separation, or steam injection underground |
| Oil shale | Rock containing solid kerogen, not yet oil | Heated (retorted) to about 450 to 550°C to break kerogen into oil vapor |
| Natural seeps | Oil reaching the surface naturally | Historically collected by hand; La Brea Tar Pits (Los Angeles), Pitch Lake (Trinidad) |
| Coal | Solid fossil carbon | Heating coal without air (coking) produces coal tar; historically "coal oil" (kerosene) |

### Oil Recovery Stages

- **Primary:** Natural reservoir pressure and pumps; recovers about 5 to 15% of the oil in place.
- **Secondary:** Injecting water or gas to push oil toward wells; total recovery reaches 30 to 50%.
- **Tertiary (enhanced):** Steam, carbon dioxide or chemicals reduce oil thickness; recovers more.

### Oil Sands Hot Water Extraction

1. Mined oil sand is mixed with hot water and agitated.
2. Bitumen attaches to air bubbles and floats as a froth.
3. Sand sinks and clay stays suspended.
4. Froth is diluted with a solvent (naphtha or paraffinic solvent) to remove water and fine solids.
5. Bitumen is upgraded (cracked) into synthetic crude.

### Refining Crude Oil

1. **Desalting:** Crude is washed with water to remove salt, which corrodes equipment.
2. **Atmospheric distillation:** Crude is heated to about 350 to 400°C and fed into a tall column. Components separate by boiling point.
3. **Vacuum distillation:** Heavy residue is distilled under vacuum to separate lubricants and waxes without cracking them.
4. **Conversion:** Cracking, reforming and other processes convert heavy fractions into gasoline and diesel.
5. **Treating:** Removes sulfur and other contaminants.

**Distillation fractions:**

| Fraction | Approximate Boiling Range | Products |
|---|---|---|
| Refinery gas | Below 30°C | Methane, ethane, propane, butane |
| Light naphtha | 30 to 90°C | Gasoline blending, solvents |
| Heavy naphtha | 90 to 200°C | Gasoline (after reforming), petrochemicals |
| Kerosene | 150 to 275°C | Jet fuel, lamp oil, heating |
| Diesel / gas oil | 200 to 350°C | Diesel, heating oil |
| Heavy gas oil | 350 to 450°C | Lubricating oils, cracker feed |
| Residue | Above 450°C | Paraffin wax, petroleum jelly, fuel oil, asphalt / bitumen |

**Mineral oil (white oil):** Highly refined lubricating fraction with all aromatic compounds removed; colorless, odorless and used in cosmetics, food equipment and laxatives.

**Petroleum jelly:** Semi-solid mixture of mineral oil and wax from the residue.

**Paraffin wax:** Solid wax separated by chilling and solvent dewaxing of lubricating fractions.

Petroleum extraction and refining require industrial equipment and are not done at home.

# 4. Glycerin

## 4.1 What Glycerin Is

**Glycerin** (glycerol, glycerine) is a simple sugar alcohol. Every fat and oil molecule (triglyceride) is built on a glycerol backbone carrying three fatty acids. Breaking fats apart releases glycerin, so it is a natural byproduct of soap making, fat splitting and biodiesel production.

**Properties:**

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Formula | C3H8O3 |
| Appearance | Clear, colorless, odorless, thick syrupy liquid |
| Taste | Sweet; about 60% as sweet as sucrose |
| Calories | About 4.3 kcal per gram |
| Density | 1.26 g/mL (heavier than water) |
| Boiling point | 290°C (554°F); decomposes near boiling |
| Melting point | 17.8°C (64°F), but it usually stays liquid well below that (supercooling) |
| Solubility | Mixes completely with water and alcohol; does not mix with oils |
| Hygroscopic | Pulls moisture from the air; the basis of its use as a humectant |
| Antifreeze | A 2:1 glycerin-to-water mix freezes at about -46°C (-51°F) |
| Preservative | Solutions above about 50% glycerin resist microbial growth |

Theoretical glycerin yield from fat is about **10% of the fat's weight** (9 to 11% depending on the fat).

## 4.2 Forms and Grades of Glycerin

### By Source

| Form | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Vegetable glycerin (VG)** | Palm, coconut, soybean and other plant oils | Most common consumer glycerin; required for vegan and most kosher products |
| **Animal glycerin** | Tallow and lard | Chemically identical to vegetable glycerin; less common now |
| **Biodiesel glycerin** | Byproduct of converting oils and fats to biodiesel | Huge supply since the 2000s; sold crude or refined |
| **Synthetic glycerin** | Made from propylene (petroleum) | Pharmaceutical-grade purity; less common since biodiesel glycerin flooded the market |
| **Fermentation glycerin** | Produced by yeast and some algae | Wine naturally contains 5 to 15 g/L, contributing body and smoothness; industrial fermentation exists but is minor |

### By Grade

| Grade | Purity | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Crude glycerin** | 40 to 88% | Contains water, salt, methanol, soap and fatty acids; brown and not for direct use; sold for refining, animal feed (purified grades), biogas and boiler fuel |
| **Technical grade** | About 95 to 98% | Industrial uses: antifreeze, resins, cleaning products; not for food or skin |
| **USP / pharmaceutical grade** | 99.0 to 99.7% | Meets United States Pharmacopeia standards; medicines, cosmetics, food |
| **Food grade (E422)** | 99%+ | Food additive approved as a humectant, sweetener and solvent |
| **Kosher / halal certified** | 99%+ | Vegetable source with certified handling |
| **Anhydrous** | 99.5%+ with very little water | Laboratory and pharmaceutical use |
| **96% glycerin** | 96% glycerin, 4% water | Common retail grade; handles more easily |

### Glycerin Products and Derivatives

| Product | What It Is | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Glycerin soap (transparent soap)** | Soap made with added glycerin, sugar and alcohol so it sets clear | Melt-and-pour soap bases |
| **Glycerites** | Herbal extracts made with glycerin instead of alcohol | Alcohol-free herbal remedies and flavorings |
| **Glycerin suppositories** | Solid glycerin with gelatin or soap | Laxative |
| **Mono- and diglycerides (E471)** | Glycerin bonded to one or two fatty acids | Emulsifiers in bread, ice cream, margarine |
| **Glyceryl stearate** | Glycerin bonded to stearic acid | Emulsifier and thickener in lotions |
| **Triacetin (E1518)** | Glycerin bonded to three acetic acid groups | Food additive, flavor carrier, cigarette filter plasticizer |
| **Glycerol ester of wood rosin (E445)** | Glycerin bonded to pine rosin acids | Keeps citrus oils suspended in soft drinks; chewing gum base |
| **PGPR (E476)** | Polyglycerol polyricinoleate, made from glycerin and castor oil fatty acids | Reduces chocolate thickness |
| **Polyglycerol esters (E475)** | Chains of glycerin bonded to fatty acids | Food emulsifiers |
| **Epichlorohydrin** | Industrial chemical made from glycerin or propylene | Epoxy resins |

## 4.3 How Glycerin Is Produced

### Saponification (Soap Making)

Fat + lye → soap + glycerin

- In home cold process soap, the glycerin stays in the bar, which is why handmade soap feels more moisturizing than commercial bars.
- In industrial boiled soap, the soap is **salted out**: salt is added to the boiling batch, the soap curdles and floats, and the glycerin dissolves into the salty water underneath (**spent lye**).
- Spent lye contains about 4 to 8% glycerin. It is treated to remove soap and impurities, evaporated to concentrate the glycerin, the salt crystallizes out and the crude glycerin is distilled.

### Fat Splitting (Hydrolysis)

Fat + water → fatty acids + glycerin

1. Fats and water are pumped through a tall column at about 250°C and 50 bar pressure.
2. Fats split into fatty acids (rising to the top) and **sweetwater** (glycerin dissolved in water, collecting at the bottom).
3. Sweetwater contains 10 to 20% glycerin and is evaporated and distilled.
4. The fatty acids go to soap, candles (stearic acid), cosmetics and industry.

This is the cleanest route to high-purity natural glycerin.

### Biodiesel Transesterification

Fat + methanol + catalyst → biodiesel (fatty acid methyl esters) + glycerin

1. Oil or fat reacts with methanol and a catalyst (usually sodium or potassium hydroxide).
2. Heavy crude glycerin settles to the bottom and is drained.
3. About 1 kg of crude glycerin forms for every 10 kg of biodiesel.
4. Crude biodiesel glycerin contains methanol, catalyst, soap and salts; it is refined by acidifying (splitting soaps into free fatty acids that float off), neutralizing, stripping methanol, then vacuum distilling and treating with activated carbon or ion exchange resins.

### Synthetic Production

Propylene from petroleum is converted through several steps (via allyl chloride and epichlorohydrin) into glycerin. Now rare because natural glycerin is cheap and abundant.

### Glycerin Refining Steps (Summary)

| Step | Removes |
|---|---|
| Acidification and phase separation | Soaps (as free fatty acids) |
| Neutralization | Excess acid or alkali |
| Evaporation | Water, methanol |
| Salt separation | Crystallized sodium or potassium salts |
| Vacuum distillation | Most remaining impurities; glycerin distills at about 160 to 200°C under vacuum to avoid decomposition |
| Activated carbon | Color and odor |
| Ion exchange | Remaining salts and trace ions |

### Glycerin at Home

- **Recovering glycerin from homemade soap or biodiesel** is possible in principle but the result is impure, salty or contaminated with methanol and lye. Purchased USP vegetable glycerin is inexpensive and far better for food, skin and remedies.
- **Keeping glycerin in soap:** Cold process soap naturally retains all of its glycerin.
- **Crude biodiesel glycerin** is commonly composted in small amounts, used in biogas digesters or burned in specialized boilers. Unrefined, it is not safe for food or skin because of residual methanol and lye.

## 4.4 Uses of Glycerin

### Food and Kitchen

| Use | How |
|---|---|
| Sweetener | Sweet with fewer blood sugar effects than sugar; common in low-sugar and keto foods |
| Humectant | Keeps baked goods, fondant and icing soft; roughly 1 teaspoon per cup of royal icing keeps it from setting rock hard |
| Alcohol-free extracts | Replaces alcohol in vanilla and other extracts (see glycerites below) |
| Solvent and carrier | Dissolves food colors and flavors |
| Frozen desserts | Lowers freezing point for softer texture |
| Preserving fruit | Candied and dried fruits stay soft |

### Glycerites (Herbal Extracts)

1. Use dried herb or fresh herb. Typical ratios: dried herb 1 part by weight to 5 parts liquid by volume; fresh herb 1:2.
2. Liquid is usually 60 to 80% glycerin with the rest distilled water (fresh herbs contribute their own water, so use more glycerin).
3. Cover the herb completely in a jar.
4. Steep 2 to 6 weeks, shaking daily, or warm gently at about 40 to 50°C for a few days.
5. Strain and press.
6. Store in dark bottles. Shelf life is roughly 1 to 2 years.

Glycerites are milder and less efficient at extracting resins and some alkaloids than alcohol, but they taste sweet and contain no alcohol.

**Alcohol-free vanilla:** Split beans in a mix of about 3 parts glycerin to 1 part water; steep 2 to 6 months.

### Skin and Personal Care

- Moisturizers and lotions: typically 2 to 10% glycerin.
- Soap (Section 5.1), shampoos, toothpaste (keeps it from drying out), lip balm and hand cream.
- Simple skin softener: 1 part glycerin to 4 to 5 parts rosewater or distilled water. Pure glycerin is sticky and works best diluted.

### Household and Craft

| Use | Recipe / Method |
|---|---|
| **Giant bubbles** | 6 parts water, 1 part dish soap, 0.25 to 0.5 part glycerin; let rest 24 hours |
| **Preserving leaves and flowers** | 1 part glycerin to 2 parts hot water; stand fresh stems in it or submerge leaves for 2 to 6 weeks until supple |
| **Fog / smoke machine fluid** | Glycerin and distilled water (or glycol) mixtures |
| **Snow globes** | Glycerin added to water slows falling glitter |
| **Leather conditioning** | Glycerin saddle soap keeps leather supple |
| **Ink and paint** | Keeps watercolors and inks from drying too fast |
| **Antifreeze** | Glycerin-water mixtures for cooling systems and sample preservation |
| **Laboratory** | Preserving biological specimens, cryoprotectant for freezing cells |
| **Stain removal** | Softens old stains (grass, ink, coffee) before washing |

# 5. Byproducts and Helpful Things

Salt, sugar, oil and glycerin production leave behind useful materials, and the four combine into many practical products.

## 5.1 Soap Making

Soap is fat or oil reacted with a strong alkali (lye).

| Alkali | Soap Type |
|---|---|
| Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) | Hard bar soap |
| Potassium hydroxide (KOH) | Soft and liquid soap |
| Wood ash lye (mostly potassium carbonate and hydroxide) | Traditional soft soap |

### Saponification Values

Grams of lye needed to fully saponify 1 g of oil (values vary slightly by source):

| Oil / Fat | NaOH per g | KOH per g | Soap Qualities |
|---|---|---|---|
| Olive | 0.135 | 0.190 | Mild, gentle; slow to harden (castile) |
| Coconut | 0.183 | 0.257 | Hard, very bubbly, cleansing; drying above about 30% of the recipe |
| Palm | 0.142 | 0.199 | Hard, long-lasting |
| Tallow (beef) | 0.143 | 0.200 | Hard, creamy, long-lasting |
| Lard | 0.139 | 0.195 | Hard, mild, creamy |
| Shea butter | 0.128 | 0.180 | Conditioning, hard |
| Cocoa butter | 0.137 | 0.192 | Hard, conditioning |
| Castor | 0.128 | 0.180 | Boosts lather; used at 5 to 10% |
| Sunflower | 0.136 | 0.191 | Soft, conditioning; prone to rancidity |
| Sweet almond | 0.136 | 0.191 | Conditioning |
| Avocado | 0.134 | 0.188 | Conditioning |
| Hemp | 0.137 | 0.192 | Conditioning; prone to rancidity |
| Beeswax | 0.069 | 0.097 | Hardens bars; used at 1 to 3% |

### Cold Process Basics

1. **Calculate:** Multiply each oil's weight by its NaOH value and add them up. Reduce the total by 5 to 8% (**superfat**) so some oil remains unsaponified for mildness and a safety margin. Online lye calculators do this automatically.
2. **Lye solution:** Use about 2 to 2.5 parts water to 1 part lye by weight. Always add lye to water, never water to lye. The solution heats to around 90°C and releases fumes; mix in a ventilated area wearing gloves and eye protection, in stainless steel or heat-resistant plastic (never aluminum).
3. **Combine:** When lye solution and melted oils are both around 35 to 50°C, pour lye into oils.
4. **Blend** with a stick blender until **trace**: the mix thickens like pudding and a drizzle leaves a visible trail.
5. **Add** fragrance, essential oils, herbs, clays or exfoliants.
6. **Pour** into a lined mold, insulate and leave 24 to 48 hours.
7. **Cut** into bars.
8. **Cure** 4 to 6 weeks on a rack so water evaporates and the bar becomes mild and hard.

**Hot process:** The traced soap is cooked (slow cooker or double boiler) until saponification finishes, then molded. Usable sooner, rustic texture.

**Melt and pour:** Pre-made glycerin soap base is melted, colored, scented and poured. No lye handling.

### Lye From Wood Ash

1. Use hardwood ash only, burned completely to white or grey; softwood ash is weaker.
2. Pack ash in a barrel or bucket with holes in the bottom over a layer of straw and gravel.
3. Pour rainwater or soft water slowly through and collect the brown liquid. Run it through again to strengthen it.
4. Traditional strength test: a fresh egg or small potato floats with a coin-sized area showing. A hydrometer reading around 1.15 to 1.20 or a pH test gives more precision.
5. Boil down to concentrate if needed.
6. Ash lye makes soft soap. Adding salt to the finished soap converts some of it toward a harder sodium soap.

### Salting Out

Adding salt to a boiled soap batch separates soap curds (floating) from glycerin-rich lye water (sinking). The curd is collected, re-boiled and molded. This is how traditional hard soaps and most commercial soap were made, and how industrial glycerin is recovered (Section 4.3).

## 5.3 Fermentation: Alcohol and Vinegar

Sugar from any source in Section 2 can be fermented.

**Alcoholic fermentation:**

- Yeast converts sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
- About 17 g of sugar per liter produces roughly 1% alcohol by volume.
- **Wine** (grapes and other fruit), **mead** (honey), **cider** (apples, pears), **beer** (malted grain), **palm wine / toddy** (palm sap), **pulque** (agave sap) and **kvass** (bread or beets).
- Home brewing of beer and wine for personal use is legal in the US. Distilling spirits requires a federal permit regardless of quantity.

**Vinegar:**

1. Ferment sugar to alcohol (or start with wine, cider or beer at 5 to 8% alcohol).
2. Expose to air with a vinegar **mother** (a cellulose mat of *Acetobacter* bacteria) or a splash of raw unpasteurized vinegar.
3. Cover with breathable cloth and keep at 20 to 30°C for 3 to 8 weeks.
4. Acetobacter converts alcohol to acetic acid; finished vinegar is typically 4 to 7% acidity.
5. Strain, bottle and optionally pasteurize at about 70°C to stop activity.

Vinegar made from palm sap, coconut water, cane juice, honey, maple sap, rice and fruit scraps all follow the same process.

**Key reference values:**

- Seawater: about 3.5% salt, 1.025 g/mL, about 3.5°Bé
- Saturated salt brine: 26.4% NaCl, about 25.7°Bé, 1.20 g/mL
- Maple syrup: 66 to 67°Brix
- Honey: under 18.6% moisture (about 80+°Brix)
- Water boiling point: 100°C (212°F) at sea level, dropping about 1°C per 300 m (1°F per 500 ft) of elevation
