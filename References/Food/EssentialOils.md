## 1. Peppermint Oil

### 1.1 The Plant

- **Species:** *Mentha × piperita* (peppermint)
- **Origin:** A natural sterile hybrid of watermint (*Mentha aquatica*) and spearmint (*Mentha spicata*). It rarely sets viable seed and is propagated from runners (stolons).
- **Oil location:** The oil is made and stored in glandular trichomes, tiny bulb-shaped hairs on the leaf surface. Rubbing a leaf ruptures them, which is why crushed mint smells stronger.
- **Oil-bearing parts:** Leaves and flowering tops. Stems contain very little oil.
- **Major producing regions:** The US Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon and Idaho) and the Midwest are leading producers of peppermint oil. India dominates production of cornmint (*Mentha arvensis*), the main source of natural menthol.

### 1.2 Chemical Composition

Typical ranges for commercial peppermint oil (values vary with growing region, harvest timing and distillation):

| Component | Typical Range | Character / Role |
|---|---|---|
| Menthol | 30 to 55% | Cooling, minty; the primary active compound |
| Menthone | 14 to 32% | Minty, slightly harsh; converts to menthol as the plant matures |
| Isomenthone | 1.5 to 10% | Similar to menthone |
| 1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol) | 3.5 to 14% | Fresh, camphor-like, eucalyptus note |
| Menthyl acetate | 2.8 to 10% | Sweet, fruity, smooths the aroma |
| Menthofuran | 1 to 9% | Musty off-note in excess; limited in quality oils |
| Limonene | 1 to 5% | Light citrus note |
| Pulegone | Up to 4% | Minty-herbal; limited due to liver toxicity at high doses |
| Carvone | Up to 1% | Spearmint note |

Higher menthol and menthyl acetate with lower menthone, menthofuran and pulegone generally indicates a better-quality oil.

### 1.3 How Menthol Works

- **Cooling effect:** Menthol activates TRPM8, the nerve receptor that normally fires in response to cold. No actual temperature change occurs.
- **Pain relief:** TRPM8 activation produces a counter-irritant effect that dulls the perception of muscle and joint pain.
- **Antispasmodic effect:** Menthol blocks calcium channels in smooth muscle, relaxing the gut wall. This is the basis for enteric-coated peppermint oil capsules used for irritable bowel syndrome.
- **Nasal sensation:** Menthol makes airflow feel easier by triggering cold receptors in the nose, even though it does not measurably widen the airway.

### 1.4 Common Uses

- Flavoring: candy, gum, baked goods, liqueurs
- Oral care: toothpaste, mouthwash, breath products
- Topical products: muscle rubs, cooling gels, lip balms
- Digestive health: enteric-coated capsules
- Aromatherapy and diffusers
- Household: scenting cleaners; used as a repellent for ants and rodents with mixed results

## 2. Essential Oils in General

### 2.1 Definition

An essential oil is a concentrated mixture of the volatile aromatic compounds produced by a plant. The word "essential" comes from "essence" (the plant's characteristic scent) and has nothing to do with nutritional necessity.

### 2.2 Chemistry

Essential oils are made mainly of:

- **Monoterpenes (C10):** limonene, pinene, myrcene
- **Oxygenated monoterpenes:** menthol, menthone, linalool, geraniol, 1,8-cineole
- **Sesquiterpenes (C15):** caryophyllene, bisabolene
- **Phenylpropanoids:** eugenol (clove), cinnamaldehyde (cinnamon), anethole (anise)
- **Esters, aldehydes and ketones** derived from the above

### 2.3 Physical Properties

- **Volatile:** They evaporate at room temperature and leave no greasy spot on paper.
- **Hydrophobic:** They do not dissolve in water but dissolve readily in alcohol, fats and oils.
- **Not true fats:** Unlike olive or coconut oil (fixed oils, which are triglycerides), essential oils are small aromatic molecules.
- **Lighter than water in most cases:** Peppermint oil has a density of about 0.90 to 0.91 g/mL and floats on water. A few oils (clove, cinnamon bark) sink.
- **Sensitive to light, heat and oxygen:** Oxidation changes the aroma and can create skin-sensitizing compounds.

### 2.4 Related Products

| Product | What It Is |
|---|---|
| Essential oil | Volatile oil separated by distillation or cold pressing |
| Hydrosol | The aromatic water left after distillation; contains trace dissolved oil |
| CO2 extract | Oil extracted with pressurized carbon dioxide; fuller aroma profile |
| Concrete | Waxy solvent extract containing oils, waxes and pigments |
| Absolute | Alcohol-washed concrete with waxes removed; used for delicate flowers |
| Tincture | Plant compounds dissolved in alcohol |
| Infused oil | Plant material steeped in a fixed oil such as olive or jojoba |
| Fixed oil (carrier oil) | Non-volatile fatty oil used to dilute essential oils |

## 3. Extraction Methods

### 3.1 Steam Distillation (Standard for Peppermint)

**Process:**

1. Plant material is packed into a chamber above (not in) boiling water.
2. Steam passes up through the material, rupturing the trichomes and carrying the volatile oil out as vapor.
3. The mixed steam and oil vapor travels to a condenser where it cools back to liquid.
4. The liquid drips into a separator. The oil floats on top of the water and is drawn off.
5. The remaining water is the hydrosol.

**Why it works:** Water and oil boil together at a temperature just under 100°C even though the oil's individual components boil at 170 to 230°C. This allows oil recovery without exposing it to damaging heat.

**Parameters:**

- Temperature: about 100°C at atmospheric pressure
- Run time: most oil comes over in the first 30 to 45 minutes; full runs typically last 60 to 90 minutes
- Yield: roughly 0.3 to 1% of plant weight (higher with partially dried material)
- Commercial yields: commonly 60 to 100 pounds of oil per acre

**Commercial practice:** Mint growers cut the crop, let it wilt in the field, then load it into large portable tubs that are hauled to an on-farm still where steam is pushed through the entire tub.

**Plants commonly steam distilled.** Steam distillation produces the large majority of the world's essential oils. Yields below are approximate and vary with region, season, chemotype and whether the material is fresh or dried.

### 3.2 Hydrodistillation

- Plant material is submerged directly in boiling water and the vapor is condensed.
- Simplest equipment: a pot, a lid connected to a condenser and a collection vessel.
- Laboratory standard for measuring oil content uses a Clevenger apparatus, which returns the condensed water to the boiling flask.
- Drawback: direct contact with boiling water can cause more hydrolysis of esters (such as menthyl acetate) and scorching if the pot runs low.

**Plants better suited to hydrodistillation:**

| Plant | Part Used | Yield | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rose otto (*Rosa damascena*) | Petals | 0.02 to 0.05% | About 3,000 to 5,000 kg of petals per kg of oil; petals clump into an impenetrable mass under steam so they are boiled in water; phenylethyl alcohol (a key rose note) dissolves into the water, so the water is redistilled (cohobation); the oil turns solid when cool because of waxy stearoptenes |
| Agarwood / oud (*Aquilaria* spp.) | Resin-infused heartwood | 0.05 to 0.5% | Wood is ground and soaked in water for days to weeks; distillation runs 3 to 10 days (Sections 3.13 and 3.16) |
| Cinnamon, nutmeg and other powders | Ground material | See Section 3.1 | Powders clump and block steam flow |
| Neroli (traditional) | Blossoms | 0.05 to 0.1% | Some producers boil blossoms in water to maximize orange flower water output |
| Frankincense, myrrh | Resin | See Section 3.1 | Water keeps melted resin from scorching on the bottom of the still |
| Any plant (testing) | Any | Measured | The Clevenger apparatus is the laboratory standard for measuring a sample's oil content |

### 3.3 Hydro-Steam (Water and Steam) Distillation

- Plant material sits on a grate just above boiling water in the same vessel.
- A compromise between the two methods above and common in small craft stills.
- Works well for lavender, rosemary, mints, thyme, conifer needles, citrus peel and most leafy herbs.
- Not suited to powders, which fall through the grate, or to rose petals, which mat together.

### 3.4 Supercritical CO2 Extraction

- Carbon dioxide is pressurized above 73.8 bar and heated above 31°C, where it acts as a solvent with properties between a gas and a liquid.
- Typical operating range: 80 to 300 bar, 35 to 60°C.
- After extraction, pressure is released and the CO2 turns back to gas, leaving no solvent residue.
- **Subcritical CO2** (lower pressure and temperature) captures the lightest aromatics and gives a cleaner "select" extract.
- **Supercritical CO2** at higher pressure also pulls heavier compounds and waxes, giving a "total" extract.
- Advantages: low heat, no water hydrolysis, aroma closer to the fresh plant.
- Drawback: requires expensive high-pressure equipment.
- Ethanol is sometimes added as a cosolvent to pull more polar compounds.

**Plants where CO2 extraction stands out:**

| Plant | Part Used | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Ginger | Rhizome | Captures gingerols, giving the pungency missing from distilled oil |
| Black pepper | Berries | Total extract includes piperine and real heat |
| Turmeric | Rhizome | Rich in turmerones; curcumin needs an ethanol cosolvent |
| Vanilla | Cured pods | Closest commercial extract to the aroma of the bean itself |
| Coffee, cocoa | Roasted beans, nibs | Aroma extracts for flavoring; CO2 is also the standard decaffeination method |
| Cardamom, clove, cinnamon, nutmeg, pink pepper | Spices | Richer, truer spice aroma than distilled oils |
| Hops | Cones | The standard brewing industry extract; captures bitter acids along with aroma |
| German chamomile | Flowers | Extract is greenish-yellow since no chamazulene forms without heat |
| Frankincense, myrrh | Resin | Total extracts include non-volatile boswellic acids and resin |
| Sandalwood, vetiver, patchouli, agarwood | Wood, roots, leaves | Deeper aroma and higher yield in less time than distillation |
| Rosemary | Leaves | Antioxidant extract rich in carnosic acid, used as a natural food preservative |
| Calendula, arnica, sea buckthorn, rosehip | Flowers, berries, seeds | Valued for non-volatile compounds; little or no essential oil |
| Cannabis / hemp | Flowers | Pulls cannabinoids along with terpenes; widely used commercially |
| Jasmine, rose, tuberose | Flowers | CO2 floral extracts compete with absolutes and carry no hexane residue |

### 3.5 Solvent Extraction

- Hexane or another solvent dissolves aromatic compounds out of the plant, then is evaporated off to leave a concrete.
- The concrete is washed in ethanol, chilled to drop out waxes, filtered and the ethanol evaporated to leave an absolute.
- Used mainly for delicate flowers (jasmine, tuberose) that do not survive distillation. Rarely used for mint.
- Common solvents: hexane and petroleum ether (historically benzene). Absolutes may carry trace residual solvent and are used mainly in perfumery.
- Resins are extracted with ethanol instead, producing a **resinoid**.

| Rose absolute (*R. centifolia*, *R. damascena*) | Petals | 0.1 to 0.2% | Higher yield than rose otto and richer in phenylethyl alcohol; smells closer to the living flower |
| Orange flower absolute (*Citrus aurantium*) | Blossoms | 0.1 to 0.2% | Heavier, more honeyed than neroli |

### 3.6 Ethanol Extraction (Tincture or Extract)

- Fresh or dried mint is steeped in high-proof alcohol (at least 40%, ideally 70 to 95%) for days to weeks, then strained.
- Captures menthol and other oils plus water-soluble compounds and chlorophyll.
- Result is a flavoring extract or tincture, not a pure essential oil.
- Easiest and cheapest home method for culinary use.
- 95% grain alcohol pulls more oil and less water-soluble material than 40% spirits.
- Ethanol also pulls chlorophyll, which turns leaf extracts green and can add grassy flavor; shorter steeps of 1 to 3 days limit this for leafy herbs.
- To concentrate the volatile oil from a tincture, dilute it with water and distill.

**Common ethanol extracts:**

| Plant | Part Used | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Vanilla | Cured pods | US "pure vanilla extract" requires at least 35% alcohol and 13.35 oz of beans per gallon; home ratio is roughly 28 g of split beans per 240 mL of spirit, aged 2 months or longer (6 to 12 months for full flavor) |
| Lemon, orange, lime | Zest only | White pith adds bitterness |
| Mint, lavender, rosemary, basil | Leaves, flowers | Short steeps keep the flavor bright |
| Cinnamon, clove, cardamom, star anise, coffee | Whole spices, beans | Lightly crush first |
| Ginger | Fresh rhizome | Captures the pungency missing from distilled oil |
| Almond | Bitter almond oil | Almond extract uses cyanide-free bitter almond oil or synthetic benzaldehyde, not steeped nuts |
| Gentian, wormwood, hops | Roots, leaves, cones | Bitter compounds are non-volatile and extract only in solvent; wormwood contains thujone |

### 3.7 Oil Infusion

- Mint is steeped in a fixed oil (olive, sunflower, jojoba), often with gentle warming, then strained.
- Produces a mild, mint-scented oil suitable for balms and massage oil.
- Concentration is far below that of a true essential oil.
- Fresh leaves introduce water that can cause spoilage; dried leaves are preferred.
- Typical method: fill a jar loosely with dried herb, cover with oil, then steep 4 to 6 weeks at room temperature or 2 to 4 hours at 40 to 60°C.

**Common infused oils:**

| Plant | Part Used | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Calendula | Dried flowers | Valued for non-volatile compounds; no meaningful essential oil |
| Arnica | Dried flowers | External use only |
| St John's wort | Fresh flowers | Oil turns deep red from hypericin; traditionally sun-infused |
| Comfrey | Leaves, roots | Contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids; external use only |
| Lavender, rosemary, mint, chamomile | Dried herb | Mildly scented massage and balm oils |
| Chili peppers | Dried pods | Capsaicin dissolves into oil; it is not an essential oil component |
| Garlic and fresh herbs (culinary) | Fresh material | Fresh garlic or herbs in oil at room temperature can grow *Clostridium botulinum*; culinary infusions are refrigerated and used within about a week, acidified or made with dried ingredients |

### 3.8 Cold Pressing (Expression)

Used almost exclusively for citrus peels, where the oil sits in large glands in the colored outer peel (the flavedo). No heat is involved, so the oil keeps the fresh aroma of the fruit. Not used for mint.

**Process:**

1. The peel is punctured, rasped or squeezed to rupture the oil glands.
2. Water sprays wash the released oil away as an emulsion.
3. A centrifuge separates the oil from the water.
4. The oil is chilled ("wintered") so waxes drop out, then filtered.

**Techniques:**

- **Sponge method (sfumatura):** Historic hand method; peel halves were pressed into natural sponges that were wrung out.
- **Écuelle à piquer:** A rotating bowl lined with spikes punctures whole fruit.
- **Machine rasping (pelatrice):** Rollers scrape the outer peel of whole fruit.
- **In-line juice extractors:** Recover peel oil and juice at the same time; most citrus oil is a byproduct of the juice industry.
- **Home method:** Zest with a microplane and squeeze the zest firmly through a cloth, or bend peel strips over a glass so the oil sprays out. Yields are tiny; home steam distillation of peel is more productive.

**Citrus oils:**

| Plant | Part Used | Yield | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sweet orange (*Citrus sinensis*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Over 90% limonene; the cheapest essential oil in the world; not phototoxic |
| Bitter orange (*C. aurantium*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Phototoxic when expressed |
| Lemon (*C. limon*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Phototoxic when expressed |
| Lime (*C. aurantifolia*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Expressed lime is strongly phototoxic; distilled lime is not (Section 3.1) |
| Grapefruit (*C. paradisi*) | Peel | 0.1 to 0.3% of fruit | Nootkatone gives the signature note; mildly phototoxic |
| Bergamot (*C. bergamia*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | High in bergapten, a strong phototoxin; "FCF" (furanocoumarin-free) grades have it removed; the flavor of Earl Grey tea |
| Mandarin / tangerine (*C. reticulata*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Methyl N-methylanthranilate gives a faint blue-violet fluorescence |
| Yuzu (*C. junos*) | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Mostly produced in Japan; expensive |
| Blood orange, clementine, kumquat, pomelo | Peel | 0.2 to 0.5% of fruit | Same process |

**Special considerations:**

- Cold-pressed citrus oils carry non-volatile waxes, pigments and furanocoumarins that distilled citrus oils lack.
- Limonene oxidizes quickly, so citrus oils have the shortest shelf life (roughly 9 to 18 months); refrigeration helps.
- Distilled citrus oils are not phototoxic but smell flatter and more "cooked."
- **Folded oils:** Citrus oil is vacuum distilled to remove part of the limonene, concentrating flavor 5, 10 or 20 times ("5-fold," "10-fold").
- **Terpeneless oils:** Nearly all terpenes are removed so the oil dissolves clearly in beverages and resists oxidation.

### 3.9 Enfleurage and Maceration

Fat absorbs scent molecules from flowers that cannot survive distillation.

**Cold enfleurage:**

1. Glass plates in wooden frames (chassis) are coated with purified, odorless fat (historically a blend of lard and tallow).
2. Fresh flowers are pressed onto the fat.
3. Spent flowers are replaced every 1 to 3 days for weeks or months until the fat is saturated, forming a **pomade**.
4. The pomade is washed in high-proof alcohol, chilled to drop out fat, filtered and the alcohol evaporated to leave an **absolute of enfleurage**.

Best for flowers that keep producing scent after picking: jasmine and tuberose.

**Hot enfleurage (maceration):** Flowers are stirred into warmed fat or oil for a few hours, strained and replaced with fresh flowers repeatedly. Used for flowers that stop producing scent once picked, such as rose, orange blossom, violet and cassie.

**Special considerations:**

- Commercially almost extinct because of labor cost; still made in small amounts in Grasse, France and by artisan perfumers.
- Practical at home with refined coconut oil, vegetable shortening or refined shea butter spread on glass.
- One of the few ways to capture scents of flowers that yield no distilled oil, including lilac, honeysuckle, gardenia, lily of the valley, plumeria, sweet pea and wisteria.
- All petal fragments are removed at each change to prevent mold.
- The finished pomade can be used directly as a solid perfume.

### 3.10 Emerging Methods

- **Microwave-assisted distillation:** Microwaves heat the water inside the plant cells, rupturing them faster. Shorter run times with similar oil quality.
- **Ultrasound-assisted extraction:** Sound waves break cell walls to speed solvent extraction.

### 3.11 Dry (Destructive) Distillation

- Wood or bark is heated without air until it breaks down, releasing tars and smoky phenolic compounds.
- **Birch tar** (*Betula* spp. bark): Smoky leather note; the classic "Russian leather" scent.
- **Cade oil** (*Juniperus oxycedrus* wood): Smoky, tarry; used in soaps and veterinary products.
- **Pine tar** (*Pinus* spp. wood): Used in soaps, rope and wood preservation.
- Crude tars contain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; perfumery and cosmetic grades are rectified by steam distillation to remove them.

### 3.12 Enzymatic Release Before Distillation

Some plants store their aroma compounds in bound, odorless forms. The material must be crushed and soaked in water so the plant's own enzymes release the volatile compound before distillation.

| Plant | Part Used | Yield | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wintergreen (*Gaultheria procumbens*) | Leaves | 0.5 to 0.8% | Leaves are soaked in warm water for several hours so gaultherin breaks down into methyl salicylate; oil is 96 to 99% methyl salicylate, heavier than water and highly toxic if swallowed; most commercial "wintergreen" is synthetic |
| Sweet birch (*Betula lenta*) | Bark, twigs | 0.2 to 0.6% | Same chemistry and process as wintergreen |
| Black mustard (*Brassica nigra*) | Seeds | 0.5 to 1% | Ground seeds are soaked so myrosinase converts sinigrin into allyl isothiocyanate; extremely irritating to eyes, skin and lungs; distinct from pressed mustard seed oil (a fixed oil) |
| Horseradish, wasabi | Roots | Low | Same chemistry as mustard |
| Bitter almond, apricot kernel, peach kernel | Kernels (press cake) | 0.5 to 1% | Fixed oil is pressed out first; the cake is soaked so amygdalin breaks down into benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide; food-grade oil must be FFPA (free from prussic acid) |
| Garlic (*Allium sativum*) | Cloves | 0.1 to 0.25% | Crushing converts alliin to allicin, which distillation turns into diallyl sulfides; oil is heavier than water |
| Onion (*Allium cepa*) | Bulbs | Under 0.02% | Extremely low yield; potent odor |

### 3.13 Fermentation, Aging and Curing

Some materials develop their aroma only after a pre-treatment that can last from days to years.

| Plant | Treatment | Special Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Patchouli | Leaves are dried and lightly fermented in bales | Breaks down cell walls; the oil continues to improve for years after distillation |
| Vanilla | Green pods are blanched, sweated, sun-dried and conditioned over several months | Fresh green pods have almost no scent; enzymes convert glucovanillin into vanillin during curing |
| Orris | Rhizomes are peeled, dried and aged about 3 years | Irones (the violet-like scent) form through slow oxidation; distillation yields a waxy solid called orris butter, one of the most expensive perfume materials |
| Agarwood (oud) | Heartwood is soaked or fermented in water for days to weeks | The wood is only fragrant after infection by a mold, which triggers resin production; plantations now inoculate trees deliberately |
| Vetiver | Roots are soaked before distillation | Improves oil release |
| Sandalwood | Trees are left to mature 15 to 30+ years | Oil accumulates in the heartwood with age |

### 3.14 Oils Heavier Than Water

Most essential oils float, but the following sink and need a heavy-oil (bottom-draw) Florentine separator where water exits from the top:

- Clove (bud, leaf, stem)
- Cinnamon (bark, leaf) and cassia
- Allspice and West Indian bay (borderline)
- Wintergreen and sweet birch
- Bitter almond
- Mustard
- Garlic
- Sassafras: safrole-rich; banned in food in the US and regulated because safrole is a controlled precursor chemical
- Vetiver (borderline; may float or sink depending on the batch)

### 3.15 Phototoxic Oils

Furanocoumarins in these oils react with ultraviolet light on skin, causing burns, blistering or long-lasting dark patches. Perfumery standards (IFRA) cap their concentration in leave-on products.

- Bergamot (expressed): strongest of the citrus oils
- Lime (expressed)
- Lemon (expressed)
- Bitter orange (expressed)
- Grapefruit (expressed): mild
- Angelica root
- Cumin
- Rue (*Ruta graveolens*)
- Fig leaf absolute (banned in fragrance)

Not phototoxic: sweet orange, distilled citrus oils, FCF bergamot, citrus leaf oils (petitgrain).

### 3.16 Protected and Endangered Sources

| Plant | Status | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| Indian sandalwood (*Santalum album*) | Harvest tightly controlled in India; heavily poached | Plantation sandalwood; Australian sandalwood (*S. spicatum*); amyris |
| Rosewood (*Aniba rosaeodora*) | CITES Appendix II | Ho wood; linalool-rich basil or coriander |
| Agarwood (*Aquilaria* spp.) | CITES Appendix II | Inoculated plantation oud |
| Spikenard (*Nardostachys jatamansi*) | CITES Appendix II | Cultivated sources; valerian for similar earthy notes |
| Atlas cedar (*Cedrus atlantica*) | IUCN Endangered | Oil from fallen or managed timber; Virginia cedarwood |
| Frankincense (*Boswellia papyrifera* and others) | Declining from overtapping | Sustainably certified resin |
| Brazilian sassafras (*Ocotea odorifera*) | Endangered | Not replaced; use is restricted regardless |

### 3.17 Scents With No True Essential Oil

Many popular scents cannot be distilled or extracted in usable amounts. Products sold under these names are **fragrance oils** built from synthetic or isolated aroma chemicals.

- **Fruits:** strawberry, raspberry, apple, peach, cherry, watermelon, mango, banana, coconut, cucumber. Fruit aromas are dominated by esters present in tiny amounts that break down in steam.
- **Flowers:** lilac, lily of the valley, gardenia, honeysuckle, freesia, cherry blossom, sweet pea, wisteria, lotus and magnolia. These either yield almost nothing or change character completely when heated. Rare, very expensive absolutes exist for a few (gardenia, lotus, magnolia), but nearly all commercial versions are synthetic.
- **Concepts:** rain, ocean, clean linen, fresh snow.

Home enfleurage (Section 3.9) or alcohol tinctures (Section 3.6) are the practical ways to capture a natural version of these flower scents.

## 4. Maximizing Yield and Quality

### 4.1 Harvesting

- **Timing:** Harvest at early flowering (roughly 10% bloom). Oil content and menthol peak at this stage; menthone converts to menthol as the plant matures.
- **Time of day:** Late morning on a dry, sunny day after dew has dried.
- **Avoid:** Harvesting after rain or in heavy shade, both of which lower oil content.

### 4.2 Preparation

- **Wilting:** Let the cut plants wilt for 1 to 2 days in shade. Removing water reduces the volume to distill and increases oil yield per batch. Over-drying loses oil to evaporation.
- **Chopping:** Coarse chopping helps steam penetrate. Fine grinding releases oil prematurely.
- **Packing:** Pack evenly and moderately. Packing too tightly causes steam channeling, where steam finds a single path and bypasses most of the material.

### 4.3 Distillation

- Keep steam flow steady and gentle.
- Keep condenser water cold so all vapor condenses; warm condensate loses oil as vapor.
- **Cohobation:** Redistill the hydrosol to recover dissolved oil. Adds a small amount of extra yield.

### 4.4 Separation and Drying

- Use a Florentine flask (an oil-water separator) or a separatory funnel.
- Remove remaining water droplets by adding a small amount of anhydrous magnesium sulfate or sodium sulfate, then filtering. Alternatively, freeze briefly so water forms ice crystals and decant the oil.

### 4.5 Storage

- Dark amber or cobalt glass bottles
- Cool location; refrigeration extends life
- Minimal headspace to limit oxidation
- Tight caps; oil vapor degrades some plastics and rubber
- Shelf life: roughly 2 to 4 years for peppermint when stored well

## 5. Home Steam Distillation Procedure

### 5.1 Equipment

- Large stockpot or pressure cooker body (stainless steel)
- Steamer basket or perforated platform to hold plant material above water
- Lid fitted with an outlet port
- Copper or stainless coil condenser (or a glass Liebig condenser)
- Cold water supply or ice bath for the condenser
- Glass separator or separatory funnel
- Heat source with steady control
- Amber glass bottles for storage
- Food-grade silicone tubing and fittings

Small copper or stainless alembic stills sold for home distilling work well and avoid improvised fittings.

### 5.2 Steps

1. Fill the pot with water below the level of the basket.
2. Load wilted, coarsely chopped mint into the basket without compressing it.
3. Seal the lid and connect the outlet to the condenser.
4. Start cold water flowing through the condenser.
5. Bring the water to a steady boil.
6. Collect the condensate in the separator. Oil appears as a thin pale-yellow layer on top.
7. Continue for 60 to 90 minutes or until no new oil appears.
8. Let the collected liquid settle, then draw off the oil.
9. Dry the oil and transfer it to an amber bottle.
10. Keep the hydrosol in a clean bottle in the refrigerator for use as a facial mist, room spray or cooking ingredient.

### 5.3 Expected Output

- 1 kg fresh mint yields roughly 3 to 5 mL of oil.
- Partially dried mint yields more oil per kilogram of starting weight.
- Small home stills produce modest volumes; several batches are typically needed for a useful quantity.

## 6. Isolating Individual Components

### 6.1 Menthol Crystallization

- Menthol is solid at room temperature (melting point about 42°C).
- High-menthol oil (especially cornmint oil, which contains 70 to 80% menthol) is chilled, causing menthol to crystallize.
- Crystals are separated by filtration or centrifuge and may be recrystallized for higher purity.
- The remaining liquid is sold as dementholized mint oil.
- Peppermint oil contains less menthol and crystallizes less readily, so commercial menthol comes mostly from cornmint or from synthesis.

### 6.2 Fractional Distillation

Separates components by boiling point. Approximate boiling points at atmospheric pressure:

| Component | Boiling Point (°C) |
|---|---|
| Limonene | 176 |
| 1,8-Cineole | 176 |
| Menthofuran | ~196 |
| Menthone | 207 |
| Isomenthone | ~208 |
| Menthol | 212 |
| Pulegone | 224 |
| Menthyl acetate | ~228 |

- Many of these boil within a few degrees of each other, so a column with high separation efficiency is required.
- Distillation is done under vacuum to lower boiling points and prevent heat damage.
- **Rectified (redistilled) peppermint oil** is produced this way: the lightest front fraction (harsh, grassy notes) and heaviest tail fraction are removed to improve flavor.

### 6.3 Chromatography

- Column chromatography or preparative gas chromatography can isolate individual compounds at high purity.
- Used in laboratories and for analytical standards rather than bulk production.

### 6.4 Chemical Conversion

- Menthone can be reduced to menthol using hydrogenation, increasing total menthol content.
- Menthol can be esterified with acetic acid to produce menthyl acetate.

---

## 7. Quality Testing

- **GC-MS (gas chromatography-mass spectrometry):** The standard method for identifying and measuring each component.
- **Physical tests:** Specific gravity, optical rotation and refractive index. Pure peppermint oil has characteristic ranges for each.
- **Common adulteration:** Blending with cheaper cornmint oil, adding synthetic menthol or diluting with carrier oils or solvents.
- **Regulatory limits:** Pharmacopoeia and ISO standards set limits on pulegone and menthofuran content.

---

## 8. Handling Facts

- Typical skin dilution is 1 to 3% in a carrier oil. Undiluted peppermint oil can irritate or burn skin and mucous membranes.
- Menthol applied near the face of infants and young children can trigger reflexive breathing difficulty.
- Cats lack enzymes to process many essential oil compounds; peppermint oil is toxic to them.
- Contact with eyes causes intense stinging; flush with a fixed oil or milk rather than water, since the oil does not dissolve in water.
- Ingestion of concentrated oil in large amounts can cause poisoning; food and capsule products use measured, diluted doses.
