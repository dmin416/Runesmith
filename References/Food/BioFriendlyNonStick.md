# Bio-Friendly Non-Stick Coatings

Earth seasoning, enamel and metal-pan care for kitchen release without synthetic nonstick. Setting pan picks and physics: `CookwareAndKnives.md`. Production methods: `KitchenCraft.md`. Metal law: `../World/Materials/Metals.md`.

## 1. Seasoned Cast Iron

**What it is:** Cooking oil polymerized into a hard, slick layer bonded to the iron surface.

**How it is made:**
1. Scrub the pan to bare, clean metal and dry it fully over heat.
2. Wipe on a very thin layer of high-smoke-point oil (flaxseed, canola, grapeseed, sunflower or shortening).
3. Wipe off as much oil as possible so the surface looks almost dry.
4. Bake upside down at 450 to 500°F (230 to 260°C) for 1 hour.
5. Let it cool in the oven.
6. Repeat 3 to 6 times for a durable base layer.

**Useful range:**
- Seasoning holds to about 500 to 600°F (260 to 315°C) and carbonizes above that.
- The pan itself has no practical cooking limit.

**Notes:**
- The surface improves with use and can be repaired at home.
- The only migration is dietary iron, which matters only for people with iron-overload conditions.
- Long simmering of acidic foods (tomato, wine, vinegar) can strip seasoning.

## 2. Seasoned Carbon Steel (including nitrided carbon steel)

**What it is:** Thin rolled or spun steel with the same polymerized oil seasoning as cast iron. Nitrided versions have a hard iron-nitride surface underneath.

**How it is made:**
- Plain carbon steel: Remove the factory wax or oil coating with hot water and soap or by baking it off. Then season with the same thin-oil oven method used for cast iron or by stovetop heating thin oil layers until they darken.
- Nitriding (factory process): Gas or salt-bath nitriding at about 930 to 1080°F (500 to 580°C) diffuses nitrogen into the surface. Seasoning is then applied on top.

**Useful range:**
- Seasoning holds to about 500 to 600°F (260 to 315°C).
- The pan itself has no practical cooking limit.

**Notes:**
- It is lighter and heats faster than cast iron.
- Nitrided surfaces resist rust better than plain carbon steel.
- Migration profile is the same as cast iron: dietary iron only.

## 3. Lead- and Cadmium-Free Vitreous Enamel

**What it is:** A layer of glass fused onto cast iron or steel.

**How it is made (factory process):**
1. Glass is melted and quenched into frit, then ground to powder.
2. The frit is mixed into a slip and sprayed or dipped onto grit-blasted metal.
3. The piece is fired at about 1470 to 1560°F (800 to 850°C) so the glass fuses to the metal.
4. Usually 2 or more coats are applied and fired.

**Useful range:**
- Typically rated to about 500°F (260°C) and higher, depending on the maker and the knob or handle material.
- Sudden temperature changes can crack or craze the enamel.
- Heating an empty pan on high heat can damage the enamel.

**Notes:**
- The surface is inert glass and does not react with acidic foods.
- Matte black interior enamel builds a seasoning-like patina over time and becomes more non-stick.
- Glossy light-colored enamel is only moderately non-stick.
- Buy from established makers (Le Creuset, Staub, Lodge enamel lines). Some cheap imported glazes contain lead or cadmium.
- Retire a pan if the cooking surface chips down to metal.

# Handling and Care

## Cast Iron

**Cooking:**
- Preheat slowly on medium-low for 5 to 10 minutes. Iron heats unevenly and builds hot spots on high heat.
- Use fat for the first several months. Frying, searing, roasting and cornbread build seasoning fastest.
- Keep long acidic simmers (tomato sauce, wine braises, vinegar) short until the seasoning is mature.
- Handles get as hot as the pan. Use a towel or handle sleeve.

**Cleaning:**
1. Clean while the pan is still warm.
2. Rinse with hot water. A small amount of dish soap is fine since modern soap contains no lye.
3. Scrub stuck food with a chain-mail scrubber, a stiff brush or coarse salt with a little oil.
4. Dry immediately with a towel, then set on a burner for 1 to 2 minutes to drive off moisture.
5. Wipe on a very thin layer of oil and heat until it just starts to smoke.
6. Let it cool and store it dry with the lid off or cracked open.

**Avoid:**
- Dishwashers.
- Soaking in water.
- Cold water on a hot pan, which can crack it.
- Storing food in the pan.

**Rust or flaking repair:**
1. Scrub the rust or loose seasoning off with steel wool.
2. Wash, dry on heat and re-season with 2 to 3 oven coats.

## Carbon Steel

- Care is the same as cast iron.
- It heats and cools faster, so preheat for less time.
- New seasoning may spot or turn patchy brown. This is normal and evens out with use.

## Stainless Steel

**Cooking without sticking:**
1. Preheat the empty pan on medium for 2 to 3 minutes.
2. Test with a few drops of water. When the drops bead up and skate across the surface, the pan is at temperature (about 350 to 400°F / 175 to 205°C).
3. Wipe out the water, add oil and let it shimmer.
4. Add food that is patted dry and close to room temperature.
5. Leave the food alone until it releases on its own.
6. Deglaze stuck bits with stock, wine or water to make a pan sauce.

**Cleaning:**
- Soak stuck-on food in hot soapy water. Simmer water with a spoonful of baking soda for stubborn residue.
- Remove white calcium spots with a splash of vinegar.
- Remove rainbow heat tint and brown discoloration with Bar Keepers Friend or a baking soda paste.
- Dishwasher safe, though hand washing keeps the finish brighter.

**Avoid:**
- Adding salt to cold water. Undissolved salt can pit the surface. Add salt once the water is boiling.
- Steel wool on polished surfaces. Use a non-scratch pad.
- Plunging a hot pan into cold water, which can warp it.

**Health note:**
- 18/10 and 18/8 stainless release small amounts of nickel and chromium during long acidic simmering. For nickel sensitivity, use 18/0 (nickel-free) stainless or enamel for acidic dishes.

## Copper

**Know the lining:**
- **Stainless-lined copper:** Most durable. Cook and clean like stainless steel on the inside.
- **Tin-lined copper:** Excellent food release. Tin melts at 450°F (232°C).
- **Unlined copper:** Only for jam pots, sugar work and egg-white bowls. Acidic foods dissolve copper into the food, which causes copper poisoning.

**Cooking with tin-lined copper:**
- Stay at medium heat or below and keep under about 425°F (220°C).
- Never heat the pan empty.
- Use wood, silicone or nylon utensils.
- Re-tin when the copper shows through the lining.

**Cleaning:**
- Hand wash only with mild soap and a soft sponge.
- Dry immediately to prevent water spots.
- Exterior polish: lemon juice and salt, ketchup, vinegar and salt, or a commercial copper polish.
- A darkened patina on the exterior is harmless. Polishing is cosmetic only.

**Avoid:**
- Dishwashers.
- Abrasive pads on the interior lining.
- Bleach and harsh chemical cleaners.

## Titanium

**Know the type:**
- **Solid titanium (mostly camping and backpacking pots):** Fully inert and safe with any food.
- **Titanium-clad (titanium cooking surface over an aluminum core):** Inert surface with better heat spread.
- **"Titanium-reinforced" non-stick:** PTFE with titanium particles. Not on the safe list.

**Cooking:**
- Solid titanium conducts heat poorly and creates hot spots. Use low to medium heat and stir often.
- Add enough liquid or fat to prevent scorching.
- Never heat empty. It can warp or discolor.
- Best used for boiling, simmering and acidic dishes rather than searing.

**Cleaning:**
- Mild soap and a non-abrasive sponge.
- Blue, gold or purple heat tint is harmless.
- Simmer water with baking soda for burnt residue.
