# Vehicle Suspension

Earth tech ladder for vehicle suspension. Each entry lists the era and the tools, materials and processes that must exist first. Companion: `VehicleWheels.md`.

**Accuracy:** solid popular engineering-history summary. Dates and “firsts” are order-of-magnitude, good enough for prerequisite checks.

**Caldris readout** (`../World/Technology.md`): craftsman + early-industrial magitech. Road carts and coaches can use **rigid axles**, strap / thoroughbrace coach bodies, and **leaf springs** once steel strip and heat treat exist in smith guilds. Rail and heavy wagons can add located live axles. **Not** baseline: telescopic gas shocks as consumer goods, hydropneumatic self-leveling cars, MagneRide / fully active systems, MacPherson / multi-link as mass auto layouts. Those need invent arcs, rare workshops, or magic substitutes.

## Pre-Industrial (Antiquity to 1800)

**1. Rigid axle with no springing** (Sumerian carts, chariots, Roman wagons)
- Pros: cheapest and most durable option; repairable with hand tools.
- Cons: transmits every impact to cargo and occupants; cracks axles and wheels.
- Tech level: Copper Age (c. 3500 BC). Stone or copper woodworking tools and basic carpentry.

**2. Flexible-floor and flexible-frame chariots** (Egyptian chariot floors of woven leather thongs; flexing wooden poles and yokes)
- Pros: adds mild cushioning and damping through material hysteresis at almost no cost.
- Cons: very small travel; leather stretches and rots; ride stays harsh.
- Tech level: Bronze Age (c. 2000 to 1500 BC). Rawhide preparation; wicker and thong weaving; bronze tools.

**3. Suspended body on straps or chains** (medieval chariot branlant; Kocs-style carriages)
- Pros: first true isolation of the passenger cabin from the running gear; cheap.
- Cons: pitching and swaying that causes motion sickness; straps stretch and fail; no control of body roll.
- Tech level: Late Medieval (c. 1300 to 1450). Tanned leather; forged iron fittings; skilled coachbuilding.

**4. Thoroughbrace** (heavy leather straps between C-shaped irons; Concord stagecoach)
- Pros: durable for rough roads; the swing absorbs large shocks; field-repairable.
- Cons: fore-aft rocking; leather ages and needs constant maintenance.
- Tech level: Early Modern to Early Industrial (c. 1700 to 1830). Heavy leather working; wrought iron forging; wheelwright and coachbuilder trades.

## Industrial Age Springs (1700s to 1900s)

**5. Leaf springs** (whip, C, platform and Dennett forms; semi-elliptic type patented by Obadiah Elliott in 1804)
- Pros: strong; locates the axle as well as springing it; interleaf friction supplies some damping; simple to service.
- Cons: heavy; stiction causes harshness; sags with fatigue; rate is hard to tune.
- Tech level: Early Industrial (c. 1750 to 1810). Crucible steel then Bessemer steel; controlled forging and heat treatment; rolling mills for flat strip.

**6. Coil springs**
- Pros: compact; efficient energy storage; easy to tune with progressive or dual rates.
- Cons: no lateral or longitudinal location; needs separate links; no built-in damping.
- Tech level: Late 19th to Early 20th Century (c. 1880 to 1930). Drawn spring wire; coiling machinery; hardening and tempering furnaces; later shot peening.

**7. Torsion bar** (Volkswagen Beetle; Tiger tank; many trucks)
- Pros: compact; adjustable ride height; long travel for the package space; suits tracked vehicles.
- Cons: fatigue failure risk; needs presetting; expensive; harder to tune.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1930s). Alloy spring steel; precision heat treatment; presetting (scragging) equipment; splined machining.

**8. Pneumatic tire as a spring element** (Dunlop, 1888)
- Pros: very light unsprung mass; constant damping.
- Cons: limited by tire pressure and sidewall; cannot handle large bumps alone.
- Tech level: Late 19th Century (c. 1888). Vulcanized rubber; rubberized fabric; airtight valves.

## Damping Devices

**9. Friction dampers** (Truffault, early 1900s)
- Pros: simple and cheap.
- Cons: friction changes with wear and dirt; not proportional to speed.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1900 to 1920). Machined steel arms; friction discs; basic metalworking.

**10. Hydraulic lever-arm dampers** (Houdaille, 1920s to 1950s)
- Pros: velocity-sensitive damping; strong and durable.
- Cons: heavy; leak-prone; limited range.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1920s). Cast housings; machined vane and piston assemblies; hydraulic fluid sealing.

**11. Telescopic dampers** (twin-tube; monotube; gas-charged after de Carbon in the 1950s)
- Pros: compact; tunable; monotubes resist fade.
- Cons: seals wear; foaming and heat fade in cheap versions.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1930s to 1950s). Precision bores and honing; synthetic rubber and PTFE seals; shim-stack valving; gas pressurization.

## Non-Metallic and Fluid Springs

**12. Rubber springs** (Mini cone springs; Neway truck suspensions)
- Pros: compact; progressive; built-in damping; no metal-on-metal noise.
- Cons: ages; sensitive to temperature; creeps; limited travel.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1950s). Compounded synthetic rubber; rubber-to-metal bonding processes; molding presses.

**13. Hydrolastic and Hydragas** (interconnected fluid or gas springs on British Leyland cars)
- Pros: front and rear interconnection reduces pitch; compact.
- Cons: leaks; specialized service; complex plumbing.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1960s). Rubber diaphragm and displacer units; pressurized fluid systems; leak-free interconnecting pipes; precision assembly.

**14. Hydropneumatic** (Citroën DS, 1955; later Citroën and Rolls-Royce systems)
- Pros: self-leveling; exceptional ride; adjustable height.
- Cons: complex; fluid leaks; expensive; specialized mineral fluid; hard to repair.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1950s). High-pressure pumps and accumulators; precision-ground spools and valves; nitrogen-charged diaphragm spheres; reliable high-pressure seals.

**15. Air springs** (bellows on buses and trucks since the 1930s; luxury cars today)
- Pros: adjustable height and rate; load leveling; excellent isolation.
- Cons: bellows perforate or crack; compressor and valve failures; costly.
- Tech level: Early to Mid 20th Century (c. 1930s bellows; c. 1950s to 2000s full car systems). Reinforced rubber bellows; compressors and valve blocks; later electronic height sensing and control.

## Electronic and Adaptive

**16. Semi-active dampers** (electronically controlled valves; magnetorheological fluid as in Delphi MagneRide)
- Pros: adapts damping in milliseconds without large power draw.
- Cons: sensor and software dependency; expensive fluid or valves.
- Tech level: Late 20th to Early 21st Century (c. 1980s to 2000s). Microcontrollers; accelerometers and position sensors; proportional solenoid valves or magnetorheological fluid.

**17. Fully active suspension** (Lotus 1980s; Mercedes Active Body Control; Bose electromagnetic)
- Pros: eliminates the compromise between ride and handling; controls roll and pitch.
- Cons: very costly; high power demand; complex; failure modes need fallbacks.
- Tech level: Late 20th to Early 21st Century (c. 1985 to 2010s). High-bandwidth hydraulic or electromagnetic actuators; real-time control software; multi-sensor arrays; redundant safety design.

## Axle and Linkage Layouts

**18. Live (solid) axle on leaf springs** (Hotchkiss drive)
- Pros: strong; simple; camber stays constant under load; ideal for trucks and off-road.
- Cons: high unsprung mass; wheel motion on one side affects the other.
- Tech level: Early Industrial (c. 1800 to 1900). Forged or cast axle beams; leaf spring manufacture; basic machining.

**19. Located live axles** (Panhard rod; Watt's linkage; three-link; four-link; torque tube)
- Pros: better axle control with coil springs; good articulation.
- Cons: bushing wear; geometry compromises.
- Tech level: Early to Mid 20th Century (c. 1910s to 1960s). Machined link arms; rubber bushings; basic suspension geometry analysis.

**20. De Dion tube**
- Pros: low unsprung mass compared with a live axle; camber control.
- Cons: fixed geometry; packaging and cost.
- Tech level: Late 19th Century (c. 1894 concept; widespread c. 1920s to 1960s). Fabricated steel tube; sliding or link location; inboard brakes and driveshafts with universal joints.

**21. Swing axle** (early Volkswagen and Mercedes rear ends)
- Pros: cheap; simple; independent movement.
- Cons: severe camber change; jacking and snap oversteer.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1920s to 1930s). Universal joints; pressed or forged arms; basic machining.

**22. Trailing arm and semi-trailing arm**
- Pros: compact; good packaging.
- Cons: toe and camber shifts (semi-trailing); poor lateral rigidity.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1930s to 1960s). Forged or cast arms; rubber bushings; kinematic analysis for semi-trailing geometry.

**23. Twist-beam (torsion beam) rear axle**
- Pros: very cheap; compact; light.
- Cons: geometry compromises; limited tuning.
- Tech level: Mid to Late 20th Century (c. 1970s). Open-section steel beams; pressed and welded assembly; computer-aided stress analysis.

**24. Sliding pillar** (Lancia Lambda 1922; Morgan)
- Pros: compact; simple; camber constant.
- Cons: friction and wear at the pillars; binding under lateral load.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1900 to 1930). Precision-ground pillars; bronze bushings; lubrication systems.

**25. Double wishbone** (unequal-length arms)
- Pros: excellent control of camber and roll; tunable.
- Cons: packaging; number of parts; cost.
- Tech level: Early to Mid 20th Century (c. 1930s to 1950s). Ball joints or pivot pins; forged or stamped arms; kinematic design methods.

**26. MacPherson strut** (Ford Vedette and Consul, c. 1949 to 1951)
- Pros: compact; cheap; light; good for front-wheel-drive packaging.
- Cons: geometry limits; strut bearing wear; transmits noise.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1949). Telescopic damper technology; monocoque body with strong strut towers; ball joints; coil springs.

**27. Multi-link** (Mercedes W201, 1982; many modern cars)
- Pros: independent control of camber and toe; best ride and handling compromise.
- Cons: many joints and bushings; expensive; complex alignment.
- Tech level: Late 20th Century (c. 1980s). Computer-aided kinematic modeling; stamped or forged steel links; precision bushings; subframe assembly.

**28. Dubonnet and trailing link**
- Pros: independent front movement; compact.
- Cons: complexity; wear; limited adoption.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1930s). Enclosed spring and damper units; precision machining; leak-free lubrication.

## Tracked and Rail Vehicles

**29. Christie suspension** (large road wheels on long coil springs; 1928)
- Pros: high speed over rough terrain; long travel.
- Cons: springs take internal hull space; complex.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1928). Heavy coil spring manufacture; alloy steel; welded armored hulls.

**30. Horstmann and bogie designs**
- Pros: rugged; simple; external and easy to service.
- Cons: limited travel; harsher ride.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1920s to 1930s). Forged steel arms; coil or leaf springs; simple machining.

**31. Hydropneumatic tracked suspension** (Challenger 2 and others)
- Pros: high travel; adjustable stance; superior ride.
- Cons: complicated; leaks; heavy maintenance.
- Tech level: Late 20th Century (c. 1970s to 1990s). High-pressure gas-oil struts; precision machining; specialized seals; hull integration.

**32. Rail primary and secondary suspension** (journal box springs plus air or coil bolster springs)
- Pros: high stability at speed; low wear on track.
- Cons: complexity; alignment sensitive.
- Tech level: Late 19th to Late 20th Century (c. 1880s to 1980s). Steel springs; rubber elements; air bags; dampers; bogie frame fabrication.

## Motorcycle and Bicycle

**33. Girder and springer forks**
- Pros: simple; robust.
- Cons: stiction; limited damping; heavy.
- Tech level: Late 19th to Early 20th Century (c. 1890s to 1940s). Steel tubing; coil springs; friction dampers; basic forging and brazing.

**34. Telescopic fork**
- Pros: simple; compact; widely serviceable.
- Cons: stiction under braking loads; flex.
- Tech level: Early to Mid 20th Century (c. 1935 to 1950s). Precision tubing and bushings; hydraulic damping; fork seals.

**35. Swingarm with twin shocks and later monoshock**
- Pros: rigid; progressive linkage possible; simple.
- Cons: chain-torque effects; packaging.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1930s twin shock; c. 1970s monoshock). Fabricated steel arms; gas-charged shocks; linkage engineering.

**36. Earles, Hossack and hub-center systems**
- Pros: reduce brake dive; alternative geometry.
- Cons: complex; costly; niche.
- Tech level: Mid to Late 20th Century (c. 1950s to 1990s). Precision fabricated links; specialized bearings; computer-aided geometry design.

## Summary by Technological Level

- **Copper and Bronze Age (3500 to 1200 BC):** rigid axle; flexible-floor and flexible-frame chariots.
- **Medieval to Early Modern (1300 to 1750):** strap-suspended body; thoroughbrace.
- **Early Industrial (1750 to 1850):** leaf springs; live axle on leaf springs.
- **Second Industrial Revolution (1850 to 1914):** coil springs; pneumatic tire; early de Dion tube; girder forks; sliding pillar (early).
- **Early 20th Century (1914 to 1945):** friction dampers; hydraulic lever-arm dampers; torsion bar; swing axle; located live axles; Dubonnet; double wishbone; Christie; Horstmann; early air springs; early telescopic forks.
- **Mid 20th Century (1945 to 1990):** telescopic dampers; rubber springs; Hydrolastic and Hydragas; hydropneumatic; MacPherson strut; trailing arm; twist beam; swingarm monoshock; hydropneumatic tracked; Earles and Hossack.
- **Late 20th to 21st Century (1980 onward):** multi-link; semi-active dampers; fully active suspension; modern electronic air suspension.

The historical trend follows metallurgy and machining precision. Each leap in performance came from either better steel or tighter tolerances and seals.
