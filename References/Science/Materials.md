## Copper Family

### Bronze

- **Composition:** about 88-90% copper and 10-12% tin
- **Density:** about 8.8 g/cm³
- **Melting range:** about 950-1000 °C
- **Tensile strength:** about 300-400 MPa
- **Strengths:** wear resistance, low friction, corrosion resistance
- **Uses:** bearings, bushings, bells, marine hardware

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 6-7 MS/m (about 11-13% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 50-60 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 380 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Tin atoms distort the copper lattice and scatter conduction electrons so strongly that 10% tin drops conductivity to roughly one eighth of pure copper.
- **Science:** Bell bronze runs about 20-22% tin. The extra tin forms the hard brittle delta phase (Cu31Sn8) which loses very little energy per vibration cycle so a struck bell rings for a long time.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** copper cathode (99.9%) and tin ingot (99.9%). Phosphor-copper master alloy (CuP15) is the standard deoxidizer for phosphor bronze.

1. Melt the copper in a graphite or clay-graphite crucible at about 1150-1200 °C under a charcoal or flux cover to limit oxidation.
2. Deoxidize with phosphor-copper at about 0.2-0.5% of melt weight before the tin goes in.
3. Add preheated dry tin at 10-12% of total weight. Tin melts at 232 °C and dissolves almost instantly. Adding it late limits tin oxide losses.
4. Stir with a preheated graphite rod and skim all dross from the surface.
5. Pour at about 1100-1150 °C into preheated sand or investment molds. Pour in one smooth stream to avoid turbulence and oxide entrainment.
6. Use risers and chills. 10% tin bronze has a wide freezing range and is prone to shrinkage porosity.
7. Shake out the casting and cut off gates.
8. Homogenize at 600-700 °C to remove tin segregation. Cold-worked parts are annealed at 600-700 °C between reduction passes.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt in a vacuum induction furnace at about 1150-1200 °C. Vacuum strips dissolved hydrogen and oxygen so phosphorus deoxidation becomes optional.
- After the tin dissolves hold at a partial pressure of about 50-200 mbar argon instead of hard vacuum to limit tin loss.
- Degas the melt for 5-10 minutes at that pressure then pour by vacuum-assisted or counter-gravity casting into preheated investment molds. This fills thin sections and cuts gas porosity.
- Anneal finished parts in a vacuum furnace at 600-700 °C for a bright oxide-free surface.

**Hazards:** molds and tools must be completely dry since moisture causes steam explosions in molten metal. Metal fume requires ventilation.

### C10100 (Oxygen-Free Electronic Copper)

- **Composition:** 99.99% copper
- **Density:** 8.94 g/cm³
- **Melting point:** 1083 °C
- **Strengths:** peak electrical and thermal conductivity with no hydrogen embrittlement
- **Uses:** bus bars, waveguides, vacuum and high-current components

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 58.6 MS/m (101% IACS minimum) with resistivity near 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 391 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 385 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Free electrons carry both charge and heat so the Wiedemann-Franz law ties the two conductivities together. Thermal conductivity divided by electrical conductivity equals the Lorenz number (2.44 × 10⁻⁸ W·Ω/K²) times temperature. Copper at room temperature lands close to that value.
- **Science:** Around 10-20 K ultra-pure copper reaches a thermal conductivity peak of several thousand W/m·K because lattice vibration scattering fades while impurity scattering is still small.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** high-purity electrorefined copper cathode.

1. Smelt copper concentrate into matte then convert it to blister copper (about 98.5% Cu).
2. Fire-refine the blister copper and cast it into anodes.
3. Electrorefine in copper sulfate and sulfuric acid cells at about 200-300 A/m². Pure copper plates onto stainless or copper starter cathodes over 7-14 days. Impurities fall to the cell bottom as anode slime.
4. Strip and wash the cathodes. These are about 99.99% copper.
5. Melt the cathodes in a channel induction furnace under a protective atmosphere of carbon monoxide and nitrogen (or in vacuum) so oxygen stays under 5 ppm.
6. Continuous-cast (Upcast or Properzi style) under the same protective atmosphere into rod or billet.
7. Hot roll or extrude then draw to final size. Anneal in a reducing or inert atmosphere.
8. Verify with the hydrogen embrittlement test. C10100 shows no embrittlement after heating in hydrogen.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum induction melt and cast the cathode directly. Oxygen drops below 5 ppm without relying on carbon monoxide cover gas.
- For ultra-high purity use electron beam melting at about 10⁻⁴ mbar. Volatile impurities such as zinc and lead and sulfur compounds boil out of the melt.
- Anneal drawn parts in a vacuum furnace at about 400-600 °C for a bright oxygen-free finish.
- Join parts by vacuum brazing to keep the oxygen-free condition through assembly.

### Brass

- **Composition:** about 60-70% copper and 30-40% zinc (C36000 free-cutting grade has added lead)
- **Density:** about 8.5 g/cm³
- **Melting range:** about 900-940 °C
- **Tensile strength:** about 340-470 MPa
- **Strengths:** machinability, formability, corrosion resistance
- **Uses:** fittings, valves, fasteners, instruments

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 15-16 MS/m (about 26-28% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 115-120 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 380 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Season cracking is stress corrosion cracking of cold-worked brass in the presence of trace ammonia. It was first documented in brass cartridge cases in India during the monsoon season. Stress-relief annealing at 250-300 °C prevents it.
- **Science:** Zinc sets both color and crystal structure. Low-zinc brass is red-gold and 30% zinc is yellow. Above about 38% zinc the harder body-centered cubic beta phase appears next to the alpha phase which is why 60/40 brass hot-works so easily.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** copper cathode or clean scrap and special high-grade zinc ingot (99.99%). C36000 adds 2.5-3.7% lead.

1. Melt the copper at about 1100 °C in an induction furnace or crucible under charcoal cover.
2. Lower the melt temperature to about 1000 °C. Zinc boils at 907 °C so it must go in last.
3. Plunge the zinc below the surface with a preheated tool. Charge 2-5% extra zinc to cover evaporation losses.
4. For C36000 add lead after the zinc and stir thoroughly.
5. Skim and pour at about 1000-1050 °C for 70/30 brass or about 950-1000 °C for 60/40.
6. Industrial route is semi-continuous casting of billets followed by extrusion at 650-800 °C.
7. Draw or roll to final size. Anneal at 425-600 °C and stress-relieve finished parts at about 250-300 °C.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt and degas the copper under vacuum at about 1100 °C first. This removes hydrogen and oxygen before any zinc goes in.
- Backfill with argon to about 1-2 bar before adding zinc. Hard vacuum boils zinc away since it vaporizes at 907 °C. A pressure-tight chamber with argon overpressure holds zinc loss near zero.
- Cast by vacuum-assisted gravity or low-pressure die casting to cut porosity in fittings and valve bodies.
- Anneal finished parts in a vacuum furnace at about 425-600 °C only with an argon partial pressure of about 10-100 mbar to prevent zinc depletion from the surface.

**Hazards:** zinc oxide fume causes metal fume fever. Local exhaust ventilation is required.

### Cupronickel

- **Composition:** 90/10 or 70/30 copper to nickel
- **Density:** about 8.9 g/cm³
- **Melting range:** about 1100-1240 °C
- **Strengths:** seawater corrosion resistance and biofouling resistance
- **Uses:** ship hulls, heat exchanger tubing, desalination piping, coinage

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 5 MS/m (about 9% IACS) for 90/10 and about 2.7 MS/m (about 4.6% IACS) for 70/30
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 50 W/m·K for 90/10 and about 29 W/m·K for 70/30
- **Specific heat:** about 380 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Copper-nickel near 55/45 (constantan) has resistivity that barely changes with temperature because alloy scattering swamps the temperature-dependent lattice scattering. That makes it the standard material for strain gauges and precision resistors. It is also one leg of type J, T and E thermocouples.
- **Science:** In seawater 90/10 grows a thin protective film of cuprous oxide with nickel and iron built in. Slow release of copper ions from the film also discourages barnacles and mussels from settling.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** copper cathode and electrolytic nickel (99.9%) with small iron and manganese additions (about 1.0-1.8% Fe and up to 1% Mn for 90/10 or 0.4-1.0% Fe and up to 1% Mn for 70/30).

1. Melt the copper at about 1250-1300 °C in an induction furnace under charcoal cover.
2. Add the nickel pieces. Nickel melts at 1455 °C but dissolves readily in molten copper at these temperatures.
3. Add iron and manganese (as ferro-manganese or pure metals).
4. Deoxidize with a small addition of manganese or titanium and magnesium.
5. Pour at about 1300-1400 °C for 70/30 or slightly lower for 90/10. Direct-chill cast billets or slabs.
6. Hot work at 900-1000 °C by extrusion or rolling.
7. Anneal at 760-815 °C. Tubes are drawn and sometimes seam-welded then re-annealed.
8. Pickle to remove scale.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum induction melting is the best route. Melt the copper and nickel together at about 1250-1300 °C under vacuum.
- Deoxidize by vacuum carbon deoxidation. Carbon reacts with dissolved oxygen and leaves as carbon monoxide gas so no residual deoxidizer stays in the alloy.
- Backfill with argon to about 200-500 mbar before adding manganese since manganese evaporates readily under hard vacuum.
- Remelt ingots by vacuum arc remelting or electroslag remelting for cleaner tube-grade stock.
- Anneal at about 760-815 °C in a vacuum furnace for oxide-free tubing.

**Hazards:** nickel dust and fume require extraction.

## Silver

### Fine Silver

- **Composition:** 99.9% silver
- **Density:** 10.49 g/cm³
- **Melting point:** 961.8 °C
- **Strengths:** top electrical and thermal conductivity and reflectivity
- **Uses:** contacts, mirrors, bullion, electronics

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 63 MS/m (about 108% IACS) which is the highest of any metal
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 429 W/m·K (also the highest of any metal)
- **Specific heat:** about 235 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Silver reflects about 95-98% of visible light but reflectivity collapses near 320 nm in the ultraviolet because of its plasma edge. The metal turns briefly transparent at about 316 nm.
- **Science:** Tarnish is silver sulfide (Ag2S) from trace hydrogen sulfide in air and not an oxide. Silver oxide is unstable and decomposes above about 200 °C.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** silver is mostly a byproduct of lead, zinc and copper refining. Scrap silver is also a major feedstock.

1. Recover silver from lead bullion with the Parkes process. Zinc is added to molten lead and a silver-rich zinc crust floats off.
2. Distill off the zinc and cupel the remaining silver-lead at about 900-1000 °C in air. Lead oxidizes to litharge and is absorbed or skimmed off. Crude silver (doré) remains.
3. Refine electrolytically with the Moebius process. Doré anodes in a silver nitrate and copper nitrate electrolyte deposit crystalline silver (99.9%+) at the cathode.
4. Scrape and wash the crystals.
5. Alternative chemical route for scrap: dissolve in nitric acid then precipitate silver chloride with salt then convert to metal by reducing with sodium hydroxide and sugar or with zinc or iron.
6. Melt the silver in a graphite crucible at about 1000-1050 °C under borax flux.
7. Pour into ingot molds or into water for casting grain.

**Vacuum option:**

- Filter silver chloride and precipitated silver powder through a Büchner funnel under vacuum. Washing and dewatering run much faster.
- Dry the washed powder in a vacuum oven at about 100-150 °C.
- Melt in a vacuum induction furnace. Molten silver absorbs many times its volume in oxygen and spits on solidification so vacuum or argon backfill gives sound dense bars.
- Vacuum refine the melt at about 1100-1200 °C and about 10⁻² mbar. Volatile impurities such as zinc and cadmium and lead and bismuth evaporate off.
- Cast under vacuum or argon into graphite molds.

**Hazards:** nitric acid and nitrogen oxide fumes require a fume hood.

### Sterling Silver

- **Composition:** 92.5% silver and 7.5% copper
- **Density:** about 10.3 g/cm³
- **Melting point:** about 893 °C
- **Strengths:** much harder and more durable than fine silver while keeping the look
- **Uses:** jewelry, cutlery, tableware

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 50 MS/m (about 85-90% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 360 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 245 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Silver and copper form a eutectic at 71.9% silver that melts at 779 °C. That is far below silver (962 °C) and copper (1085 °C). Sterling starts melting at 779 °C and is fully liquid at about 893 °C.
- **Science:** Copper barely dissolves in solid silver at room temperature. Quenched sterling holds the copper in solution and stays soft. Aging at about 300 °C precipitates copper-rich particles and the metal hardens. Copper near the surface also oxidizes during heating and leaves dark firescale.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** fine silver (99.9%) and copper (OFE or casting grain). Weigh by mass at 92.5 to 7.5.

1. Place both metals in a graphite crucible with a borax and boric acid flux or work under a reducing flame.
2. Heat to about 1000-1050 °C. The two metals are miscible in all proportions at this temperature.
3. Stir with a graphite rod to prevent copper segregation.
4. Pour at about 950-1000 °C into ingot molds or quench into water for casting grain.
5. Roll or draw with intermediate anneals at about 600-650 °C then quench or air cool.
6. Pickle in dilute sulfuric acid or a commercial pickle to remove copper oxide.
7. Optional age hardening at about 300 °C for 1-2 hours.
8. Add about 1.2% germanium to make Argentium-type tarnish and firescale resistant sterling.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt in a vacuum induction furnace and backfill with argon before pouring. This prevents copper oxidation and oxygen porosity and the firescale that follows.
- Use vacuum-assist or vacuum-pressure casting for lost-wax work. Metal is drawn into fine detail and gas porosity drops sharply.
- Evacuate freshly mixed investment for 1-2 minutes before pouring the flask to remove trapped air bubbles.
- Anneal in a vacuum furnace at about 600-650 °C to avoid firescale entirely.

## Gold

### 24k Gold

- **Composition:** 99.9%+ gold
- **Density:** 19.3 g/cm³
- **Melting point:** 1064 °C
- **Strengths:** total corrosion immunity and stable low-resistance contact surface
- **Weakness:** very soft
- **Uses:** electrical contacts, plating, bullion

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 45 MS/m (about 78% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 318 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 129 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Gold's color is a relativistic effect. Its inner electrons move at a sizable fraction of light speed so the 6s orbital contracts and the 5d orbitals expand. That narrows the gap between them to about 2.4 eV so gold absorbs blue light and reflects yellow and red.
- **Science:** One gram of gold can be beaten into a sheet of about 0.5 m² at leaf thickness (about 100 nm) or drawn into about 165 m of 20 µm wire.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** doré bullion from mining or scrap gold.

1. Miller process: bubble chlorine gas through molten gold at about 1100 °C. Silver and base metals form chlorides that float off as slag leaving about 99.5% gold.
2. Wohlwill process: cast the Miller gold into anodes and electrolyze in a gold chloride and hydrochloric acid electrolyte at about 60-70 °C. Cathode gold reaches 99.99%.
3. Chemical refining route: dissolve scrap in aqua regia (3 parts hydrochloric acid to 1 part nitric acid) then filter out silver chloride.
4. Precipitate gold from solution with sodium metabisulfite or oxalic acid or ferrous sulfate.
5. Wash the gold powder with hot water and dilute hydrochloric acid then dry.
6. Melt the powder in a graphite or clay crucible at about 1100-1150 °C under borax flux.
7. Pour into ingot molds preheated to about 100-150 °C then roll or cast as needed.

**Vacuum option:**

- Filter precipitated gold powder under vacuum through a Büchner funnel then dry in a vacuum oven at about 100-150 °C.
- Melt in a vacuum induction furnace at about 1100-1150 °C. Cast bars come out clean and free of flux inclusions.
- Vacuum refine at about 1200 °C and about 10⁻² mbar. Zinc and lead and cadmium and bismuth evaporate from the melt before the Wohlwill step.
- For thin films evaporate gold from a tungsten boat or electron beam source at about 10⁻⁶ mbar onto a substrate.

**Hazards:** aqua regia and chlorine require a fume hood or closed system.

### 18k Gold

- **Composition:** 75% gold with copper and silver or palladium
- **Density:** about 15-16 g/cm³ depending on alloy
- **Melting range:** about 885-1000 °C
- **Strengths:** durable and tarnish resistant with color variety (yellow, rose, white)
- **Uses:** fine jewelry, watch cases

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 6-12 MS/m (roughly 10-20% IACS depending on alloy)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 70-100 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 170 J/kg·K for yellow 18k (mass-weighted from its metals)
- **Science:** Purple gold is the intermetallic AuAl2 which is about 78.5% gold by mass so it qualifies as 18k. It is brittle like a ceramic and gets its color from its electronic structure rather than from alloying.
- **Science:** Red and rose gold harden by atomic ordering. Gold-copper forms ordered AuCu superlattice phases below roughly 400 °C. Heat treatment moves atoms into those ordered positions which raises hardness and shifts color slightly.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** 24k gold plus copper and silver or palladium. Weigh so gold is exactly 75% of total mass.

Typical recipes per 100 g:

- **Yellow:** 75 g gold, 12.5 g silver, 12.5 g copper
- **Rose:** 75 g gold, 22.25 g copper, 2.75 g silver
- **Red:** 75 g gold, 25 g copper
- **White:** 75 g gold with 15-25 g palladium and balance silver or copper
- **Green:** 75 g gold, 25 g silver

1. Melt the gold and copper first in a graphite crucible at about 1100 °C under borax flux.
2. Add silver or palladium and stir with a graphite rod until uniform.
3. Pour at about 1100-1150 °C into preheated ingot molds for rolling and drawing.
4. For lost-wax jewelry casting burn out the investment flask at about 500-700 °C then cast by centrifugal or vacuum-assist at about 1000-1100 °C.
5. Quench the flask after 5-10 minutes then divest.
6. Anneal wrought stock at about 650-700 °C and quench or air cool.
7. Pickle and finish.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt gold and copper under vacuum then backfill with argon before adding silver or palladium. Pour under argon or in vacuum-assist casting.
- Add any zinc (master alloy) only after backfilling to about 200-500 mbar argon. Zinc boils off under hard vacuum.
- Vacuum-invest the flask. Evacuate the mixed investment before pouring and again after filling the flask to remove air bubbles that cause surface nodules.
- Cast by vacuum-assist or vacuum-pressure so the metal fills fine detail with minimal porosity.
- Anneal wrought stock in a vacuum furnace at about 650-700 °C for oxide-free surfaces.

### Electrum

- **Composition:** gold and silver (natural form is roughly 20-50% silver)
- **Density:** about 13-16 g/cm³ depending on ratio
- **Melting range:** about 1000 °C
- **Strengths:** pale yellow color and historical significance
- **Uses:** ancient coinage and decorative work

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 8-12 MS/m (about 15-20% IACS) depending on ratio and far below either pure gold or pure silver
- **Thermal conductivity:** roughly 70-100 W/m·K (estimated from the Wiedemann-Franz law)
- **Specific heat:** about 160-185 J/kg·K depending on ratio
- **Science:** Gold and silver mix in all proportions. Resistivity follows Nordheim's rule and peaks near 50/50 because maximum atomic disorder scatters electrons most. Mixing two excellent conductors gives a poor one.
- **Science:** Density tracks composition (19.3 g/cm³ for gold against 10.5 for silver) so specific gravity estimates the gold fraction. The wide gold content swings in natural Lydian electrum coins pushed ancient mints toward the touchstone assay and later toward separate pure gold and pure silver coinage.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** fine gold and fine silver. Natural electrum is also panned or mined as a native alloy in placer and vein deposits.

1. Weigh the target ratio. Historic coinage electrum ran roughly 50-75% gold. Silver content of 25-45% gives the classic pale gold tone.
2. Melt the gold first at about 1100-1150 °C in a graphite crucible under borax flux.
3. Add the silver. The two metals are fully miscible.
4. Stir thoroughly with a graphite rod and pour at about 1050-1100 °C into a preheated ingot mold.
5. Roll or hammer into sheet or blank with anneals at about 600-700 °C.
6. For coinage strike the annealed blanks between dies.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt gold under vacuum then backfill with argon to about 200-500 mbar before adding silver. Silver absorbs oxygen and spits on freezing and has a higher vapor pressure than gold so a partial pressure limits silver loss.
- Degas the melt briefly at that pressure then pour into a preheated mold under argon or by vacuum-assist.
- Anneal blanks in a vacuum furnace at about 600-700 °C for a clean surface before striking.

## Iron

### Ductile Cast Iron

- **Composition:** iron with 3.2-3.6% carbon and 2.2-2.8% silicon (graphite in spheroidal form)
- **Density:** about 7.1 g/cm³
- **Melting range:** about 1150-1200 °C
- **Tensile strength:** about 400-800 MPa
- **Strengths:** castability with real ductility and good wear and vibration damping
- **Uses:** pipes, crankshafts, gears, suspension parts

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 1.7-2 MS/m (about 3% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 32-36 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 470 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Graphite precipitating during solidification takes up more volume than the iron it came from. That expansion partly offsets shrinkage and lets ductile iron castings feed themselves.
- **Science:** Round graphite nodules do not concentrate stress the way the sharp flakes in gray iron do. A crack tip meets a smooth sphere and blunts so elongation jumps from near zero in gray iron to roughly 5-18% in ductile iron.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** pig iron and steel scrap and foundry returns. Carburizer (graphite or coke). Ferrosilicon. Magnesium nodularizer (FeSiMg containing about 5-6% Mg). Inoculant (75% ferrosilicon).

1. Melt the charge in an induction furnace or cupola at about 1450-1500 °C.
2. Adjust chemistry to about 3.4-3.6% carbon and 2.2-2.8% silicon. Sulfur must be under 0.02% since sulfur consumes magnesium.
3. Tap into a treatment ladle holding the FeSiMg (about 1.5-2% of melt weight) in a pocket or under a cover.
4. Magnesium boils at about 1090 °C so the reaction is violent. Residual magnesium of 0.03-0.05% makes the graphite grow as spheres.
5. Inoculate with ferrosilicon in the ladle or stream to promote nodule count.
6. Pour within about 10-15 minutes before magnesium fades at about 1350-1400 °C into green sand or resin-bonded molds.
7. Cool and shake out.
8. Heat treat as needed. Ferritic anneal at about 900 °C for 2-4 hours then furnace cool. Normalize for pearlitic strength. Austemper (austenitize at 870-950 °C then hold in a salt bath at 250-400 °C) for austempered ductile iron.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt in a vacuum induction furnace at about 1450-1500 °C. Hydrogen and nitrogen and oxygen drop and sulfur control gets easier.
- Do the magnesium treatment in a pressure-tight vessel under argon at about 1-2 bar. Hard vacuum would pull magnesium vapor straight out of the iron.
- Use the V-process for molds. A thin plastic film is vacuum-formed over the pattern then dry binder-free sand is held rigid by vacuum at about 400-600 mbar below atmospheric. Castings have smooth surfaces and fine detail.
- Heat treat in a vacuum furnace for clean ferritic anneals and austempering without surface scale.

**Hazards:** magnesium treatment produces flare and splash. Full face shield and ladle covers are standard.

### Silicon Iron (Electrical Steel)

- **Composition:** iron with about 2-3.5% silicon (up to 6.5% in specialty grades)
- **Density:** about 7.65 g/cm³
- **Strengths:** low magnetic core loss and high permeability
- **Uses:** transformer cores, motors, generators

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 2 MS/m (about 3.5% IACS) which is roughly one fifth of pure iron
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 25 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 460 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Adding 3% silicon raises resistivity about fivefold over pure iron (from about 0.1 to about 0.47 µΩ·m). Higher resistivity chokes eddy currents inside the core which cuts heat loss.
- **Science:** Magnetostriction makes the core change length slightly as it magnetizes. The change repeats at twice the mains frequency so transformers hum at 100 Hz or 120 Hz. Near 6.5% silicon magnetostriction almost vanishes and the hum drops.
- **Science:** Saturation flux density is about 2.0 T and the Curie temperature is about 730 °C.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** low-carbon steel and ferrosilicon.

1. Melt in an electric arc or basic oxygen furnace and add ferrosilicon to reach 2-3.5% silicon.
2. Vacuum degas (RH process) to bring carbon below about 0.005%. Carbon causes magnetic aging.
3. Continuous cast into slabs.
4. Hot roll at about 1100-1250 °C to about 2-2.5 mm strip.
5. Cold roll to final gauge (0.23-0.65 mm).
6. **Grain-oriented grade:** add MnS or AlN inhibitors in the melt then cold roll in two stages with an intermediate anneal. Decarburize at about 800-850 °C in wet hydrogen and nitrogen. Coat with MgO and give a high-temperature anneal at about 1200 °C. This drives secondary recrystallization into the Goss (110)[001] texture.
7. **Non-oriented grade:** single cold reduction then final anneal at about 800-1000 °C.
8. Apply an insulating coating and slit or stamp laminations.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum degas the liquid steel in an RH or tank degasser at about 1 mbar. Carbon and nitrogen and hydrogen and oxygen all drop and carbon can reach under 0.003%.
- Vacuum induction melt small heats for lab or specialty grades with exact silicon and low impurity levels.
- Run the final purifying anneal in vacuum or dry hydrogen at about 1100-1200 °C. Sulfur and nitrogen leave the strip and the Goss texture develops cleanly.
- Anneal punched laminations in a vacuum furnace at about 750-800 °C for stress relief with no oxidation.

### Wrought Iron

- **Composition:** iron with under 0.08% carbon and fibrous slag inclusions
- **Density:** about 7.7 g/cm³
- **Tensile strength:** about 300 MPa
- **Strengths:** forge welding, corrosion resistance and fatigue tolerance
- **Uses:** ornamental work, historic restoration, blacksmithing

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 7-10 MS/m (about 12-17% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 55-60 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 450 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Iron loses ferromagnetism at its Curie point of 770 °C. At 912 °C the lattice switches from body-centered to face-centered cubic which packs tighter so the iron briefly shrinks while heating through that point.
- **Science:** Silicate slag stringers run through the metal like wood grain. They make it tough along the grain and keep corrosion pits from spreading deep. They also act as flux during forge welding.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** iron ore (hematite or magnetite) and charcoal. Pig iron for the puddling route.

**Bloomery route:**

1. Build a clay shaft furnace with a tuyere for forced air from bellows or a blower.
2. Layer charcoal and crushed ore and run the furnace at about 1100-1250 °C. This is below iron's melting point. Carbon monoxide reduces the ore to a solid sponge of iron.
3. Slag (iron silicate) runs out the bottom.
4. After several hours extract the bloom (a spongy mass of iron and slag).
5. Hammer the hot bloom at about 1150-1250 °C to squeeze out slag and consolidate.
6. Fold and forge-weld repeatedly to refine and form bar.

**Puddling route (industrial era):**

1. Melt pig iron in a reverberatory furnace with an iron oxide lining.
2. Stir (rabble) the bath so oxygen burns out carbon. As carbon drops the melting point rises and the iron turns pasty.
3. Gather the pasty iron into balls (blooms) of about 40-50 kg.
4. Squeeze the balls under a hammer then roll into bar. Slag stays as fibrous stringers that give wrought iron its grain.

**Vacuum option:**

- Carbothermic reduction runs at lower temperature under vacuum since removing carbon monoxide pulls the reaction forward. Ore and charcoal briquettes in a vacuum retort reduce to iron sponge at about 1000-1100 °C.
- Can the sponge or bloom pieces in a mild steel tube then evacuate and seal. Hot press or forge at about 1100-1200 °C. This consolidates the iron with no scale and keeps the slag fiber structure.
- Forge-weld bar stacks sealed in an evacuated steel can. Oxide never forms on the mating faces so welds close fully.
- Remove the can by machining or acid after consolidation.

## Steel

### 1095 (High-Carbon Steel)

- **Composition:** iron with about 0.95% carbon
- **Density:** about 7.85 g/cm³
- **Hardness:** up to about 65 HRC when quenched
- **Strengths:** edge retention and spring behavior
- **Uses:** knives, blades, springs, hand tools

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 5-6 MS/m (about 10% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 46 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 470 J/kg·K
- **Science:** The eutectoid composition is 0.77% carbon. At 0.95% carbon 1095 is slightly hypereutectoid so slow cooling leaves a network of iron carbide (cementite) at grain boundaries. Overheating coarsens that network and makes the steel brittle.
- **Science:** Quenching traps carbon in a distorted body-centered tetragonal lattice called martensite. The transformation expands volume by a few percent which is why quenched blades warp and sometimes crack.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** pig iron and scrap steel. Carburizer and ferro-manganese for adjustments.

**Mill production:**

1. Melt in an electric arc furnace or refine hot metal in a basic oxygen furnace.
2. Adjust to 0.90-1.03% carbon and 0.30-0.50% manganese with phosphorus under 0.040% and sulfur under 0.050%.
3. Ladle refine and continuous cast into blooms or billets.
4. Hot roll at about 1100-1200 °C into bar or strip.
5. Spheroidize anneal (hold near 750 °C then cool slowly) to soften for machining.

**Heat treatment for blades:**

1. Normalize 2-3 cycles (heat to about 870 °C then air cool) to refine grain.
2. Austenitize at about 790-815 °C for 10-15 minutes. The steel turns non-magnetic at this point.
3. Quench in fast oil preheated to about 50-70 °C.
4. Temper immediately at about 175-230 °C for 1-2 hours twice for 58-62 HRC. Springs temper at about 400-480 °C.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum degas the liquid steel (VD or RH) to cut hydrogen and oxygen and nitrogen. Vacuum induction melting followed by vacuum arc remelting gives the cleanest steel with the finest carbides.
- Austenitize in a vacuum furnace at about 790-815 °C. No decarburization and no scale form so the surface hits full hardness.
- Quench in a vacuum oil-quench chamber. 1095 needs a fast quench and high-pressure gas (nitrogen at 6-10 bar) is too slow for thick sections.
- Add a cryogenic soak at about -80 to -196 °C after the quench then temper in the vacuum furnace under partial pressure.

## Carbon

### Carbon Fiber Composite

- **Density:** about 1.6-1.8 g/cm³
- **Fiber tensile strength:** about 3.5-7 GPa
- **Fiber modulus:** about 230-600 GPa
- **Strengths:** top strength-to-weight and stiffness-to-weight
- **Uses:** aerospace structures, racing, bicycles, prosthetics

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 0.02-0.06 MS/m along the fibers but only about 1-100 S/m through the thickness where epoxy separates the plies
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 5-10 W/m·K along standard PAN fibers (pitch fibers reach 500-1000) and about 0.5-1 W/m·K through the thickness
- **Specific heat:** about 800-1000 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Carbon fiber shrinks slightly along its length when heated (about -0.1 to -1 × 10⁻⁶/K) while epoxy expands. Layups can be tuned to near-zero expansion for telescope and satellite structures.
- **Science:** Carbon is a noble cathode against aluminum so direct contact in damp air or seawater corrodes the aluminum fast. Aircraft skins also carry embedded copper mesh because the composite carries lightning current poorly.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** polyacrylonitrile (PAN) precursor fiber and epoxy resin.

**Fiber:**

1. Spin PAN into filaments by wet or dry-jet wet spinning then stretch.
2. Stabilize (oxidize) in air at about 200-300 °C for 30-120 minutes under tension. The fiber turns black and becomes infusible.
3. Carbonize in nitrogen at about 1000-1500 °C. Hydrogen and nitrogen and oxygen leave and the fiber becomes about 92-95% carbon.
4. Optional graphitization at about 2000-3000 °C in argon for high-modulus grades.
5. Electrolytic surface treatment then apply sizing and wind onto spools.

**Composite part:**

1. Cut prepreg (fiber pre-impregnated with epoxy) into plies and orient at 0/±45/90 degrees as the load requires.
2. Lay up on a mold and cover with release film and breather and vacuum bag.
3. Cure in an autoclave at about 120-180 °C and about 6-7 bar for 1-3 hours.
4. Alternatives: resin infusion (VARTM) and resin transfer molding and filament winding and pultrusion and wet layup with vacuum bagging.
5. Target fiber volume fraction of about 55-60%.
6. Trim and drill with carbide or diamond-coated tools.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum degas the epoxy at about 5-20 mbar before use to remove dissolved air and volatiles. Voids in the laminate drop sharply.
- Vacuum bag every layup. Debulk the stack under vacuum after every 3-4 plies to compact the plies and remove trapped air.
- Use vacuum-assisted resin infusion (VARTM). Dry fabric is bagged and evacuated and resin is drawn through at about 0.9-1.0 bar below atmospheric then cured at 60-120 °C.
- Cure out-of-autoclave prepreg under vacuum bag alone at about 120-180 °C. This avoids autoclave pressure and gives low void content.
- Carbonize or graphitize fiber in a vacuum furnace at 1500-3000 °C when an inert-gas furnace is not available.

**Hazards:** cutting and sanding dust is an irritant. Use respirators and extraction.

### Diamond

- **Density:** 3.51 g/cm³
- **Hardness:** 10 Mohs (hardest natural material)
- **Strengths:** unmatched hardness and heat spreading with electrical insulation
- **Uses:** cutting and grinding tools, heat spreaders, optics, gemstones

- **Electrical conductivity:** essentially none. Pure diamond is an insulator with resistivity above 10¹² Ω·m and a band gap of 5.47 eV
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 2000-2200 W/m·K for natural type IIa and up to about 3300 W/m·K for isotopically pure carbon-12 synthetic
- **Specific heat:** about 509 J/kg·K at 300 K
- **Science:** Heat travels through diamond as lattice vibrations (phonons) not as electrons. Stiff bonds give it a Debye temperature of about 2230 K so it has an unusually low specific heat for its atomic mass at room temperature and still moves heat better than any common bulk material.
- **Science:** A refractive index of about 2.42 gives a critical angle of only 24.4°. Light entering the crown is trapped by total internal reflection until it exits through the top which produces the brilliance.

#### Production

**HPHT (high pressure high temperature):**

1. Load a graphite carbon source with a molten metal catalyst (iron and nickel and cobalt alloy) and a diamond seed into a cell.
2. Press in a belt or cubic or BARS press at about 5-6 GPa and about 1300-1600 °C.
3. Dissolved carbon precipitates onto the seed over days to weeks.
4. Recover by dissolving the metal in acid.

**CVD (chemical vapor deposition):**

1. Place a diamond seed plate in a vacuum chamber.
2. Feed hydrogen with about 1-5% methane.
3. Ignite a microwave plasma at about 700-1000 °C and about 100-200 mbar.
4. Carbon deposits layer by layer at roughly 1-10 µm per hour (faster in some reactors).
5. Optional HPHT anneal to improve color.

**Polycrystalline diamond (PCD) for tools:**

1. Sinter diamond powder with cobalt at about 5-6 GPa and about 1400-1500 °C onto a tungsten carbide substrate.

**Finishing:** cut and polish with diamond-impregnated wheels.

**Vacuum option:**

- CVD needs vacuum. Pump the chamber to about 10⁻⁶ mbar base pressure to remove air and moisture then run the process gas at about 100-200 mbar for microwave plasma or about 10-50 mbar for hot filament reactors.
- Outgas diamond powder under vacuum at about 800-1000 °C before HPHT or PCD sintering. Adsorbed gases and surface oxides leave and the sintered bond strengthens.
- Anneal CVD stones in vacuum or inert gas at about 1200-1600 °C to relieve strain. Higher temperatures start to graphitize the surface in low-pressure conditions.
- Deposit metal contact layers on finished diamond by vacuum sputtering or evaporation.

### Graphite

- **Density:** about 2.2 g/cm³
- **Sublimation point:** about 3650 °C
- **Strengths:** heat resistance, electrical conductivity and self-lubrication
- **Uses:** electrodes, crucibles, lubricants, battery anodes, brushes

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 2.5 MS/m in-plane for oriented crystal (HOPG) and about 0.1-0.2 MS/m for electrode-grade graphite. Across the planes HOPG conducts roughly a thousand times worse or more
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 1500-2000 W/m·K in-plane for HOPG and about 5-10 W/m·K across the planes. Electrode grade runs about 100-150 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 710 J/kg·K at 25 °C
- **Science:** Graphite gets stronger when hot. Strength roughly doubles between room temperature and about 2500 °C because microcracks close as the lattice expands.
- **Science:** Pyrolytic graphite is the strongest room-temperature diamagnet in common use. It levitates over a checkerboard of strong magnets with no power.

#### Production

**Natural:** mine flake or vein graphite then purify by flotation and acid leaching or thermal treatment.

**Synthetic:**

1. Calcine petroleum coke (needle coke for high grades) at about 1300 °C.
2. Crush and grind then blend with coal tar pitch binder at about 150 °C.
3. Form the green body by extrusion or molding or isostatic pressing.
4. Bake at about 800-1200 °C for several weeks in a ring furnace buried in packing coke. This carbonizes the binder.
5. Impregnate with pitch and re-bake 1-3 times to raise density.
6. Graphitize at about 2500-3000 °C in an Acheson furnace. Electric current flows directly through the packing coke and the pieces. Heating and cooling together take 1-2 weeks and the carbon rearranges into hexagonal graphite crystal.
7. Optional purification at about 2400 °C with halogen gas to remove metal ash.
8. Machine to shape with standard tooling.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum impregnate the baked body. Evacuate the part first then flood with molten pitch and pressurize. Pitch fills the pores far deeper than atmospheric dipping and density rises faster per cycle.
- Bake and graphitize in a vacuum furnace at about 2000-3000 °C. This removes the need for packing coke and gives cleaner higher-purity graphite.
- Purify at about 2000-2400 °C under vacuum. Metal impurities and sulfur evaporate and ash content drops below 5 ppm in high-purity grades.
- Deposit pyrolytic graphite by low-pressure CVD. Methane decomposes on a heated substrate at about 1800-2200 °C and a few mbar and forms dense highly oriented layers.

### Graphene

- **Structure:** single atomic layer of carbon
- **Tensile strength:** about 130 GPa (intrinsic)
- **Strengths:** extreme strength and conductivity with near-total transparency
- **Uses:** composites, coatings, sensors, battery research

- **Electrical conductivity:** carrier mobility reaches about 200,000 cm²/V·s in suspended sheets (silicon is about 1,400). Undoped CVD films typically show 100-1000 Ω/sq sheet resistance
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 3000-5000 W/m·K measured on suspended single layers
- **Specific heat:** about 700 J/kg·K at room temperature (close to graphite)
- **Science:** A single layer absorbs 2.3% of white light. That equals π times the fine-structure constant so a pure number set by fundamental physics fixes how much light one atom-thick sheet blocks.
- **Science:** Electrons in graphene act like massless particles moving at about 10⁶ m/s (roughly 1/300 of light speed) with a linear Dirac-cone energy-momentum relation. This gives a half-integer quantum Hall effect visible at room temperature. Two layers twisted by about 1.1° (the magic angle) become superconducting near 1.7 K.

#### Production

**Mechanical exfoliation (lab quality):**

1. Press adhesive tape against highly oriented pyrolytic graphite and peel repeatedly.
2. Press the thinned flakes onto a silicon oxide wafer and locate monolayers by optical contrast.

**CVD on copper (large-area film):**

1. Load copper foil into a tube furnace and heat to about 1000 °C.
2. Flow hydrogen and methane at low pressure. Carbon dissolves and precipitates as a single layer on the copper surface.
3. Spin-coat PMMA on top then etch away the copper in ammonium persulfate or ferric chloride.
4. Float the film onto the target substrate and dissolve the PMMA with acetone.

**Liquid-phase exfoliation (bulk powder):**

1. Sonicate or shear-mix graphite in NMP or surfactant-water solution.
2. Centrifuge to separate thin flakes from thick ones.

**Hummers method (graphene oxide):**

1. Oxidize graphite with sulfuric acid and sodium nitrate and potassium permanganate to make graphite oxide.
2. Exfoliate in water by sonication into graphene oxide sheets.
3. Reduce with hydrazine or ascorbic acid or heat to make reduced graphene oxide.

**Epitaxial growth:** heat silicon carbide wafers to about 1200-1600 °C to sublimate silicon and leave graphene on the surface.

**Flash Joule heating:** pulse high current through carbon black or waste carbon to reach about 3000 °C in milliseconds and produce turbostratic graphene.

**Vacuum option:**

- Run CVD at low pressure (about 0.1-10 mbar) in the tube furnace. Growth is more uniform and contamination drops.
- Anneal the copper foil at about 1000 °C under vacuum and hydrogen before growth to clean and enlarge the copper grains.
- Grow epitaxial graphene on silicon carbide in ultra-high vacuum (about 10⁻⁹ mbar) at about 1200-1300 °C for the best layer control.
- Vacuum anneal transferred films at about 200-400 °C to drive off PMMA residue.
- Assemble graphene or graphene oxide films by vacuum filtration of the dispersion through a membrane.
- Run flash Joule heating in a low-pressure chamber so volatiles leave quickly.

**Hazards:** the Hummers reaction is strongly exothermic and releases toxic gases.

## Titanium

### Ti-6Al-4V (Grade 5)

- **Composition:** titanium with 6% aluminum and 4% vanadium
- **Density:** 4.43 g/cm³
- **Tensile strength:** about 950 MPa
- **Melting range:** about 1600-1660 °C
- **Strengths:** best all-around titanium with high strength-to-weight and heat tolerance
- **Uses:** aircraft frames, engine parts, implants, racing hardware

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 0.58 MS/m (about 1% IACS) with resistivity near 1.7 µΩ·m
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 6.7 W/m·K which is among the lowest of any structural metal
- **Specific heat:** about 526 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Poor heat conduction keeps cutting heat at the tool edge instead of letting it flow into the part. That is a main reason titanium is hard to machine.
- **Science:** The surface oxide (TiO2) grows only a few nanometers thick on its own. Anodizing thickens it to tens or hundreds of nanometers where thin-film interference produces vivid colors with no dye. Voltage sets thickness and therefore color.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** rutile or ilmenite ore then titanium sponge then aluminum-vanadium master alloy.

**Titanium sponge (Kroll process):**

1. Chlorinate the ore with chlorine gas and coke at about 900-1000 °C to make titanium tetrachloride (TiCl₄).
2. Purify by fractional distillation.
3. Load molten magnesium into a sealed stainless steel reactor under argon and feed in TiCl₄ at about 800-850 °C. Titanium sponge and magnesium chloride form.
4. Remove magnesium and magnesium chloride by vacuum distillation or leaching over several days.
5. Crush and screen the sponge.

**Alloying and melting:**

1. Blend sponge with aluminum-vanadium master alloy (such as 60Al-40V or 85V-15Al) and extra aluminum.
2. Press blends into compacts and weld them into a consumable electrode.
3. Melt by vacuum arc remelting (VAR) two or three times for homogeneity. Cold-hearth electron beam melting is used for high-integrity aerospace stock.
4. Forge above the beta transus (about 995 °C) then finish in the alpha-beta field.

**Heat treatment:**

1. Mill anneal at about 700-785 °C for 1-4 hours then air cool.
2. Or solution treat at about 955 °C then age at about 540 °C.
3. Pickle in nitric and hydrofluoric acid to remove the oxygen-rich alpha case.

**Vacuum option:**

- Titanium picks up oxygen and nitrogen and hydrogen above about 500-600 °C so vacuum is the standard environment for every hot step.
- Melt by vacuum arc remelting (already vacuum) or electron beam cold hearth melting at about 10⁻⁴ mbar. Add excess aluminum in the electrode since aluminum evaporates under vacuum.
- Vacuum induction skull melting works for small castings in a water-cooled copper crucible.
- Run all anneals and stress relief in a vacuum furnace at 10⁻⁴ mbar or better. Alpha case does not form.
- Dehydrogenate in vacuum at about 700-750 °C to bring hydrogen below 125 ppm.
- Consolidate powder by vacuum hot pressing or hot isostatic pressing and join parts by vacuum brazing or electron beam welding.

**Hazards:** titanium fines and turnings are combustible. Kroll process involves chlorine and molten magnesium.

### Commercially Pure Grade 2

- **Composition:** unalloyed titanium
- **Density:** 4.51 g/cm³
- **Tensile strength:** about 345 MPa minimum
- **Melting point:** about 1665 °C
- **Strengths:** top corrosion resistance and weldability with good formability
- **Uses:** chemical processing, marine hardware, heat exchangers

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 1.8 MS/m (about 3% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 16-17 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 523 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Titanium changes from hexagonal alpha to body-centered cubic beta at 882 °C. Aluminum raises that transition temperature and vanadium lowers it which is the basis of alloys like Ti-6Al-4V.
- **Science:** The oxide film heals itself within moments of a scratch whenever oxygen or water is present which makes the metal essentially immune to chloride pitting in seawater. Pure titanium also turns superconducting below about 0.4 K.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** titanium sponge from the Kroll process (see Ti-6Al-4V).

1. Press sponge into compacts with no alloy additions and weld them into an electrode.
2. Melt by VAR two or three times. Control oxygen to 0.25% max and iron to 0.30% max. Oxygen content sets the grade strength.
3. Forge or roll into slab or bar. Rolling into coil gives strip for tube and plate.
4. Anneal at about 650-760 °C.
5. Form tube by cold pilgering or by roll-forming strip and welding the seam under argon.
6. Pickle in nitric and hydrofluoric acid.
7. Weld only with full inert-gas shielding of the weld and trailing area.

**Vacuum option:**

- Melt by VAR or electron beam cold hearth melting under vacuum. Hydrogen and volatile chlorides leave the melt and oxygen is held to the grade limit.
- Anneal at about 650-760 °C in a vacuum furnace at 10⁻⁴ mbar or better. No alpha case forms and no pickling is needed.
- Dehydrogenate in vacuum at about 700-750 °C to bring hydrogen below 125 ppm.
- Join by vacuum brazing or electron beam welding for oxygen-free joints.

### Nitinol

- **Composition:** about 55% nickel and 45% titanium by weight
- **Density:** about 6.45 g/cm³
- **Melting point:** about 1310 °C
- **Strengths:** shape memory and superelasticity
- **Uses:** stents, eyeglass frames, actuators, springs

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 1.2-1.3 MS/m (about 2% IACS). Resistivity shifts with phase at about 0.82 µΩ·m for austenite and about 0.76 µΩ·m for martensite
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 18 W/m·K for austenite and about 8.6 W/m·K for martensite
- **Specific heat:** about 450-500 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Superelastic strain recovers up to about 8% against roughly 0.5% for spring steel. The strain is carried by a reversible crystal transformation between austenite and stress-induced martensite rather than by stretching atomic bonds.
- **Science:** The same transformation gives an elastocaloric effect. Releasing a loaded wire absorbs latent heat and cools it by roughly 20 K which is the basis of solid-state refrigeration research.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** high-purity nickel and titanium. Weigh to about 55-56 wt% nickel (near-equiatomic). A shift of 0.1 at% nickel moves the transformation temperature by about 10 °C.

1. Melt by vacuum induction melting (VIM) in a high-density graphite or yttria crucible. Carbon pickup must be controlled.
2. Remelt by vacuum arc remelting (VAR) for composition uniformity.
3. Hot work at about 800-950 °C by forging or extrusion or rolling.
4. Cold work 30-40% per stage with intermediate anneals at about 700-800 °C.
5. Draw wire or roll strip to final size.
6. **Shape setting:** constrain the part on a fixture and heat at about 450-550 °C for 5-60 minutes then water quench.
7. Fine-tune the transformation temperature with the aging time and temperature.
8. Electropolish or passivate for corrosion resistance.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum induction melting at 10⁻³ mbar or better is the standard route. Titanium reacts violently with air at melt temperature.
- Use a high-density graphite or yttria crucible and keep the melt time short to hold carbon pickup low.
- Remelt by vacuum arc remelting two or three times for composition uniformity.
- Run shape-setting and aging heat treatments in a vacuum or argon furnace at about 450-550 °C. Surfaces stay clean and the oxide layer stays thin.
- Fire up electron beam melting or vacuum induction skull melting for high-purity medical grade stock.

## Tungsten

### Tungsten Carbide with Cobalt Binder

- **Composition:** tungsten carbide with about 6-12% cobalt
- **Density:** about 14.5-15 g/cm³
- **Hardness:** about 1400-1800 HV
- **Strengths:** extreme hardness and wear resistance with usable toughness
- **Uses:** cutting inserts, drill bits, dies, wear parts

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 4-5 MS/m (roughly 7-9% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 80-110 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 200-250 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Young's modulus is about 550-650 GPa which is roughly three times steel. Hardness and toughness trade off through grain size and cobalt content. Finer grains and less cobalt make it harder. Coarser grains and more cobalt make it tougher.
- **Science:** Cobalt is ferromagnetic so magnetic saturation of a sintered part reveals the carbon balance in the binder. Too little carbon forms brittle eta phase and too much leaves free graphite. Tungsten carbide also acts as a catalyst with platinum-like behavior (Levy and Boudart 1973).

#### Production

**Raw materials:** tungsten ore (scheelite or wolframite) then ammonium paratungstate (APT) then tungsten powder. Cobalt powder. Carbon black.

1. Digest the concentrate in sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate and purify by solvent extraction to make APT.
2. Calcine APT at about 500-800 °C into tungsten oxide.
3. Reduce in hydrogen at about 700-1000 °C into tungsten metal powder. Temperature and gas flow set particle size.
4. Carburize the tungsten powder with carbon black at about 1400-1600 °C to make WC (6.13% carbon).
5. Mill the WC powder with 6-12% cobalt and a wax binder in an attritor or ball mill for 10-40 hours in ethanol or hexane.
6. Spray dry into free-flowing granules.
7. Press in a die or by cold isostatic pressing at about 200 MPa.
8. Dewax at about 200-400 °C.
9. Sinter at about 1350-1500 °C in vacuum or in sinter-HIP at 50-100 bar argon. Cobalt melts and wets the carbide grains in liquid-phase sintering and the part shrinks about 17-20% linearly.
10. Grind with diamond wheels. Optionally coat with TiN or TiAlN by PVD or CVD.

**Vacuum option:**

- Dry the milled slurry in a vacuum rotary dryer. Solvent comes off at low temperature and the powder stays free of oxidation.
- Dewax under vacuum at about 200-400 °C with a small hydrogen or argon flow to carry vapors out.
- Vacuum sinter at about 1350-1450 °C. Hold about 1-10 mbar argon partial pressure during the hot hold since cobalt evaporates under hard vacuum.
- Finish with sinter-HIP. After vacuum sintering raise argon pressure to 50-100 bar at temperature to close residual porosity.
- Outgas pressed compacts at about 600-800 °C under vacuum to remove adsorbed moisture.

**Hazards:** cobalt and tungsten carbide dust require respirators and wet processing.

### Tungsten Heavy Alloy

- **Composition:** 90-97% tungsten with nickel and iron
- **Density:** about 17-18.5 g/cm³
- **Tensile strength:** about 900-1000 MPa
- **Strengths:** high density with machinability
- **Uses:** counterweights, radiation shielding, kinetic penetrators

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 7-8 MS/m (about 12-14% IACS) for 90W and rising with tungsten content
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 75-100 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 150 J/kg·K
- **Science:** During liquid-phase sintering the molten nickel-iron dissolves the smallest tungsten grains and redeposits that tungsten onto larger ones (Ostwald ripening). The result is rounded tungsten grains floating in a tough matrix.
- **Science:** The iron-nickel binder is ferromagnetic so the alloy is weakly magnetic. Nickel-copper binders give non-magnetic versions for use near MRI machines.
- **Science:** For the same gamma attenuation a tungsten heavy alloy shield can be roughly a quarter to a third thinner than lead.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** tungsten powder (3-5 µm) with nickel and iron powders. Typical grades are 90W-7Ni-3Fe and 95W-3.5Ni-1.5Fe (nickel to iron near 7:3).

1. Blend the powders in a V-blender or ball mill for several hours.
2. Press at about 100-200 MPa in a die or by cold isostatic pressing.
3. Presinter in hydrogen at about 800-1000 °C to remove binder and oxides.
4. Liquid-phase sinter at about 1450-1550 °C in hydrogen for 1-2 hours. The nickel-iron matrix melts and wets the tungsten grains and the part reaches near full density.
5. Cool then vacuum anneal at about 1000-1100 °C and water quench to prevent hydrogen embrittlement.
6. Optionally swage 10-30% cold work to raise strength to about 1.2 GPa.
7. Machine with carbide tooling.

**Vacuum option:**

- Vacuum sinter at about 1450-1550 °C instead of hydrogen. This removes hydrogen embrittlement as a concern and the post-sinter anneal can often be dropped.
- Outgas the pressed compact at about 600-800 °C under vacuum before the liquid-phase hold. Moisture and adsorbed gases leave before they can trap as pores.
- Run the final stress-relief anneal at about 1000-1100 °C in vacuum then water quench.
- Hot isostatic press after sintering at about 1400 °C and 1000-1500 bar argon to close the last porosity.

### Pure Tungsten

- **Density:** 19.25 g/cm³
- **Melting point:** 3422 °C (highest of any metal)
- **Strengths:** extreme heat resistance and low thermal expansion
- **Uses:** filaments, electrodes, furnace parts, rocket nozzles

- **Electrical conductivity:** about 18.9 MS/m (about 32% IACS)
- **Thermal conductivity:** about 173 W/m·K
- **Specific heat:** about 132 J/kg·K
- **Science:** Resistivity rises roughly 14-fold between room temperature and a lamp filament at about 2800 K. A cold incandescent bulb therefore draws around ten times its running current at switch-on.
- **Science:** Tungsten has the highest melting point of any metal (3422 °C) and the lowest vapor pressure along with the lowest thermal expansion of any pure metal at room temperature (about 4.5 × 10⁻⁶/K).
- **Science:** Its ductile-to-brittle transition sits around 200-400 °C so it is brittle at room temperature unless heavily worked. That is why filament wire is drawn warm.

#### Production

**Raw materials:** ammonium paratungstate (APT) from tungsten ore (see Tungsten Carbide above).

1. Calcine APT to tungsten oxide.
2. Reduce in hydrogen at about 600-1000 °C to tungsten powder.
3. Press the powder in steel dies at about 200 MPa into bars.
4. Presinter at about 1200 °C in hydrogen.
5. Sinter by passing high current directly through the bar in hydrogen to about 2800-3000 °C. This gives about 90-95% density.
6. Swage or rotary forge at about 1200-1500 °C.
7. For wire draw through diamond dies at progressively lower temperatures from about 1000 °C down to about 400 °C. Lamp filament wire is doped with potassium to control grain growth.
8. Recrystallization occurs near 1300 °C.
9. Large ingots are made by arc melting or electron beam melting instead of powder metallurgy.

**Vacuum option:**

- Sinter bars in vacuum at about 2000-2400 °C (resistance or induction heated) instead of hydrogen. Oxygen and carbon and volatile impurities leave the metal.
- Electron beam melt at about 10⁻⁵ mbar. Potassium and oxygen and carbon and most metallic impurities evaporate and the ingot reaches 99.99%+ purity with large grain size.
- Vacuum arc melt consumable electrodes for dense ingots.
- Anneal wire and sheet in vacuum at about 1200-1800 °C for recrystallization with no oxide scale. Tungsten oxidizes rapidly in air above about 400 °C.
- Deposit dense tungsten coatings by low-pressure CVD from tungsten hexafluoride and hydrogen at about 500-600 °C.
- Hot press or hot isostatic press powder compacts under vacuum or argon for near-full density.
