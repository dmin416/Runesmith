## Part 2: Shop Tool Chain (Build Order)

Everything below depends on the tier before it. A smith cannot skip tiers without magic or purchase.

### Tier 0: Bare hands and found materials
- Stone anvil or stump anvil
- Charcoal fire pit
- Hand hammer (stone or bought iron)
- Water bucket for quenching

### Tier 1: Basic forge shop
- Forge hearth with clay or brick lining
- Bellows (leather and wood) or a bellows rune
- Anvil (forged wrought iron body with steel face, or bought)
- Hammers (cross peen, straight peen, rounding)
- Tongs (forge-welded or riveted)
- Hot chisels and punches
- Swage blocks and fullers
- Vise (leg vise, forged)
- Files (forged then cut by hand with a chisel and hardened)
- Charcoal supply (a charcoal burner is its own project)

### Tier 2: Precision hand tooling
- Drawplate (hardened steel plate with tapered holes) for making wire
- Grindstone (sandstone wheel on a hand crank or treadle)
- Pole lathe or treadle lathe for wood
- Spoon knife, drawknife, plane, saw, auger
- Sand molds and flasks for casting
- Crucible (clay and graphite or clay and sand)
- Raising hammers and stakes for hollow forms
- Scales (balance beam with stone or brass weights) for calibration

### Tier 3: Water or wind power
- Water wheel (undershot, overshot, or breast wheel) or windmill
- Line shaft with belts (leather) or gears (wood or iron)
- Cam shaft driving a tilt hammer
- Powered grindstone
- Powered bellows
- Powered drawbench for wire

### Tier 4: Machine tools
- Metal lathe (cast iron bed, hand-filed ways, lead screw cut by a hand-made tap and die set)
- Boring bar for cylinders
- Rolling mill (two iron rollers, geared, water driven) for sheet and bar
- Screw press (forged and cut screw, wooden or iron frame)
- Hydraulic press (see Part 4)
- Shaper or planer for flat surfaces

### Magic shortcuts at any tier
- Rotation rune replaces a water wheel or crank (limited by mana cost and runtime)
- Heat rune replaces bellows and fuel for a forge
- Hardening or tempering rune holds an exact temperature
- Shaping spell moves metal without hammering (high mana cost and skill)
- Precision spell for measurement or alignment (replaces a lathe for small jobs)

## Part 3: Production Methods by Item

### Core and Full Minimal Kit

#### 1. 12 inch skillet
- **Material:** Cast iron or carbon steel. Stainless is not available without chromium alloy knowledge or magic. Use steel for plain cookware; for corrosion-resistant faces see Part 9 substitutions (mythril or deep steel).
- **Cast iron method:** Carve a wooden pattern (two halves for a lip). Pack green sand around the pattern in a two-part flask. Remove the pattern and cut a gate and vent. Melt iron in a furnace with a bellows or a heat rune. Pour. Let cool. Break out the sand. Clean the casting with files and a grindstone. Machine the cooking surface smooth on a lathe if available. Season with oil and heat.
- **Forged carbon steel method:** Heat a sheet or billet. Hammer over a stake or into a swage die to raise the sides. Planish (smooth hammering) the interior. Rivet or forge-weld a handle on. Season.
- **Fast version:** A press with a die stamps a flat disc into a pan shape in one stroke.
- **Tools needed:** Forge or furnace, hammers, stakes, lathe (optional), files.
- **Magic assist:** Even-heat enchantment on the base. Non-stick enchantment on the surface.

#### 2. 3 qt saucepan with lid
- **Material:** Copper, iron, or tinned iron.
- **Method:** Cut a disc for the base and a strip for the wall. Raise or roll the wall into a cylinder. Braze or rivet the seam. Attach the base by seaming (folding and hammering the edges together) or by riveting. Roll a rim around a wire for stiffness. Rivet a handle on. For the lid, hammer a disc into a shallow dome over a stake and add a knob.
- **Tools needed:** Snips, stakes, planishing hammer, rivets, brazing flux, solder.
- **Alternate:** Cast iron saucepan using a sand mold.
- **Tinning:** Dip iron in molten tin to prevent rust and food contamination. Tin is a separate supply chain.

#### 3. Stockpot or Dutch oven
- **Dutch oven method (cast):** Make a pattern for the pot and a separate one for a flat lid with a raised rim. Sand cast both. Cast three short legs and a bail handle attachment points. Finish by grinding the lid seating surface so it fits tightly. Season.
- **Stockpot method (raised):** Same as the saucepan but larger. Use thicker sheet. Rivet two loop handles.
- **Scaling note:** A large cast pour needs enough furnace capacity. Otherwise pour in sections or use a water-wheel blower.
- **Magic assist:** Heat distribution rune. Weight-reduction enchantment for transport.

#### 4. Sheet pan
- **Material:** Iron sheet, steel sheet, or aluminum (unlikely without magic).
- **Method (no mill):** Forge a billet flat with a sledge and hand hammers. Repeat heating and hammering until thin. This is slow and uneven.
- **Method (rolling mill):** Pass heated bar through rollers repeatedly. Cut to size with shears. Fold or wire the edges for stiffness. Hammer the corners.
- **Method (press):** Stamp a flat sheet with a rimmed die.
- **Tools needed:** Rolling mill or heavy hammer, shears, brake (folding jig).

#### 5. Chef's knife
- **Material:** High-carbon steel. Wrought iron is too soft. Steel comes from a bloomery with carburizing, crucible steel, or a magic-assisted alloy. For deep steel / mana steel / mythril knife roles see Part 9.
- **Method:**
  1. Forge a billet into a flat bar.
  2. Draw out the blade shape and tang with hammer blows.
  3. Forge in the bevel lines.
  4. Normalize by heating and air cooling three times to relieve stress.
  5. Rough grind on a grindstone.
  6. Harden by heating to critical temperature (non-magnetic) and quenching in oil or water.
  7. Temper in an oven or on a hot plate to relieve brittleness.
  8. Final grind and polish.
  9. Fit a handle (scales riveted to a full tang or a hollow handle epoxied with pitch).
  10. Sharpen on a whetstone.
- **Tools needed:** Forge, anvil, hammers, grindstone, files, whetstone, drill (bow drill or lathe).
- **Magic assist:** Edge-keeping enchantment. Self-sharpening rune. Hardening rune for a perfect quench.

#### 6. Cutting board
- **Material:** Hardwood (maple, beech, oak, walnut).
- **Method:** Mill planks. Joint the edges. Glue strips (hide glue or pine pitch glue) into a panel. Clamp. For end grain boards, cut the panel into strips, rotate 90 degrees, and reglue. Plane flat. Sand or scrape. Rub with oil.
- **Tools needed:** Saw, plane, clamps (forged or wooden screw), glue pot.
- **Alternate:** Single slab cut from a tree round.

#### 7. Large mixing bowl
- **Material:** Wood, copper, or ceramic.
- **Wood method:** Rough out a blank with an axe. Turn on a lathe with hook tools. Hollow the inside and shape the outside. Sand and oil.
- **Copper method:** Raise a disc over a bowl-shaped stake with repeated annealing and hammering. Planish smooth.
- **Ceramic method:** Throw on a wheel, dry, glaze, fire in a kiln. This is a separate trade and may need a potter.
- **Tools needed:** Lathe (pole or powered), hook tools, or raising hammers and stakes.

#### 8. Wooden spoon
- **Method:** Split a billet from green wood. Rough out with an axe. Carve the bowl with a spoon knife or gouge. Refine the handle with a drawknife and knife. Dry slowly. Sand and oil.
- **Tools needed:** Axe, spoon knife, knife.
- **Alternate:** Turn the handle on a lathe and carve the bowl.

#### 9. Tongs
- **Method (riveted):** Forge two matching arms from flat bar. Draw the jaws and flatten the reins. Punch a hole at the pivot. Rivet the arms together. Add a stop if needed.
- **Method (spring tongs):** Forge a single bent bar with a flat spring at the bend. Shape the jaws. Heat treat the spring section.
- **Tools needed:** Forge, hammers, punch, rivet.
- **Note:** Smith tongs and kitchen tongs differ in jaw shape and spring tension.

#### 10. Spatula (metal turner)
- **Method:** Forge a thin flat blade with a tang. Bevel the leading edge. Bend the neck slightly upward. Fit a wooden handle by burning in the tang or riveting scales on.
- **Tools needed:** Forge, anvil, files, grindstone.

#### 11. Measuring cups and spoons
- **Material:** Brass, copper, or tinned iron.
- **Method:**
  1. Decide the volume standard. Calibrate with water and a scale, since one unit of water has a known mass.
  2. Raise or roll a cylinder of the right internal volume. Check by filling with water.
  3. Solder or braze the seam and base.
  4. Add a handle (soldered or riveted).
  5. For spoons, stamp or hammer a small bowl on a swage and add a stem.
- **Tools needed:** Balance beam scale and reference weights, stakes, solder.
- **Key point:** The scale comes first because it defines the units. Without it every cup is a different size.

#### 12. Colander or fine mesh strainer
- **Colander method:** Raise a bowl shape from sheet. Punch holes in a pattern with a hardened punch and a lead or wooden backing block. Deburr. Add a rim and feet.
- **Mesh method:** Draw fine wire through the drawplate. Weave it on a small frame loom. Solder or crimp the weave into a rim hoop. Add a handle.
- **Tools needed:** Punch, drawplate, loom, snips, solder.

### Upgrades

#### 13. Paring knife
- Same process as the chef's knife at smaller scale. A smaller blade quenches more evenly and heat treats more easily, so this is a good first knife for apprentices.

#### 14. Instant-read thermometer
- **No electricity options:**
  - **Bimetallic coil:** Bond two metals with different expansion rates (brass and steel) into a strip. Coil it. The coil twists as temperature changes and turns a needle over a graduated dial. Calibrate against boiling water and ice.
  - **Liquid-in-glass:** Fill a thin glass tube with mercury or colored alcohol. Seal. Mark a scale. Requires a glassblower.
  - **Magic version:** An enchanted gem or rune that glows or displays a number tied to local heat. Easiest to build.
- **Tools needed:** Drawplate, soldering tools, precision lathe, calibration references.

#### 15. Whisk
- **Method:** Draw wire to a consistent thin gauge. Cut equal lengths. Bend each into a loop. Gather the loops and bind the ends into a handle with a wire wrap or a ferrule. Spread the loops into a balloon shape.
- **Tools needed:** Drawplate, snips, pliers, small mandrel.
- **Alternate:** Bundle of birch twigs (traditional, no metalwork).

#### 16. Peeler
- **Method (swivel blade):** Forge a small steel blade with a slot. Make a U-shaped frame from flat bar. Pivot the blade between the arms with a rivet. Add a handle.
- **Method (fixed blade):** Forge a loop of steel with a sharpened inner edge. Add a wooden handle.
- **Tools needed:** Forge, files, whetstone, drill.

#### 17. Box grater
- **Method:**
  1. Roll or hammer thin sheet.
  2. Punch raised teeth by driving a pointed punch from the back with a hammer. Each hit makes one tooth.
  3. Bend the sheet into a four-sided box and rivet or solder the seam.
  4. Add a handle on top.
- **Faster:** A press with a tooth die stamps many teeth in one stroke.
- **Tools needed:** Rolling mill or sheet, pointed punches, backing block, solder.

#### 18. Kitchen scale
- **Balance beam method:** Forge a beam with a central knife-edge pivot hardened and ground. Hang two pans. Make standard weights by casting brass and filing to match a reference mass. Calibrate against the reference.
- **Spring scale method:** Coil a spring from tempered wire and attach it to a dial. Calibrate with weights.
- **Magic version:** Gravity or mass rune with a readout.
- **Tools needed:** Precision files, drawplate, lathe for weights, reference mass (the hard part).
- **Reference mass:** Defining the standard may be a political or guild question in the setting.

#### 19. Blender or immersion blender
- **Hand crank version:**
  1. Cast or turn a cylindrical jar from metal or glass.
  2. Forge and file a blade assembly with two or three angled blades on a shaft.
  3. Mount the shaft through a sealed bearing (leather gasket and bushing) in the base.
  4. Add a gear train (bevel gears) to a hand crank. Gear ratio multiplies blade speed.
- **Treadle or water power:** Connect the shaft to a belt drive.
- **Magic version:** A spinning rune on the shaft. Mana cost scales with load.
- **Tools needed:** Lathe for the shaft and bearings, gear cutting setup (hobbing or hand filing), file work, leather seals.
- **Alternate:** A mortar and pestle or a sieve and a spoon does most of the same work.

## Part 4: Machine Builds the Character Must Make First

### Lathe
- **Wood (pole lathe):** A frame with two centers, a springy pole overhead, a cord wrapped around the work, and a foot treadle. The cord spins the work back and forth. Hand-held tools cut on the forward stroke.
- **Wood (treadle with flywheel):** Add a crank and a heavy flywheel for continuous rotation.
- **Metal:**
  1. Cast or forge a rigid bed. Cast iron is best.
  2. Make a headstock with a hardened spindle and bearings (bronze bushings).
  3. Make a tailstock with a sliding center.
  4. Make a tool rest or a sliding carriage (slide rest).
  5. Cut a lead screw by hand with a file and a guide, then use that screw to cut better screws.
  6. Power from a water wheel through belts or an enchanted rotation rune.
- **Bootstrapping note:** The first lead screw is cut by hand and is inaccurate. Each lathe generation makes a better screw for the next. This is a good story beat (precision compounds).

### Motor (non-electric)
- **Water wheel:** Wooden frame and buckets or paddles. Needs a stream or a millrace. Drives a shaft with gears or belts. Output is steady but site-bound.
- **Windmill:** Sails on a rotating cap. Needs wind. Less steady.
- **Treadle or hand crank:** Portable, low power. Use a flywheel to smooth output.
- **Steam engine:** Boiler, piston, cylinder, valves. Needs bored cylinders (lathe and boring bar), pressure-safe boiler plate (rolling mill and riveting), and good seals. A major leap and a good late-story milestone.
- **Magic motor:** Rotation rune or an elemental (wind or water) bound to a shaft. Mana source decides runtime. A mana crystal battery can store energy.

### Press
- **Screw press:** Cut a large screw and a nut (wood or iron). A lever turns the screw to drive a platen down. Used for stamping, pressing sheet, and gluing.
- **Hydraulic press:**
  1. Bore a thick-walled cylinder (cast iron or forged bronze) on a lathe.
  2. Make a piston with leather cup seals (a soaked and shaped leather ring that seals better as pressure rises).
  3. Make a small pump cylinder and piston with a lever handle.
  4. Connect the two with a pressure pipe. Add check valves (ball and seat).
  5. Fill with water or oil.
  6. Build a heavy frame to contain the force.
  - A small pump piston area against a large ram area multiplies force. Output force equals pressure times ram area.
  - **Hard parts:** Sealing, strong castings, and a frame that does not fail.
- **Magic version:** A force enchantment on a platen or a bound earth elemental pressing down.

### Rolling mill
- Two hardened iron rolls, geared together, with adjustable spacing by screws. Power from a water wheel. Pass heated bar through repeatedly, tightening the gap each time.

### Tilt hammer
- A heavy hammer head on a pivoting arm. A cam on a water wheel shaft lifts the tail and lets it fall. Replaces a striker.

## Part 6: Story Hooks

- The first lathe is rough. Every precision tool made on it inherits the flaw until a better one is built.
- A hydraulic press fails if the seals leak. A leather supplier or a tanner becomes a plot-relevant ally.
- Steel quality varies by region. Crucible steel might be a trade secret.
- Enchantments on cookware (heat, non-stick, self-clean) are luxury goods. A plain iron skillet is a commoner item.
- Standard measures (cup, pound) may be tied to a guild or a ruling house. Calibrating a scale could be a political act.
- A mana-powered motor drains reserves. A water wheel does not. The character chooses between convenience and independence.
- Stainless steel does not exist. A magical alloy that resists rust could be a prize (mythril or deep steel face; see Part 9).
- Glass is a different trade. A thermometer or glass bowl may require a partnership with a glassblower.

## Part 7: How Japanese Makers Get Smooth Cast Iron Pans

Smoothness comes from five stages. The first four are about the casting and finish. The fifth is surface treatment.

1. **Fine mold sand.** Sand is sieved to a very small grain size and mixed with clay binder. Grain size sets the surface texture of the casting. Coarse sand gives the pebbly look of typical Western pans.
2. **Smooth pattern and mold face.** The pattern is polished. The mold face gets a thin wash of fine charcoal or clay slip and is dried or baked before pouring. This keeps molten iron from burning into the sand.
3. **Hot, fluid iron.** Iron is melted very hot with low impurities and poured fast. That lets thin walls fill cleanly with no cold shuts or rough spots.
4. **Machining after casting.** The cooking surface is turned on a lathe and then polished with abrasives. This step does most of the work. Western pans are often sold close to as-cast.
5. **Heat-formed oxide layer.** The pan is fired at high temperature to form a hard black iron oxide skin. Some makers (Nambu ironware, for example) then bake on urushi lacquer or oil. The result is rust-resistant and slick.

**Story notes:**
- Every step works without electricity.
- A water-powered lathe cuts the surface.
- Sandstone and charcoal powder polish it.
- A sieve set controls sand grade.
- Charcoal firing makes the oxide layer.
- A mage could replace the lathe step with a shaping or smoothing spell and keep the rest.

## Part 8: Top Five Pans (With Vacuum Welding)

### Vacuum welding in-world
1. Polish both faces to a mirror finish.
2. Scrub off the oxide layer so bare metal is exposed.
3. Remove the air with a mage's void spell or a piston pump with leather valves.
4. Press the faces together in a screw press.
5. Clean metals bond atom to atom with no solder or heat.
6. Lacquer goes on only after bonding because it cannot survive the process.

See also `Vacuum.md` (Void-Weld).

### 1. Hex-Lattice Clad Iron
- A smooth cast iron body with a corrosion-resistant hex mesh (mythril or deep steel; not stainless) bonded to the cooking face.
- Sear happens on the ridges. Oil pools in the pockets.
- **Strength:** iron heat retention with a scratch-proof hard surface.
- **Weakness:** heavy. The pockets need careful cleaning.

### 2. Ceramic-Pocket Hex Pan
- A hex lattice (mythril or deep steel) bonded to iron with glazed ceramic fired into each cell first.
- The ridges shield the ceramic from spoons and tongs.
- **Strength:** near-nonstick with metal durability.
- **Weakness:** ceramic can chip under hard impact. Fire the ceramic before bonding.

### 3. Urushi-Sealed Nambu Pan
- A fine-sand cast, lathe-turned, mirror-polished iron pan with a fired oxide skin and baked lacquer.
- **Strength:** the smoothest surface here. Rust-resistant and easy to maintain.
- **Weakness:** lacquer dislikes high dry heat and metal utensils.

### 4. Vacuum-Bonded Tri-Ply Pan
- Corrosion-resistant faces (mythril or deep steel), a copper core, and matching face bonded with no solder layers.
- **Strength:** fast and even heat. It will not delaminate like soldered clad.
- **Weakness:** copper is expensive and the bonding step is demanding.

### 5. Rune-Core Iron Pan
- A cast iron pan with an enchanted rune disc vacuum-bonded into the base.
- The rune holds an even temperature or boosts heat spread.
- **Strength:** it fixes hot spots and cuts fuel use.
- **Weakness:** a cracked disc kills the enchantment. It costs mana and skill to repair.
- **Disc metal:** copper is cheap at 80% path efficiency. Mythril or mana steel suits a luxury version. Plain iron discs are the worst host (60%). Prefer steel or better.

### Best overall for the character
- Pan 1 or 2 as a signature piece.
- Pan 5 as a luxury export.
- Pan 3 as the traditional benchmark the others get compared against.

## Part 9: Knife Materials (Runesmith World Metals)

Values come from the world notes. Knife use is the kitchen application of each metal. Stainless and "magic steel" are not on the metal list. Use the substitutions at the end of this part wherever earlier sections mention stainless.

### Mundane (common craft and coin)

| Metal | Role and qualities | Knife use |
|---|---|---|
| **Copper** | Soft craft metal. Rune path efficiency 80%. Coin base (SC/LC). Easy to work. Heats under flow. | Too soft for an edge. Use for rune inlays or ferrules on a handle. |
| **Bronze / brass** | Cast tools, fittings, early gear. Softer than steel. Corrosion-resistant. Path ranking not locked. | Cheap cast or hammered blades. Edge rolls easily. Good for fittings and bolsters. |
| **Tin / lead** | Soft solders, weights, cheap cast. Path ranking open. | No blade use. Solder for handle joins. Tin also coats pans. |
| **Iron** | Baseline weapon and armor stock. Soft versus steel. Rune path worst (60%). Few rune charges before burn-out. Pierce hardness about 700 MPa (2 mm plate). | Starter or peasant knife. Dulls fast. Poor rune host. |
| **Steel** | Hardened blades, armor, tools. Path 85%. Pierce about 1400 MPa (1 mm). Tier-1 default. | Standard chef's knife material. Holds an edge and takes a quench. |
| **Silver / gold** | Coin (SS/LS/SG/LG) and luxury. Path ranking open. Guild and noble trade. | Decorative inlay and handle fittings. Not for edges. |
| **Electrum** | Source alloy name (Au-Ag mix). Appears in late analyze lines. | Luxury trim only. |

### Magical and fantasy (story)

| Metal | Qualities | Knife use |
|---|---|---|
| **Deep iron** | Mana-rich ore. Needs magical blast furnaces. Harder and tougher than plain iron. Mana-resistant. Rune charges about 10x regular iron. Repairs remove less stock. Hard to inscribe without softening fire. Bandit mace and early runic stock. | Tough utility blade. Runes last long but are hard to carve. Needs a softening fire before inscription. |
| **Deep steel** | Deep iron plus the steel process. Tier-2 default. Crude deep steel beats fine normal steel. Runesmiths use it most. Harder to rune than soft steel. Charges last much longer. Slave collars, axes, trial hammers. More common than mythril or orichalcum. | Best working knife for a runesmith. Strong edge with long-lived runes. |
| **Mythril / mithril** | Magically saturated **silver–copper** alloy (near eutectic). **Pearlish** light silvery gold. Superconducting mana path from the saturation. Excellent reusable runic gear. Price floor about 1 large gold for gear. Thin plate often over a form core. Pierce hardness gate about 3500 MPa. Variants: red mythril (fire resist) and plain mythril for prosthetics. Shop: `CraftMetal.md`. **Not titanium.** | Luxury reusable runic knife. Thin plate over a core. Red mythril suits a hearth or forge knife. |
| **Orichalcum** (also Orihalcum) | **Magical titanium** (`CraftMetal.md`): vacuum / inert shop pain, goldish tint. **Reduced** mana conductivity (~titanium η band). Cannot hold runes. Anvil damp / mage-hostile plate and ships. Rarer than deep steel. Unrelated to mythril. | Prestige blade or noble set with **no** enchant path. Anvil inserts. |
| **Adamantium** | **Supersteel**: magically saturated ultra-steel. Top path (~98%). Extreme hardness, edge life, strain life. Prestige and late craft (thermometer sheath and similar). Shop: `CraftMetal.md`. | Legendary heirloom. Best path metal and longest plate life. |
| **Dark steel / mana steel** | Locked on the rune-path ladder (`Energy.md`): dark 88% and mana steel 92%. Better conductors. Less waste. Longer plate life. Not the same axis as deep iron's mana resistance. | Enchanted knives that cast often. Mana steel is the best non-mythril rune host. |
| **Durasteel** | Source forge metal (with fire-mythril) for heavy smelters. | Forge-side tooling rather than kitchen blades. |
| **Arcanite, etherium and aether alloys, meteoric iron, deep aether steel** | Late Source exotic blend names (golem, arm, analyze lists). Not fully rewritten yet. | Reserved. Placeholders for rare or boss-tier items. |

**Supersteel** means **adamantium**: extreme strength / edge life / stiffness at usable weapon weight. Geometry still decides cut versus thrust. Do not use “supersteel” as a nickname for mythril.

**Mythril shop:** magically saturated Ag–Cu (pearlish silvery gold), not titanium. See `CraftMetal.md` Mythril section. Deep steel stays the mid-story workhorse a normal forge can still hit. **Orichalcum** is magical titanium. **Adamantium** is the supersteel.

### Rune-path ladder (rewrite lock)

Worst to best conductor: **iron, copper, steel, dark steel, mana steel, mythril, adamantium**.

Derived / proposal table with real-property modes (wire, window, soft channel) and fantasy-metal cards: `../Runes/ManaMaterials.md`. That file is not a lock over this ladder until you say so.

| Metal | Path efficiency |
|---|---|
| Iron | 60% |
| Copper | 80% |
| Steel | 85% |
| Dark steel | 88% |
| Human body | about 90% (between dark and mana steel) |
| Mana steel | 92% |
| Adamantium | 98% |

Mythril sits between mana steel and adamantium on the ladder. No percentage is locked.

Higher means more useful joules per mana and less waste heat or strain. It also means longer enchanted plate life.

### Two different "good metal" ideas

- **Deep iron and deep steel:** resist mana so runes burn slower and hold more charges. They are harder to carve.
- **Mana steel, mythril, and adamantium (path table):** conduct mana cleanly so each cast wastes less and the metal lasts under heavy flow.

Deep-line metals dominate mid-story gear. Mythril and above is the expensive reusable tier Roland wants for wands and lasting runic kit.
