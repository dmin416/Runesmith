# Medieval Arrowheads

Earth forging reference for physical arrowheads (not runic Fire Arrow scrolls). Modern shortcuts are for Roland’s knowledge / shop path. Magic heat and barrier molds: `CraftMetal.md`. Era: `../World/Technology.md`.

Time and fuel bands are **order-of-magnitude**.

## Medieval process (forged)

1. Heat a bar of iron or steel to a bright orange-yellow.
2. Cut off a slug about 2–3 inches long.
3. Draw out the point and flatten the blade on the anvil.
4. Shape the neck and tang (tapered spike into the shaft) or the socket.
5. For sockets, flatten a wing at the back, wrap it around a tapered mandrel and forge-weld or close the seam.
6. File to shape. Steel heads get heated to critical (non-magnetic), quenched, then tempered to a straw or purple color. Iron heads were often steeled at the tip or case-hardened.

## Arrowhead types

- **Bodkin:** Square or diamond cross-section, no cutting edges. Best against mail and padded armor. Low drag and flies very true.
- **Broadhead (leaf or lozenge):** Flat cutting blade. Used against unarmored targets, horses and for hunting. Cuts a large wound channel but catches air.
- **Barbed:** Swept-back barbs hold in flesh. Hard to remove and adds drag.
- **Swallowtail (forked):** Cuts rope and rigging and does heavy bleeding damage. Erratic flight without good fletching.
- **Round or blunt:** Knocks out small game without ruining the pelt. High drag and short range.
- **Trilobate (three-winged):** A Scythian and Roman bronze design. Strong section with decent penetration.

## Aerodynamics vs durability

- Small, compact heads (bodkin) fly best and are the strongest per gram because the thick section resists bending.
- Wide, flat heads (broadhead) act like a sail. Wind steers the arrow ("planing"), so bigger fletching is needed and accuracy drops past about **40 yards**.
- A heavier head shifts balance forward (front of center). Around **10–15% FOC** is the stable target. Too heavy kills speed and range.
- Thin blades and barbs are the weak points and bend or chip on bone or armor. Iron bends and can be straightened. Hardened steel holds an edge but can chip if left too hard.

## Materials

- **Steel (best):** Earth grades **1075, 1084, 1095** or **5160** (leaf spring) all work. Heat-treat as above. Caldris uses bloomery / crucible stock with the same HT logic (`CraftMetal.md`).
- **Iron or mild steel:** Easy to forge and cheap but soft. Fine for target or hunting. Case-hardening improves tips.
- **Bronze:** Softer than hardened steel but work-hardens with hammering. Corrosion resistant and easy to cast. Best for broad, thick, low-stress designs like the trilobate.

## Modern shortcuts

- **Stock removal:** Cut a shape from 1/8 inch flat bar or spring steel with an angle grinder or hacksaw, file the edges, then heat-treat. Fastest route to a good tanged head.
- **Nail or bolt stock:** Forge a quick bodkin from a large square nail or a bolt with the tip drawn out.
- **Laser or plasma cut blanks:** Ordered then ground and hardened (Earth; Caldris uses barrier-cut / file).
- **Sheet metal:** A socketed broadhead can be rolled from thin sheet around a rod and brazed.

## Casting

- **Bronze:** Cast in two-part molds carved from soapstone or made with sand or lost wax. Historically common. Hammering the edges afterward hardens them noticeably.
- **Iron:** Cast iron is too brittle. Arrowheads shatter on impact so casting and hammering does not work.
- **Steel:** Needs foundry-level temperatures beyond a simple backyard setup without serious furnace work.

## Practical picks (no stainless)

- **Most durable:** Forged or ground 5160 or 1075 bodkin, tempered.
- **Easiest first project:** Stock-removal broadhead from mild steel or spring steel.
- **Best casting route:** Bronze trilobate socketed heads.

## Time and energy (rough)

| Method | Hands-on time per head | Fuel per head | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forged, experienced smith | 5–10 min | 0.3–0.6 kg charcoal | About 40 kcal of human effort |
| Forged, beginner | 30–60 min | ~1 kg charcoal | Most of the time goes to reheats |
| Stock removal (flat bar) | 15–30 min | Near zero | Heat treat 10 heads in one 30 min batch |
| Bronze cast (10–20 head batch) | 3–5 min | 0.2–0.4 kg charcoal | Melt around 1000 °C |
| Steel cast (10–20 head batch) | 10–15 min | 0.3–0.5 kg charcoal or coke | Includes mold work and finishing |

Numbers assume a charcoal or coke fire with forced air. Electricity and propane run lower on fuel and higher on setup cost.

## Optimistic steel casting

### What is needed

- **Steel feed:** High-carbon scrap such as old files, saw blades, 1080–1095 or 5160 leaf spring. Steel near **1.0–1.2%** carbon melts lowest (about **1400–1450 °C**).
- **Furnace:** Insulated charcoal or coke crucible furnace with a blower and tapered tuyere, aiming for **1550–1600 °C**. A propane furnace with preheated air also works.
- **Crucible:** Clay-graphite or silicon carbide rated to **1600 °C** or higher.
- **Cover flux:** Borax or glass to keep the melt from oxidizing.
- **Deoxidizer:** A pinch of aluminum (about **0.1%** by weight) or ferrosilicon so the steel pours without gas porosity.
- **Molds:** Ceramic shell (lost wax) from fireclay or zircon slurry with grog stucco (plaster alone fails at steel temperatures), or machined graphite two-part molds (reusable, fastest cycle).
- **Mold preheat:** Hold molds at **800–900 °C** so thin blades fill without cold shuts.
- **Safety:** Face shield, leather or aluminized apron, pouring shank, tongs, dry molds only.

### Steps

1. Make wax or burnout patterns and attach **8–12** heads to a sprue tree with a thick feeder. Skip for graphite molds.
2. Build the ceramic shell with **4–6** slurry and stucco coats, drying between coats. Melt out the wax and fire the shell to about **900 °C**.
3. Preheat the mold to **800–900 °C** and hold until pour.
4. Charge the crucible with small dense steel pieces and cover with borax. Bring the furnace to about **1550 °C**. A **1–2 kg** melt takes **45–90 min**.
5. Plunge the aluminum pinch into the melt, wait a few seconds, then skim the slag.
6. Pour fast into the hot mold and keep the sprue full through the pour.
7. Let the casting cool slowly in the mold then break out the shell or open the graphite.
8. Cut the heads off the sprue.
9. Normalize by heating to about **900 °C** (non-magnetic) and air cooling. Repeat **2–3** times to refine coarse as-cast grain.
10. Optional cast-then-hammer: forge lightly at bright orange to close porosity and tighten grain. High-carbon cast steel with low sulfur takes this well.
11. Grind or file edges and points.
12. Harden at **800–820 °C**, quench in oil, temper at **200–300 °C**. Bodkins and thick heads suit the higher temper for toughness.

### Yield and time

- One **1 kg** melt makes roughly **20** heads after sprue loss.
- Optimistic yield **70–90%** good heads once dialed in. First runs are lower.
- Hands-on: about **1–2 h** patterns; **2 h** shell (spread over drying days); **2–3 h** melt and pour; **1 h** finishing per **10** heads.
- Graphite mold cycle cuts to roughly **30 s** per pour once the furnace is hot.

### Cast-then-hammer alternative: malleable iron

White cast iron heads can be annealed for a day or more at about **950 °C** in a sealed pot with packing. Carbon migrates and the brittle iron becomes workable. Han dynasty smiths used this route for tools and fittings. The result is tough and can be straightened or lightly hammered like wrought iron.
