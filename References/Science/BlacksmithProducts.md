# Medieval Blacksmith Products

What medieval smiths made and how hard each item was for a skilled craftsman. Difficulty assumes a journeyman or master with a working forge and reliable iron stock. Companion forge metallurgy: `CraftMetal.md`. Machine-tool path: `MedievalIndustrialization.md`. Kitchen kit and cookware chain: `KitchenCraft.md`. Arrowheads detail: `Arrowheads.md`.

Time, fuel and force bands are **order-of-magnitude**.

**Difficulty scale**

- **Easy:** repeatable with little risk of ruin
- **Moderate:** needs practiced technique and judgment
- **Hard:** frequent failures even for skilled hands
- **Very hard:** specialist work where one error wastes days of labor and material

---

## Part 1: Top products

### 1. Nails and hooks (Easy)

Heating a square rod to orange heat and drawing a point on all four sides. The nail is cut off and dropped into a heading tool. Two or three hammer blows form the head.

Difficulty is speed and consistency. A skilled nailer could produce several hundred per day. Hooks add a bend and tapered point.

**Failure modes:** Split heads from cold working and bent shanks from uneven drawing.

Bread-and-butter village work. Later centuries often used specialist nailers.

### 2. Hinges and door hardware (Easy to Moderate)

Strap hinges, pintles, latches, bolts, staples and hasps. Draw a flat strap, split or scroll the end, roll an eye around a mandrel, punch fixing holes hot.

Difficulty is fit between mating parts. Decorative scrollwork on manor doors raises skill a lot.

**Failure modes:** Cold shuts in scrolls, misaligned eyes and thin spots at bends.

### 3. Horseshoes (Moderate)

Bend a bar into a U, punch or stamp nail holes with a fuller groove, fit hot to the hoof.

Smithing is easy; farrier reading of hoof and gait is not. Nervous horses add a non-metal skill.

**Failure modes:** Poor fit causing lameness; holes too close to the edge causing cracking.

Widespread in Europe around the **10th–11th** centuries. Farriers later split as a distinct trade in many towns.

### 4. Agricultural tools (Moderate)

Plowshares, coulters, sickles, scythes, billhooks, spades and felling axes. Most had an iron body with a steel edge welded on.

Forge welding steel to iron cleanly is the main skill. Scythe blades are the hardest here: extremely thin and long, straight, peenable in the field.

**Failure modes:** Failed welds where the steel edge lifts; warping during quench.

Village smiths often repaired more than they made new.

### 5. Chains and rings (Moderate)

Each link bent from rod and forge welded closed. Heavy fortification chain needs a striker.

Weld reliability is everything. A chain is only as strong as its worst link.

**Failure modes:** Cold welds that look sound but part under load.

Mail rings are related specialist work (wire, punch/cut, rivet or weld).

### 6. Knives and eating utensils (Moderate to Hard)

Full steel or iron spine with steel edge. Forge profile and tang, grind, quench and temper, fit wood or bone handle.

Heat treatment is the hard part. Unknown carbon behaves unpredictably. Color judgment takes years.

**Failure modes:** Quench cracks, soft edges and warped blades.

Cutlers often became a separate guild for grinding and finishing.

### 7. Locks and keys (Hard)

Warded padlocks and door locks with fine plates and springs. Keys cut to match wards. Chests used more complex levers.

Precision filing and fitting at small scale. Much work is cold with files after rough forging.

**Failure modes:** Poor tolerances causing sticking; weak springs from bad tempering.

Specialist trade in most large towns.

### 8. Tools for other trades (Hard)

Chisels, gouges, planes, augers, adzes, axes, hammers, saws and masons' picks. Temper must match use.

A chisel too hard chips; too soft rolls. Saws are hardest: thin flat plate, even temper, accurate tooth cutting and setting.

**Failure modes:** Chipped edges, uneven temper and warped plates.

Other tradesmen judge the smith by these tools.

### 9. Armor pieces (Very Hard)

Helmets, gauntlets, greaves, cuirasses and later full plate. Sheet raised over stakes, planished, hardened, polished.

Fit and hardening without warp. Articulated joints need dozens of interlocking parts. Late medieval Italian and German armorers were among Europe’s top metalworkers.

**Failure modes:** Cracking from over-hardening; thin spots from over-hammering.

Full harness took weeks or months in dedicated armorer guilds. General smiths might make simple helmets or repairs.

### 10. Swords (Very Hard)

Long blade with controlled distal taper, hard edge over tough core. Pattern welding and composites combine steels.

Everything at once: taper, high-stakes quench, balance by feel, then hilt fit.

**Failure modes:** Quench cracks, warping, soft spots and delamination in composites.

Often a specialty. Blade forging and hilt fitting sometimes split. Famous centers included Toledo and Solingen.

## Part 2: The smith's own tools

### Tongs (Moderate)

Each work shape needs matching jaws (flat, pick-up, bolt, hollow bit, box). Smiths made most of their own. Riveting reins and joint tension are the key skill.

### Punches, drifts and chisels (Easy to Moderate)

Simple shapes; correct hardening and temper matter. Hot and cold chisels need different tempers. Punches and drifts need taper to withdraw without sticking.

### Hammers (Moderate)

Head forged from steel with a punched eye. Face hardened; eye left tougher. Cross peen, ball peen, straight peen, sledge. Wood handle fitted and wedged.

### Anvil (Very Hard)

Medieval anvils: wrought iron body with a hardened steel face forge-welded on. Large anvils well over **50 kg** need a team and a big fire. Many smiths bought or inherited them. Horn, hardy hole and pritchel hole shaped by forging or hot punching.

### Bellows (Specialist)

Carpentry plus leatherwork. Wooden boards, leather body, valves. Usually commissioned; repaired in place. Larger shops used double-acting designs, later water power.

### Files and rasps (Hard)

Hand-cut teeth into hardened blanks. Even spacing and hardening without warping or dulling the cut is difficult. Valuable; often recut when worn. See also `MedievalIndustrialization.md` (files as machine-tool bottleneck).

## Part 3: Materials and constraints

- **Iron supply was the real limiter.** Most smiths bought bar iron and did not smelt their own. Steel was scarcer and more expensive.
- **Steel-dependent items cost more.** Blades, edge tools and files carried a steel premium beyond labor time.
- **Carbon control was empirical.** Spark tests, fracture appearance and fire behavior.
- **Fuel mattered.** Charcoal for fine work. Coal in some regions; sulfur can harm iron.
- **Specialization grew with town size.** Village smiths did everything. Cities supported locksmiths, cutlers, armorers and farriers.

## Part 5: Time, energy and force estimates

Rough figures for a skilled smith working alone unless noted. **Order of magnitude only.**

### Assumptions

- A hand hammer of **1.2 kg** at about **6 m/s** delivers about **25 J** per blow.
- A striker's sledge of **4–5 kg** delivers about **100–120 J** per blow.
- Heating iron to forging or welding heat (about **1150 °C**) takes about **0.7 MJ/kg** of metal.
- A charcoal forge is only **5–8%** efficient and charcoal holds about **29 MJ/kg**.
- A working fire burns about **2–3 kg** of charcoal per hour. Big fires for anvil work burn more.
- Only **10–25%** of swing energy becomes actual deformation. The rest is rebound and heat.

### Products

| Item | Finished mass | Skilled time | Hammer blows | Swing energy | Charcoal (fuel energy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nail | 15 g | 40 s each | ~15 | 0.4 kJ | 0.02 kg (0.6 MJ) |
| Plain strap hinge | 0.6 kg | 1–2 h | 600–1,000 | 20–35 kJ | 2–4 kg (60–115 MJ) |
| Horseshoe | 0.4 kg | 20–40 min with fitting | 150–250 | 5–9 kJ | 0.7–1.2 kg (20–35 MJ) |
| Felling axe or plowshare | ~2 kg | 3–5 h | 2,500–4,000 | 100–200 kJ | 8–12 kg (230–350 MJ) |
| Chain, 1 m (20 links) | 1.2 kg | 1–1.5 h | 600–900 | 20–35 kJ | 2–3 kg (60–85 MJ) |
| Knife | 0.25 kg | 3–4 h (1–1.5 h forging) | 600–1,000 | 18–30 kJ | 2–3 kg (60–85 MJ) |
| Warded lock | 0.5 kg | 12–30 h (3–4 h at the forge) | 1,000–1,500 | 25–40 kJ | 4–6 kg (115–175 MJ) |
| Chisel or plane iron | 0.25 kg | 2–3 h | 500–800 | 15–28 kJ | 2–3 kg (60–85 MJ) |
| Helmet | 2 kg | 15–40 h | 15,000–40,000 | 225–600 kJ (light blows ~15 J) | 12–20 kg (350–580 MJ) |
| Sword | 1.2 kg (2 kg stock) | 15–35 h (8–20 h forging) | 10,000–20,000 | 400–800 kJ | 30–60 kg (0.9–1.7 GJ) |

| Item | Mass | Skilled time | Hammer blows | Swing energy | Charcoal (fuel energy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pair of tongs | 0.8 kg | 1.5–3 h | 1,200–2,000 | 40–70 kJ | 3–5 kg (90–145 MJ) |

### Force per action (rough peak values)

| Action | Force |
|---|---|
| Hand hammer blow on hot iron | 5–15 kN |
| Sledge blow on hot iron | 25–50 kN |
| Punching a 10 mm hole through 8 mm hot bar | ~15 kN of shear |
| Drifting a hammer eye in 25 mm bar | 50–100 kN equivalent (done in stages) |
| Planishing blow on cold sheet | 1–3 kN |

### Takeaways

- Fuel energy runs about **1,500–4,000×** the mechanical energy of the hammering. The fire is the real cost.
- A smith's metabolic output is about **2 MJ/h**. A sword's **15–35 h** of labor costs **30–70 MJ** of food energy against **0.9–1.7 GJ** of charcoal.
- Pattern welding multiplies sword forging time and fuel by about **2–3×**.
- A full plate harness runs about **500–1,000 h** with roughly **500k–1M** blows and **300–500 kg** of charcoal.
- Charcoal yield is about **15–25%** of wood weight. A sword's **30–60 kg** of charcoal represents **150–400 kg** of wood.
- Striker labor roughly doubles person-hours on heavy jobs (axes, anvils).
