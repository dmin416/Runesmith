# Economy

## Land values

**Core rule:** Land sold for a multiple of its annual rent (about 10 to 20 years' rent in settled regions) and location set the rent. City plots cost far more per acre than any field. Frontier land was nearly free per acre because the real price was labor and risk.

### Established city

- Plots were priced by street frontage rather than acreage. Spots near markets, ports and cathedrals were the scarce asset.
- Illustration: a 1/8-acre tenement renting 5s a year at 12 years' purchase costs about £3. That works out to roughly £24 per acre, while nearby English farmland ran about 5 to 20s per acre in the 13th and 14th centuries.
- Walls capped supply, so plots were subdivided and buildings rose higher.
- Renaissance boom cities such as Amsterdam, Venice and Florence saw plot values follow trade cycles. Urban land held value better than rural land after plague years.

### Frontier town

- Founders gave plots cheaply to attract settlers. English new boroughs and the bastides of France and Wales often charged a fixed burgage rent near 12d a year per plot, with market privileges attached.
- German eastern colonization (12th to 14th centuries) gave settlers a full farm (a Hufe of roughly 15 to 20 hectares) for a low fixed rent. Rent-free years ran from a few to over a dozen.
- Colonial land was even cheaper. William Penn sold 5,000 acres for £100 in 1681 (under 5d per acre) with a token quit rent. Virginia's headright system granted 50 acres per person whose passage was paid.

### Why the gap

- Frontier land was plentiful while labor was scarce. Clearing, building and defense cost more than the ground itself.
- Values jumped once a town gained walls, a market charter and a mill. Early buyers profited most when the settlement matured.
- Cities sold legal security and market access, which made every square foot expensive.

Sources: general economic history (Postan on English land values; Ostsiedlung and bastide studies; Penn's 1681 land terms).

## Beds and wages (14th century England anchors)

Earth research for pricing inn beds, mattress quality and household furniture against Caldris coin later. Keep as anchors; map to small/large copper and silver when a scene needs it.

### Wage anchor

- Unskilled laborer: roughly **1–2 pence** a day (haymaking capped at **1d**, reaping **2d**).
- Carpenter: about **4d** a day. Mason: about **5d**.
- Knight: about **4 shillings (48d)** a day.
- Prices stayed close to flat through most of the medieval period then climbed sharply after **1500** (Price Revolution). Inflation was near zero for much of the medieval span until then.

### Frame (inventory values)

- Truckle / trundle bed: **8–10 shillings** (about 10s for a yeoman; about 8s for a poorer man who owned little else).
- Standing bed with a matching trundle: about **20 shillings**.
- Plain joined four-poster with canopy and curtains (yeoman level): about **£2 10s** (one 1673 inventory: £2 11s 4d).
- Rail bedstead, yeoman quality: **£4**.
- Gentry-level pair of bedchamber beds: **£16** combined.
- Best noble bed with full curtains and valance: over **£20** (record piece with joint frame, curtains and valance: £20 12s 3d).

### Mattress and stuffing (inventory values)

- Chaff or flock mattress with basic coverlets: **6s 8d to 13 shillings** (mattress with three coverlets, a blanket and a pillow at 6s 8d; bolster and two mattresses together at 13s).
- Single featherbed alone: **10 to 32 shillings** by quality and date (10s in a 1637 inventory; featherbed with pillowbere, sheets, blankets and coverlets at 32s 4d).
- Two featherbeds together: **50 shillings**.
- Top-tier down bed over straw: **£8 10s** (down bed with bolster).

### Bedding

- Pillow: **1 penny** (1457).
- Linen sheets: several shillings a pair, commonly **4 shillings**.
- Wool blankets: roughly **6s 8d to 10 shillings** a pair (two pairs of woollen blankets valued together at 13s 4d).
- Towel: **6 pence** (mid-13th century).

### Hangings

- Plain fabric hangings around a chamber: **40 shillings** (green saye about the chamber).
- Damask tester with silk sarsnet curtains: **£5** (blue damask tester with blue sarsnet curtains).

### Everyday furniture for scale

- Chair, plain: **3 pence** (1457). Cloth-covered: **6s 8d** (green cloth).
- Table, plain: **6 pence** (about twice a plain chair). With trestles: up to **10 shillings**.
- Bucket: **6 pence** (1457).

### Renting a bed for the night

- Shared bed at a London inn: **1 penny per head** per night (more than one traveler might share).

### Scale notes

- For a peasant, a good featherbed at **10 shillings** is about a month's wages.
- For gentry, a **£20** canopied bed is years of a laborer's income. One bed was often the single most valuable object in a household inventory.

### How beds were built

**Frame**

- Jointed hardwood or softwood: oak for high-status pieces; ash, beech, elm or pine as common alternatives.
- Rope beds were the standard. Holes in the side rails held a criss-cross lacing of hemp or flax rope pulled tight with a bed key. Slats replaced rope in some designs.
- Poor households often had no frame. They slept on a pallet on the floor or on a bench by the hearth.
- Servants and apprentices used truckle (trundle) beds that slid under the master's bed.

**Mattress**

A sack of tightly woven linen or canvas ticking, stuffed with local materials.

- Poor: straw, chaff, hay, rushes, bracken or heather.
- Middle class: wool flock (shearing scraps) or straw topped with a wool layer.
- Wealthy: a feather or down bed (goose or duck) laid over a straw palliasse. Horsehair stuffing appeared later.
- Aromatic herbs such as lavender, wormwood, rosemary or bedstraw mixed into the stuffing to repel fleas and mask smells.

**Bedding**

- Linen sheets for those who could afford them; coarser hemp for those who could not.
- Wool blankets with fur or sheepskin coverlets for warmth.
- Pillows and bolsters stuffed with feathers or wool.

**Hangings**

- Wealthy beds had a wooden canopy (tester) with curtains of wool or heavy linen. Silk or velvet was reserved for nobility.
- Curtains cut drafts and gave privacy in shared halls.

**On campaign**

- Soldiers slept in bedrolls of wool blankets and cloaks laid over straw or bare ground.
- Officer tents might include a folding wooden cot with a small straw or wool pad.
