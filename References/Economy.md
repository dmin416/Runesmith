## 15. Land & Property

**Core rule:** land sells for 10-20 years' annual rent in settled regions. Location sets the rent; city plots price by frontage, not acreage, and run far higher per acre than any field. Frontier land is nearly free per acre because the real cost is labor and risk, not ground.

| Item | Historical price | LC value |
|---|---|---|
| Farmland, annual rent (per acre) | 5-20s/yr | 300-1,200 LC/yr |
| Farmland, freehold purchase (per acre, 10-20 years' rent) | £2.50-£20 | 3,000-24,000 LC |
| City tenement plot (small, by frontage) | ~£24/acre-equivalent | ~28,800 LC/acre-equivalent |
| Frontier plot (founder's price, cheap to attract settlers) | under 5d/acre | under 25 LC/acre |
| Cottage (small house, purchase) | £2 | 2,400 LC (2.4 SG) |
| Craftsman's house (annual rent) | 20s | 1,200 LC/yr |
| Wealthy merchant's house (annual rent) | £2-3 | 2,400-3,600 LC/yr |

Values jump once a settlement gets walls, a market charter, and a mill; early buyers in a growing town profit most as it matures.

### Historical color (anchors, not Caldris law)

**Established city:** plots priced by street frontage. Spots near markets, ports and cathedrals were the scarce asset. Illustration: a 1/8-acre tenement renting 5s a year at 12 years' purchase costs about £3 (~£24/acre), while nearby English farmland ran about 5 to 20s per acre in the 13th and 14th centuries. Walls capped supply so plots subdivided and buildings rose higher. Renaissance boom cities (Amsterdam, Venice, Florence) saw plot values follow trade cycles; urban land held value better than rural land after plague years.

**Frontier town:** founders gave plots cheaply to attract settlers. English new boroughs and French/Welsh bastides often charged a fixed burgage rent near 12d a year per plot with market privileges. German eastern colonization (12th to 14th centuries) gave a full farm (Hufe, ~15-20 hectares) for a low fixed rent; rent-free years ran from a few to over a dozen. Colonial land was cheaper still: William Penn sold 5,000 acres for £100 in 1681 (under 5d/acre) with a token quit rent; Virginia's headright granted 50 acres per person whose passage was paid.

**Why the gap:** frontier land was plentiful while labor was scarce. Clearing, building and defense cost more than the ground. Cities sold legal security and market access.

Sources: Postan on English land values; Ostsiedlung and bastide studies; Penn's 1681 land terms.

### Runic repairs

Simple runic weapon or armor fix (restore pathways, not a full rewrite): **~3 LS** fair rate. Full re-craft or rare materials sit much higher.

### Merchant upcharge

Any greedy merchant can upcharge. Fair rates above assume a known customer in a town with competition. Outsiders, kids in torn gear, dungeon-gate stalls, or one-smith towns commonly ask **+25% to 2x**. Worse gouging is allowed when the plot needs it. Buyers can haggle, walk, or wait for a friendlier shop.

## 23. Mana Stones (monster cores)

First seen: Chapter 7. Gem-like crystals that form inside some monsters. Used as crafting / fuel ingredients (mana-stone trains, later runic slots). Goblins have a **low** drop rate (~1 in 5); estate training kill yields a **rice-grain** stone. Servants at Arden claimed cores as Baron property; Roland starts pocketing them. Mana Sense can detect a stone in a corpse without cutting. Evolved monsters (one+ evolution) always have a core; more evolutions -> larger/denser. Size is not always quality; stones can rate lesser->legendary like runes. Chapter 11: dungeon beasts drop stones more often and larger (spiked-boar ~goblin-leader size); floor density is the stated reason. Value tracks physical size (mana density), not rarity or danger.

## 25. Beds & Furniture (historical anchors)

### Wage scale note

### How beds were built (quick)

- **Frame:** jointed hardwood or softwood (oak high-status; ash, beech, elm or pine common). Rope beds standard (hemp/flax laced through side-rail holes, tightened with a bed key); some used slats. Poor households often had no frame (pallet on floor or bench by the hearth). Servants/apprentices used truckle beds that slid under the master's bed.
- **Mattress:** linen or canvas ticking stuffed with straw/chaff/hay/rushes (poor), wool flock or straw topped with wool (middle), feather/down over straw palliasse (wealthy). Herbs (lavender, wormwood, rosemary, bedstraw) mixed in to repel fleas and mask smells.
- **Bedding:** linen sheets when affordable, coarser hemp otherwise; wool blankets; fur or sheepskin coverlets; pillows/bolsters of feathers or wool.
- **Hangings:** wealthy beds had a wooden canopy (tester) with wool or heavy linen curtains; silk/velvet for nobility. Curtains cut drafts and gave privacy in shared halls.
- **On campaign:** bedrolls of wool blankets and cloaks over straw or ground; officer tents might include a folding cot with a small straw or wool pad.

Prices stayed close to flat through most of the medieval period then climbed sharply after **1500** (Price Revolution). Inflation was near zero for much of the medieval span until then.

## 28. Simulation Cheatsheet

**Peasant / commoner year:** use **~4 LS/month** income and §27 food split. Do **not** put them in inns every night. Latrine wage alone is poverty; assume farm share, spouse work, or town trade.

**Early adventurer month:** lodging **270 LC** monthly (10% off) or camp; food 100-200 LC if cooking/trail; ears+stones as variable income; mundane repair **~40-80 LC** light / **1-2 LS** heavy (merchants may upcharge).

**Break-even goblin day (no stone):** 7 ears = 35 LC = 7 inn meals or 3.5 lodging nights. **One rice-grain stone (20 LC)** = 4 ears. Cores still help; ears alone can cover basic room+meal if volume is high.

**Craft runway:** **10 SG** mats ~ **6 months** frugal research (canon). Plan **~1.5-2 SG/month** mat burn while learning.

**Big life purchase:** home/workshop **~30 SG**; ship move **5 LS** + food; keep **9 SG** in reserve for a bad curse heal.
