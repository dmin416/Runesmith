# Economy

## Land values

**Core rule:** Land sold for a multiple of its annual rent (about 10 to 20 years' rent in settled regions) and location set the rent. City plots cost far more per acre than any field. Frontier land was nearly free per acre because the real price was labor and risk.

### Established city

- Plots were priced by street frontage rather than acreage. Spots near markets, ports and cathedrals were the scarce asset.
- Illustration: a 1/8-acre tenement renting 5s a year at 12 years' purchase costs about £3. That works out to roughly £24 per acre, while nearby English farmland ran about 5 to 20s per acre in the 13th and 14th centuries.
- Walls capped supply, so plots were subdivided and buildings rose higher.
- Renaissance boom cities such as Amsterdam, Venice and Florence saw plot values follow trade cycles. Urban land held value better than rural land after plague years.

### Frontier town

- Founders gave plots cheaply to attract settlers. English new boroughs and the bastides of France and Wales often charged a fixed burgage rent near 12d a year per plot, with market privileges attached.
- German eastern colonization (12th to 14th centuries) gave settlers a full farm (a Hufe of roughly 15 to 20 hectares) for a low fixed rent. Rent-free years ran from a few to over a dozen.
- Colonial land was even cheaper. William Penn sold 5,000 acres for £100 in 1681 (under 5d per acre) with a token quit rent. Virginia's headright system granted 50 acres per person whose passage was paid.

### Why the gap

- Frontier land was plentiful while labor was scarce. Clearing, building and defense cost more than the ground itself.
- Values jumped once a town gained walls, a market charter and a mill. Early buyers profited most when the settlement matured.
- Cities sold legal security and market access, which made every square foot expensive.

Sources: general economic history (Postan on English land values; Ostsiedlung and bastide studies; Penn's 1681 land terms).

### Spatial / storage bags (Chapter 11)

Enchanted bags with internal storage space. Becky's party bag is slightly under **2 m³**; bought used from a retiring adventurer for **5 small gold** (a steal). Typical price for that size is **7–10 small gold**; larger bags can cost about **10×**. House Arden servants of higher status carried similar bags. Dungeon materials (tusks, spikes, leather, herbs) go in the bag; monster meat is usually left (tough, bad taste). Needle Worm spikes sell poorly (Chapter 12). Chapter 15: living beings cannot enter; **dead** bodies can (watcher stuffed in; dumped to floor-3 Wereboars). Watcher's bag goes to the girls with the armor sale.

Hangover potions exist in Carwen (Chapter 12); Roland skips them to save coin. Chapter 14: low-grade recovery potions (drink and pour on wounds); slow tick; often still need bandages.

Chapter 17 assassin loot: gear + spatial bag sell for **over 20 small gold** total; Roland already held ~**15 SG** from prior sales; girls receive **+5 SG**; Reyna keeps throwing daggers. No red-seal letters in the bag. Chapter 18 parting gift: party's smaller spatial bag with starter coin (they keep the watcher's bag, about twice as large). Chapter 19: bag holds **10 small gold** (more than the 5 he wanted of their ~20).

## Mana stones (monster cores)

First seen: Chapter 7
Gem-like crystals that form inside some monsters. Used as crafting / fuel ingredients (mana-stone trains, later runic Slots). Goblins have a **low** drop rate; estate training kill yields a **rice-grain** stone. Servants at Arden claimed cores as Baron property; Roland starts pocketing them. Chapter 9 Carwen sale: tiny goblin stones fetch **4 small silver** each. Mana Sense can detect a stone in a corpse without cutting. Chapter 11 dungeon beasts drop stones more often and larger (spiked-boar stone ~goblin-leader size); density in dungeon floors is the stated reason. Chapter 24: Helci's mountain-goblin stone is worth a few small silvers.
