## 18. Glass-Ceramics

### 18.1 What They Are

Glass-ceramics start as glass, then are reheated under controlled conditions so that crystals nucleate and grow inside. The result is typically 30 to 99 percent crystalline with a residual glass phase.

Discovered by **S. Donald Stookey** at Corning in 1953 when a furnace malfunctioned and overheated a photosensitive lithium silicate glass to about 900 C instead of 600 C. The sample turned opaque white and did not shatter when dropped. This led to **Pyroceram** and **CorningWare**.

### 18.2 Two-Step Heat Treatment

1. **Nucleation**: hold near or slightly above Tg (often 50 to 100 C above) so many tiny nuclei form. Nucleating agents: TiO2, ZrO2, P2O5, metals (Au, Ag, Pt, Cu).
2. **Crystal growth**: heat higher so crystals grow on the nuclei.

Nucleation rate and growth rate peak at different temperatures (nucleation lower, growth higher), which is why two separate steps give fine, uniform microstructures.

### 18.3 Important Systems

| System | Crystal phase | Key property | Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li2O-Al2O3-SiO2 (LAS) | β-quartz solid solution | Near-zero expansion, transparent | Ceran and similar cooktops, Zerodur telescope mirrors (CTE about 0 ± 0.007×10^-6/K), fireplace windows |
| LAS | β-spodumene | Low expansion, opaque | CorningWare, missile radomes |
| MgO-Al2O3-SiO2 | Cordierite | Low expansion, radar transparent | Radomes |
| Li2O-SiO2 | Lithium disilicate | High strength, translucency | Dental crowns, bridges |
| Fluormica | Interlocking mica plates | Machinable | Macor |
| Ca-P-Si | Apatite-wollastonite | Bioactive | Bone implants |
| Nepheline-based | Nepheline + ion exchange | Very high strength | Some cookware, armor |

### 18.4 Why Glass-Ceramics Are Tougher

- Crystals deflect cracks and make them follow tortuous paths.
- Interlocking elongated crystals (lithium disilicate, mica) bridge cracks.
- Residual stresses from thermal expansion mismatch between crystals and glass can close cracks.
- Fracture toughness rises from about 0.75 for glass to about 1.5 to 3.5 MPa·m^0.5 for many glass-ceramics.

### 18.5 Transparency

Glass-ceramics stay transparent when crystals are much smaller than the wavelength of light (well under about 50 nm) and/or when crystal and glass refractive indices match closely.

**Transformation toughening in zirconia**: stabilized tetragonal zirconia grains near a crack tip transform to the monoclinic phase, expanding about 3 to 5 percent in volume. This puts the crack tip in compression and resists propagation. It is the ceramic equivalent of a surface-compression strategy, triggered locally by the crack itself. Prolonged exposure to moisture at moderate temperatures can trigger the transformation spontaneously at surfaces ("low-temperature degradation" or aging).

**Ceramic glazes use compression too**: a glaze with slightly lower thermal expansion than its clay body ends up in compression after firing, making ware stronger. A glaze with higher expansion ends up in tension and **crazes** (a network of fine cracks). Too much compression causes **shivering** (glaze flaking off edges).

**Porcelain-fused-to-metal crowns** rely on the porcelain having slightly lower expansion than the metal so that the porcelain ends up in mild compression.

### 19.3 Metallic Glasses

- Metals cooled fast enough to skip crystallization.
- First reported by Pol Duwez (Caltech, 1960) for Au-Si alloy cooled at about 10^6 K/s.
- **Bulk metallic glasses** (1990s onward) have critical cooling rates as low as about 1 to 10 K/s, allowing centimeter-thick parts. Example: Vitreloy 1 (Zr41.2 Ti13.8 Cu12.5 Ni10 Be22.5).
- Properties: yield strength about 1.5 to 2 GPa for Zr-based alloys (higher for Fe- and Co-based, up to about 5 GPa), elastic limit about 2 percent (several times that of crystalline metals), fracture toughness widely variable from about 20 to over 100 MPa·m^0.5 depending on alloy.
- Lack ductility in tension because deformation localizes into shear bands.
- Used in golf club faces, phone parts, transformer cores (Fe-based amorphous ribbon cuts core losses sharply) and precision gears.

### 19.4 Other Glass Types

- **Chalcogenide glasses** (As2S3, GeSbTe, Ge-As-Se): infrared transmission; phase-change memory and rewritable optical discs switch between glassy and crystalline states.
- **Fluoride glasses** (ZBLAN): very low phonon energy, mid-infrared transmission, fiber lasers.
- **Phosphate glasses**: laser glass hosts (Nd-doped for fusion lasers like the National Ignition Facility), bioresorbable implants.
- **Oxynitride glasses**: nitrogen replaces some oxygen, raising stiffness, hardness and Tg.
- **Bioactive glass (Bioglass 45S5)**: invented by Larry Hench (1969); bonds to bone by forming a hydroxycarbonate apatite layer.
- **Polymer glasses**: PMMA, polystyrene and polycarbonate are glasses in the scientific sense (amorphous with a Tg).
- **Water as glass**: amorphous ice forms when water is cooled extremely fast; much of the water ice in interstellar space is amorphous.
- **Sugar glass**: cooled sugar syrup, used in film stunt props because it is weak and breaks into blunt pieces.

### 19.5 Steel Tempering vs Glass Tempering

Same word, opposite meaning:
- **Steel**: quenching hardens it (forms brittle martensite); tempering is a **reheating** step (about 150 to 650 C) that reduces brittleness and relieves stress at the cost of some hardness. Temper colors on steel (straw, bronze, blue) come from oxide film thickness.
- **Glass**: tempering is the **quench itself**, which deliberately locks in stress for strength.

## 20. Natural and Biological Glasses

### 20.1 Natural Glasses

| Glass | Origin | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Obsidian** | Rapidly cooled rhyolitic lava | Fracture edges as thin as about 3 nm; obsidian scalpels have been used in some surgeries |
| **Pumice** | Frothy volcanic glass | Floats in water |
| **Pele's hair and Pele's tears** | Lava spray drawn into fibers and droplets | Natural glass fibers and drops |
| **Fulgurites** | Lightning striking sand or rock | Hollow glassy tubes |
| **Tektites (moldavite, australites)** | Melt ejected by meteorite impacts | Aerodynamic shapes from re-entry |
| **Libyan Desert Glass** | Impact or airburst about 29 million years ago | Nearly pure silica; a carved piece sits in Tutankhamun's scarab pectoral |
| **Trinitite** | First nuclear test, Trinity site, 1945 | Green glass from melted desert sand |
| **Impact glasses (suevite)** | Meteorite craters | |
| **Lechatelierite** | Pure silica glass from lightning or impacts | |

### 20.2 Biological Glass

- **Glass sponges (Hexactinellida)**, such as Venus' flower basket (Euplectella aspergillum), build skeletons from amorphous silica spicules.
- The spicules are layered: concentric silica shells separated by thin organic layers. Cracks deflect at each interface, giving much higher effective toughness than monolithic glass.
- Some deep-sea sponge spicules transmit light like optical fibers.
- **Diatoms** build intricate silica shells (frustules); diatomaceous earth is made from their remains.
- **Nacre** (mother of pearl) is not glass but follows a similar design principle: brittle aragonite tablets glued by thin organic layers, about 95 percent mineral, with toughness several times that of pure aragonite.
- These structures inspire laminated and "brick and mortar" glass composites, including laser-engraved glass with designed crack paths that has been shown to raise impact toughness substantially.

## 22. Science Facts Collection

### Structure and Nature
1. Glass has no sharp melting point; it softens gradually over hundreds of degrees.
2. The glass transition temperature shifts by roughly 3 to 5 C for each tenfold change in cooling rate in typical oxide glasses.
3. Almost any liquid can form a glass if cooled fast enough. Pure metals need cooling rates over about 10^12 K/s; silica needs well under 1 K/s.
4. Window glass at room temperature has a relaxation time far longer than the age of the universe.
5. Quartz (crystalline SiO2) and fused silica have the same composition but quartz is denser (2.65 vs 2.20 g/cm3).
6. Fused silica shows anomalous behavior: its stiffness rises with temperature; its expansion is nearly zero.
7. Water has a density maximum; silica glass has similar anomalies, including a minimum in compressibility around 1,000 C.
8. The "boron anomaly": adding alkali to B2O3 first converts trigonal boron to tetrahedral boron (stiffening the network) before eventually creating non-bridging oxygens.
9. The "mixed alkali effect": glass containing two alkalis (say Na and K) has much lower ionic conductivity than glass with either one alone at the same total alkali content.

### Strength and Fracture
10. A clean, freshly drawn silica fiber is stronger per unit weight than steel wire.
11. Real window glass is about 100 to 300 times weaker than its theoretical strength because of microscopic surface flaws.
12. Breathing on glass can weaken it measurably during a test, because water vapor speeds crack growth.
13. Glass under constant load can break days, months or years later from slow crack growth.
14. Glass compresses elastically more than many people expect: a 1 GPa hydrostatic pressure shrinks soda-lime glass by about 2 to 3 percent.
15. At very high pressures (over about 10 GPa for silica) glass permanently densifies by about 20 percent.
16. Glass can deform plastically under a sharp indenter. Vickers indents show permanent impressions through densification and shear flow.
17. Crack speed in glass tops out around 1,500 m/s in soda-lime.
18. The fracture mirror radius tells the failure stress, used in forensic investigations.
19. Bullet holes in glass have a cone-shaped crater (Hertzian cone) widening on the exit side, revealing the direction of fire.
20. Concentric and radial cracks around an impact show which side the force came from (radial cracks start on the side opposite the impact; the "3R rule" in forensics: radial cracks form right angles on the reverse side).
21. Glass breaking under a single sharp sound requires the sound frequency to match the glass resonance and high amplitude (typically over about 100 dB at the resonance); it has been demonstrated with amplified tones on lead crystal wine glasses.
22. A glass's "ring" when struck reveals its low internal friction; lead crystal rings longer than soda-lime.

### Tempering
23. A tempered pane holds enough stored elastic energy to break into thousands of pieces.
24. Surface compression in tempered glass is about twice the central tension.
25. The compressive layer in thermally tempered glass is about 21 percent of the thickness on each side.
26. Ion-exchanged glass can carry surface compression over 1 GPa, roughly ten times commercial thermal tempering.
27. Prince Rupert's drops reach surface compression around 400 to 700 MPa.
28. Polarized sunglasses reveal the quench pattern in car rear windows and tempered building glass.
29. Tempered glass cannot be cut, drilled or edged after tempering without shattering.
30. Heating tempered glass above about 300 C for a long time relaxes the stress and gradually un-tempers it; above about 450 to 500 C the stress relaxes substantially within minutes to hours.
31. Fire testing shows tempered glass resists fire-induced cracking much longer than annealed glass; stress relaxation eventually weakens it.
32. Tempering does not change hardness, stiffness, density (significantly), chemical resistance or scratch resistance. It only adds built-in stress.

### Thermal Behavior
33. Ordinary glass cracks from a temperature difference of about 40 to 60 C across a pane; borosilicate tolerates about three times that.
34. Low-expansion glass-ceramic cooktops can be heated red hot on one spot and splashed with cold water without cracking.
35. Glass conducts heat about 50 times worse than steel and about 400 times worse than copper.
36. Hot glass glows only at temperatures over about 500 C, so hot glass can look cold; burns from invisible hot glass are common in workshops.

### Optics
37. Light travels about 33 percent slower in typical glass than in vacuum (n ≈ 1.5).
38. Each uncoated glass surface reflects about 4 percent of light at normal incidence.
39. Modern optical fiber loses only about 0.15 to 0.2 dB per kilometer near 1,550 nm; light can travel about 100 km before losing 99 percent of its power.
40. The 200-inch Palomar telescope mirror (1934 pour) is borosilicate glass and took about 10 months to anneal.
41. Uranium glass fluoresces green under UV light; manganese-containing glass turns purple in sunlight over years (solarization, "sun-purpled glass").

### Industry and Use
42. A single float glass line runs continuously for 15 to 20 years without stopping.
43. Fire-flat float glass made by molten tin replaced ground and polished plate glass worldwide within about 20 years.
44. Phone cover glass is ion-exchanged aluminosilicate typically 0.4 to 1 mm thick.
45. Foldable phone displays use ultra-thin glass about 30 to 100 µm thick that can bend to a radius of a few millimeters because thin glass has very low bending stress for a given curvature.
46. Bending stress at the surface of a bent glass sheet is σ = E × t / (2R): halving thickness halves the stress for a given bend radius.
47. Deep-sea flotation spheres made of borosilicate glass survive depths over 6,000 m because glass is so strong in compression.
48. Glass is one of the most chemically durable common materials. It slowly dissolves in strong alkali and in hydrofluoric acid.
49. Window glass reacts with water over time; silica-rich gel layers form on old glass, causing iridescence on ancient Roman glass.
50. US consumer "Pyrex" products sold since the late 1990s are mostly tempered soda-lime, while European "PYREX" ware remains borosilicate; the two behave differently under thermal shock.

## 23. Worked Calculations

### 23.1 Strength From Flaw Size

Soda-lime glass, K_Ic = 0.75 MPa·m^0.5, edge crack a = 20 µm, Y = 1.12:

```
σ_f = K_Ic / (Y × sqrt(π × a))
    = 0.75 / (1.12 × sqrt(3.1416 × 20×10^-6))
    = 0.75 / (1.12 × 0.007927)
    = 0.75 / 0.008878
    ≈ 84.5 MPa
```

### 23.2 Benefit of Surface Compression

Same flaw in tempered glass with 100 MPa surface compression. Assuming the flaw is shallow compared with the compressive layer, the applied stress must first overcome the compression:

```
σ_applied at failure ≈ 84.5 + 100 = 184.5 MPa
```

A 2.2 times strength increase for this flaw. Smaller flaws gain proportionally less; larger flaws (still inside the compression layer) gain proportionally more. This is why tempering helps most for typical handling damage.

### 23.3 Tempering Temperature Difference

Desired surface compression 120 MPa in soda-lime (instant freeze model):

```
σ_s = (2/3) × E × α × ΔT / (1 - ν)
ΔT  = 1.5 × σ_s × (1 - ν) / (E × α)
    = 1.5 × 120 × 0.78 / (70,000 × 9×10^-6)
    = 140.4 / 0.63
    ≈ 223 K
```

The center must be about 220 C hotter than the surfaces when the glass passes through the freezing range.

### 23.4 Heat Flux Needed (6 mm glass)

Half-thickness L = 3 mm, k ≈ 1.2 W/m·K at high temperature, target ΔT ≈ 190 K:

```
q = 2 × k × ΔT / L = 2 × 1.2 × 190 / 0.003 ≈ 152,000 W/m²
```

With surface about 600 C and air 30 C (difference 570 K):

```
h ≈ 152,000 / 570 ≈ 267 W/m²·K
```

### 23.5 Thermal Shock Limit

Borosilicate, σ_f = 40 MPa, E = 64 GPa, α = 3.3×10^-6/K, ν = 0.2:

```
ΔT_c = 40 × 0.8 / (64,000 × 3.3×10^-6) = 32 / 0.2112 ≈ 152 C
```

### 23.6 Load Duration

Annealed glass, n = 16. Short-term (3 s) strength 45 MPa. Strength for a 50-year sustained load (1.58×10^9 s):

```
σ_50yr = 45 × (3 / 1.58×10^9)^(1/16)
       = 45 × (1.9×10^-9)^(0.0625)
       = 45 × 0.285
       ≈ 12.8 MPa
```

### 23.7 Annealing Stress From Cooling Rate

10 mm soda-lime slab cooled through the annealing range at 2 C/min (0.0333 K/s), κ = 5×10^-7 m²/s:

```
ΔT = r × t² / (8 × κ) = 0.0333 × (0.01)² / (4×10^-6) ≈ 0.83 K
σ_s ≈ (2/3) × 0.81 MPa/K × 0.83 K ≈ 0.45 MPa
```

Well-annealed by structural standards (residual stress under about 1 MPa).

### 23.8 Weibull Size Effect

Test coupon (ring-on-ring loaded area 0.001 m²) characteristic strength 120 MPa, m = 6. Façade pane with 2 m² stressed area:

```
σ_pane = 120 × (0.001 / 2)^(1/6) = 120 × (5×10^-4)^(0.1667) = 120 × 0.282 ≈ 34 MPa
```

### 23.9 Ion Exchange Depth

D = 5×10^-15 m²/s, target DOL = 40 µm, using DOL ≈ 1.4 × sqrt(D × t) as a rough rule:

```
t = (DOL / 1.4)² / D = (40×10^-6 / 1.4)² / 5×10^-15
  = (2.857×10^-5)² / 5×10^-15
  = 8.16×10^-10 / 5×10^-15
  ≈ 163,000 s ≈ 45 hours
```

Real aluminosilicate cover glasses reach this depth much faster because their diffusivity at 410 to 450 C is higher, around 10^-14 m²/s or more. The calculation shows why diffusivity is the single most important composition target for ion-exchange glass.

### 23.10 Stored Energy in Tempered Glass

10 mm soda-lime, central tension 45 MPa:

```
U/A = 0.8 × (1 - ν) × σ_CT² × t / E
    = 0.8 × 0.78 × (45×10^6)² × 0.01 / (70×10^9)
    = 0.624 × 2.025×10^15 × 0.01 / 7×10^10
    ≈ 180 J/m²
```

A 1 m × 2 m door stores about 360 J of elastic energy, roughly the kinetic energy of a 1 kg mass moving at 27 m/s.

## 24. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Anisotropy (in tempered glass)** | Variation of stress across a pane producing visible birefringence patterns |
| **Annealing** | Controlled cooling to remove residual stress |
| **Annealing point** | Temperature where viscosity is 10^12 Pa·s; stresses relax in minutes |
| **Arrissed edge** | Edge with sharp corners lightly ground off (seamed) |
| **Biot number** | Ratio of surface heat transfer to internal conduction |
| **Birefringence** | Different refractive indices for different polarizations, caused by stress in glass |
| **Central tension (CT)** | Tensile stress at the mid-plane of strengthened glass |
| **COE** | Coefficient of thermal expansion, art-glass shorthand (×10^-7/C) |
| **Compressive stress (CS)** | Surface compression, especially in ion-exchanged glass |
| **Cord** | Streak of different composition in glass |
| **Cullet** | Crushed recycled glass for remelting |
| **Depth of layer (DOL)** | Depth of the ion-exchanged layer or compressive zone |
| **Devitrification** | Unwanted crystallization in glass |
| **Fictive temperature** | Temperature at which the frozen glass structure would be in equilibrium |
| **Fining** | Removal of bubbles from molten glass |
| **Float glass** | Flat glass formed on a molten tin bath |
| **Fragility** | Measure of how sharply viscosity changes near Tg |
| **Frangibility** | Tendency of glass to break into many small pieces |
| **Fully tempered (FT)** | Thermally strengthened glass meeting safety glass requirements |
| **Glass-ceramic** | Glass crystallized under control into a fine-grained ceramic |
| **Glass transition (Tg)** | Temperature range where a supercooled liquid becomes a rigid glass |
| **Griffith flaw** | Microscopic crack that controls the strength of brittle materials |
| **Hackle** | Rough fracture surface region from crack branching |
| **Heat soak** | Reheating tempered glass to cause NiS failures before installation |
| **Heat-strengthened (HS)** | Thermally strengthened glass with moderate surface compression |
| **Ion exchange** | Chemical strengthening by swapping small ions for larger ones |
| **K_Ic** | Fracture toughness: critical stress intensity factor in mode I |
| **Laminated glass** | Glass plies bonded with a polymer interlayer |
| **Lehr** | Annealing oven or tunnel |
| **Mirror, mist, hackle** | Sequence of features on a brittle fracture surface |
| **Network former** | Oxide that builds the glass network (SiO2, B2O3, P2O5) |
| **Network modifier** | Oxide that breaks the network (Na2O, CaO) |
| **NiS inclusion** | Nickel sulfide particle that can cause spontaneous breakage in tempered glass |
| **Non-bridging oxygen (NBO)** | Oxygen bonded to only one network former |
| **Quench** | Rapid cooling, in tempering by air jets |
| **Roller wave** | Optical distortion from sagging between furnace rollers |
| **Softening point** | Temperature where viscosity is 10^6.6 Pa·s; glass slumps under its own weight |
| **Strain point** | Temperature where viscosity is 10^13.5 Pa·s; below this, permanent stress cannot form |
| **Stress corrosion** | Water-assisted slow crack growth |
| **Stress-optic coefficient** | Ratio relating birefringence to stress |
| **Tin side** | Bottom surface of float glass carrying diffused tin |
| **Weibull modulus (m)** | Measure of strength scatter |
| **Working point** | Temperature where viscosity is 10^3 Pa·s; suitable for forming |
