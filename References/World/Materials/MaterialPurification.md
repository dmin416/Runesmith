## 1. Silica (SiO₂)

### Where it is found
- Quartz sand in rivers, beaches, dunes and sandstone
- Quartz veins in granite and metamorphic rock
- Pegmatites, geodes, alpine clefts
- Flint and chert nodules in chalk and limestone

### Purity grades
| Grade | SiO₂ content | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Common sand | 80–98% | Concrete, fill |
| Glass sand | 99.5%+, iron oxide under 0.03% | Clear window and bottle glass |
| High-purity quartz (HPQ) | 99.99%+ | Lamp tubes, solar and semiconductor parts |
| Ultra-HPQ | 99.998%+ | Crucibles for growing silicon crystals |
| Synthetic silica | 99.9999%+ (metals in parts per billion) | Optical fiber, lithography lenses |

### Methods
**1. Washing and sizing.** Sand is scrubbed in water so grains grind against each other and knock off clay coatings, then sieved and rinsed. Removes clay, silt and loose iron stains.
- Result: 98–99.5%
- Energy: 0.05–0.2 GJ/t

**2. Mineral separation.** Froth flotation floats off feldspar and mica; magnets pull out iron-bearing grains; gravity spirals separate heavy minerals.
- Result: 99.5–99.8%
- Energy: 0.05–0.15 GJ/t plus 0.15–0.3 GJ/t to dry the sand

**3. Acid leaching.** Hot hydrochloric, sulfuric or oxalic acid at 60–90°C dissolves iron, aluminum and other metal coatings. Hydrofluoric acid etches grain surfaces for deeper cleaning.
- Result: 99.9–99.99%
- Energy: 0.5–2 GJ/t including heat and the energy embodied in the acids

**4. Calcination and quenching.** Quartz is heated to 900–1,000°C and dropped into cold water. Thermal shock cracks the crystals and opens trapped fluid and mineral inclusions for leaching.
- Result: improves leaching to 99.99%+
- Energy: 1.5–3 GJ/t

**5. Hot chlorination.** Crushed quartz is roasted at 1,000–1,200°C in chlorine or hydrogen chloride gas. Metal impurities become volatile chlorides and are carried off as vapor. Requires an exceptionally clean source rock such as Spruce Pine, North Carolina.
- Result: 99.995–99.999%
- Energy: 2–5 GJ/t

**6. Dissolve and reprecipitate.** Sand is fused with soda ash at about 1,300°C or cooked with caustic soda under pressure to make sodium silicate (water glass). Solids are filtered out, acid is added and pure silica precipitates as gel or powder.
- Result: 99–99.9% as fine powder
- Energy: ~10–20 GJ/t (estimate)

**7. Vapor route (synthetic silica).** Silica is converted to silicon tetrachloride (SiCl₄), distilled to extreme purity and burned in a hydrogen-oxygen flame. The silica soot is collected and fused into glass.
- Result: 99.9999%+
- Energy: ~50–200 GJ/t (estimate)

### Summary
| Method | Max purity | Energy (GJ/t) | kWh per kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Washing, sizing | 99.5% | 0.05–0.2 | 0.01–0.06 |
| Flotation, magnetic, drying | 99.8% | 0.2–0.45 | 0.06–0.13 |
| Acid leaching | 99.99% | 0.5–2 | 0.14–0.56 |
| Calcine and quench | 99.99%+ | 1.5–3 | 0.4–0.8 |
| Hot chlorination | 99.999% | 2–5 | 0.6–1.4 |
| Dissolve and reprecipitate | 99.9% | ~10–20 | ~3–6 |
| Vapor route | 99.9999%+ | ~50–200 | ~14–56 |

### Theoretical minimum
Separating 0.5% impurities from silica requires about **0.0013 GJ/t** (1.3 kJ/kg). Real processes use 100 to 100,000 times more because of heating, pumping, grinding, drying and reagent production.

### Preindustrial methods
- **Source selection:** Venetian glassmakers used white quartz river pebbles from the Ticino and Adige rivers instead of beach sand.
- **Fire and quench:** English lead crystal makers heated flint or quartz red-hot and plunged it into water so it would crumble for grinding.
- **Washing and hand sorting:** repeated washing and picking out dark grains by eye.
- **Acid leaching:** distilled acids such as oil of vitriol (sulfuric acid) and spirit of salt (hydrochloric acid) remove iron stains. Vinegar has little effect.
- **Decolorizing in the melt:** manganese dioxide masks remaining iron color in glass.

## 2. Clay

### Where it is found
- **Kaolin (china clay):** forms where feldspar in granite weathers or is altered by hot fluids. Classic sources: Gaoling hill near Jingdezhen in China (origin of the name "kaolin"), St Austell in Cornwall, Georgia in the US.
- **Ball clay:** fine, plastic sedimentary clay deposited in ancient lakes and swamps.
- **Common/brick clay:** river floodplains, lake beds, glacial deposits, weathered shale. Usually red from iron.
- **Fire clay:** found beneath coal seams. Resists high heat.
- **Bentonite:** weathered volcanic ash. Swells in water.

### Purity grades
| Grade | Character | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Common clay | Mixed minerals, high iron, sand and organics | Bricks, tiles, earthenware |
| Ball clay | Fine, plastic, some organics | Stoneware, sanitaryware |
| Water-washed kaolin | 85–95% kaolinite, low iron | Porcelain, paper filler |
| Bleached/high-brightness kaolin | Iron and titanium minerals removed | Paper coating, paint, fine porcelain |
| Calcined kaolin | Fired to remove water and organics | Paint, plastics, refractories |
| Synthetic/refined clays | Lab-controlled composition | Pharmaceuticals, catalysts |

Clay purity is measured mainly by kaolinite content, whiteness (brightness) and low iron and titanium.

See also: [../Science/Invent/Toilets.md](../Science/Invent/Toilets.md) (vitreous china manufacturing for sanitary fixtures).

### Methods
**1. Weathering and slaking.** Raw clay is left outdoors through rain and freeze-thaw cycles, then soaked in water until it breaks down into slurry.
- Result: workable clay, impurities loosened
- Energy: near zero (time and labor)

**2. Levigation (blunging and settling).** Clay is stirred into water. Sand and coarse grit sink quickly; the fine clay stays suspended and is poured off into settling tanks. Industry uses hydrocyclones and centrifuges.
- Result: removes sand, mica and coarse particles
- Energy: 0.05–0.2 GJ/t (estimate)

**3. Magnetic separation.** High-gradient magnets pull out fine iron and titanium-bearing particles from the slurry.
- Result: large brightness gain
- Energy: 0.1–0.3 GJ/t (estimate)

**4. Froth flotation.** Removes titanium dioxide (anatase) and other colored minerals.
- Result: further brightness gain
- Energy: 0.1–0.3 GJ/t (estimate)

**5. Chemical bleaching.** Sodium dithionite in acidic slurry converts insoluble iron to a soluble form that is washed away. Oxidizing agents remove organics.
- Result: highest brightness
- Energy: 0.2–0.5 GJ/t including reagents (estimate)

**6. Dewatering and drying.** Filter presses squeeze out water; spray dryers or rotary dryers finish the job. This is usually the largest energy cost.
- Energy: 1–2.5 GJ/t (estimate)

**7. Calcination.** Kaolin is fired at 1,000–1,100°C to drive off chemically bound water and burn organics.
- Result: calcined kaolin
- Energy: 2–4 GJ/t additional (estimate)

### Summary
| Method | Result | Energy (GJ/t) | kWh per kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weathering, slaking | Workable clay | ~0 | ~0 |
| Levigation | Sand-free fine clay | 0.05–0.2 | 0.01–0.06 |
| Magnetic, flotation | High brightness | 0.2–0.6 | 0.06–0.17 |
| Bleaching | Highest brightness | 0.2–0.5 | 0.06–0.14 |
| Drying | Dry powder | 1–2.5 | 0.28–0.7 |
| Calcination | Calcined kaolin | 2–4 additional | 0.56–1.1 |
| Typical full processed kaolin | Paper/porcelain grade | ~1.5–3 (washed), ~4–6 (calcined) | ~0.4–1.7 |

### Theoretical minimum
Similar to silica: on the order of **0.001 GJ/t** to separate a few percent of impurities. Nearly all real energy goes into drying water.

### Preindustrial methods
- **Weathering:** potters aged clay outdoors for months or years. Chinese tradition held that clay aged for a generation was best.
- **Settling pits:** clay was stirred in water and run through a chain of pits. Each pit collected finer material than the last.
- **Jingdezhen porcelain:** porcelain stone was crushed by water-powered trip hammers, washed, settled and pressed into small white bricks for sale to potters.
- **Sieving:** slurry was passed through cloth or fine hair sieves.
- **Kneading and wedging:** hand-working removed air pockets and evened out moisture.

## 3. Iron

### Where it is found
- **Banded iron formations:** huge ancient layers of hematite and magnetite (Lake Superior region, Pilbara in Australia, Brazil). The main modern source.
- **Bog iron:** brown limonite that precipitates in wetlands and lake bottoms from iron-rich groundwater. Renews over decades. The main source for many preindustrial cultures.
- **Magnetite sands:** black sand on beaches and riverbeds (New Zealand ironsands, Japanese satetsu used for tamahagane).
- **Siderite and laterites:** iron carbonate beds and iron-rich tropical soils.
- **Meteorites:** iron-nickel metal ready to forge. The source of the earliest worked iron.

### Purity grades
| Grade | Iron content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pig iron / cast iron | 93–95% | 3.5–4.5% carbon; hard and brittle |
| Steel | 97–99.9% | 0.05–2% carbon plus alloying elements |
| Wrought iron | 99%+ metal | Low carbon with fibrous slag inclusions |
| Electrolytic / carbonyl iron | 99.5–99.99% | Magnetic cores, powders, research |
| Ultra-high-purity iron | 99.999%+ | Research; very corrosion resistant |

### Methods
**1. Ore beneficiation.** Ore is crushed, concentrated by magnets and flotation and baked into pellets or sinter.
- Result: 60–68% Fe concentrate
- Energy: 1–2 GJ/t of iron

**2. Bloomery.** Ore and charcoal are layered in a clay shaft furnace with bellows at about 1,200°C. Iron is reduced without melting and forms a spongy bloom mixed with slag, which is hammered to squeeze out the slag.
- Result: wrought iron or uneven steel
- Energy: ~100–300 GJ/t in charcoal (estimate). Producing the charcoal from wood multiplies this several times.

**3. Blast furnace + basic oxygen furnace.** Coke, ore pellets and limestone feed a tall furnace with a hot air blast. Iron melts and absorbs carbon to make pig iron. Oxygen is then blown through the molten iron to burn the carbon down to steel levels.
- Result: steel
- Energy: ~18–25 GJ/t of steel

**4. Direct reduction + electric arc furnace.** Natural gas or hydrogen reduces pellets to solid sponge iron at 800–1,000°C. The sponge iron is melted in an electric arc furnace.
- Result: steel
- Energy: ~12–18 GJ/t

**5. Electric arc furnace from scrap.** Recycled steel is melted with electric arcs.
- Result: steel
- Energy: ~1.5–2.5 GJ/t

**6. Electrolytic refining.** Iron is dissolved from an anode and plated onto a cathode in a sulfate or chloride solution.
- Result: 99.9–99.99%
- Energy: ~10–20 GJ/t on top of the feed iron (estimate)

**7. Carbonyl process.** Iron reacts with carbon monoxide under pressure to form liquid iron pentacarbonyl, which is distilled and then decomposed at 200–300°C into pure iron powder.
- Result: 99.5%+ fine powder
- Energy: ~20–40 GJ/t (estimate)

**8. Zone refining.** A molten zone passes slowly along a bar many times and sweeps impurities to one end.
- Result: 99.999%+
- Energy: high per kg; small-scale only

### Summary
| Method | Max purity | Energy (GJ/t) | kWh per kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bloomery | Wrought iron | ~100–300 | ~28–83 |
| Blast furnace + BOF | Steel | 18–25 | 5–7 |
| Direct reduction + EAF | Steel | 12–18 | 3.3–5 |
| Scrap EAF | Steel | 1.5–2.5 | 0.4–0.7 |
| Electrolytic | 99.99% | +10–20 | +2.8–5.6 |
| Carbonyl | 99.5%+ powder | ~20–40 | ~5.6–11 |
| Zone refining | 99.999%+ | Very high | n/a |

### Theoretical minimum
Reducing hematite (Fe₂O₃) to iron requires **7.4 GJ/t**. Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) requires **6.7 GJ/t**. Modern blast furnaces run at about twice the minimum.

### Preindustrial methods
- **Meteoric iron:** cold-hammered and forged directly.
- **Bog iron gathering:** dredged from wetland bottoms, dried and roasted to drive off water.
- **Roasting:** ore was heated in open fires to crack it and remove water and sulfur.
- **Bloomery smelting:** described above. Workers hammered the bloom repeatedly while hot to consolidate it and expel slag.
- **Tatara (Japan):** magnetite sand and charcoal were fed into a clay furnace for about three days, producing a large bloom of mixed steel (tamahagane) that smiths sorted by carbon content.
- **Finery and puddling:** later methods converted cast iron to wrought iron by stirring it in an oxidizing fire to burn out the carbon.
- **Carburizing:** wrought iron was packed with charcoal and heated for days to make steel (cementation or blister steel).
- **Crucible steel:** wrought iron and carbon sources were sealed in clay crucibles and fully melted (wootz in India, later Huntsman steel in England).

## 4. Titanium

### Where it is found
- **Mineral sands:** black beach and dune sands rich in ilmenite (FeTiO₃) and rutile (TiO₂). Major sources: Australia, South Africa, India, Mozambique.
- **Hard-rock ilmenite:** large deposits in Norway and Quebec.
- **Anatase and leucoxene:** altered titanium minerals in weathered rocks.
- **Basalt and weathered tropical soils:** titanium concentrates as other elements wash away (see Hawaii).

### Purity grades
| Grade | Titanium content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Titanium dioxide pigment | 99%+ TiO₂ (not metal) | White paint, paper, sunscreen |
| Titanium sponge | 99.3–99.8% | Porous raw metal from the Kroll process |
| Commercially pure (Grades 1–4) | 99–99.5% | Chemical plants, implants |
| Alloys (e.g. Ti-6Al-4V) | ~90% | Aerospace, medical |
| High-purity titanium | 99.99–99.999% | Electronics, sputtering targets |

### Methods
**1. Mineral sand separation.** Sand is dredged and run through gravity spirals, magnetic separators (ilmenite is weakly magnetic) and electrostatic separators (rutile and ilmenite conduct; zircon does not).
- Result: ilmenite and rutile concentrates
- Energy: ~0.5–1 GJ/t of concentrate (estimate)

**2. Ilmenite upgrading.** Ilmenite is smelted in electric furnaces to separate iron and produce titanium slag (80–95% TiO₂). Alternatively, the Becher process reduces the iron, rusts it out with air and water and leaves synthetic rutile.
- Result: 85–95% TiO₂
- Energy: ~8–15 GJ/t (estimate)

**3. Chlorination.** TiO₂ concentrate is reacted with carbon and chlorine gas at about 1,000°C to form titanium tetrachloride (TiCl₄), a liquid. It is purified by distillation.
- Result: very pure TiCl₄
- Energy: included in Kroll totals below

**4. Kroll process.** TiCl₄ drips onto molten magnesium in a sealed steel vessel under argon at 800–900°C. Magnesium strips the chlorine away and leaves porous titanium sponge. The byproduct magnesium chloride is split by electrolysis to recycle the magnesium and chlorine. Leftover magnesium is boiled off the sponge under vacuum.
- Result: 99.3–99.8% sponge
- Energy: ~100–200 GJ/t of sponge (estimate)

**5. Vacuum arc remelting / electron beam melting.** Sponge is pressed into electrodes and melted under vacuum. This removes volatile impurities and produces solid ingots.
- Result: ingot
- Energy: total from ore to ingot ~300–700 GJ/t (estimate)

**6. Iodide process (van Arkel–de Boer).** Crude titanium reacts with iodine vapor to form titanium tetraiodide. The vapor breaks down on a white-hot tungsten filament (~1,400°C), which grows a bar of extremely pure titanium crystals.
- Result: 99.99%+
- Energy: extremely high per kg; specialty only

**7. Direct electrolysis (emerging).** TiO₂ is used as the cathode in molten calcium chloride and electricity strips the oxygen directly.
- Result: powder of varying purity
- Energy: lower than Kroll in theory; not yet established at large scale

### Summary
| Method | Max purity | Energy (GJ/t) | kWh per kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sand separation | Concentrate | ~0.5–1 | ~0.14–0.28 |
| Ilmenite upgrading | 85–95% TiO₂ | ~8–15 | ~2.2–4.2 |
| Kroll sponge | 99.3–99.8% | ~100–200 | ~28–56 |
| Ore to ingot | 99–99.5% | ~300–700 | ~83–194 |
| Iodide process | 99.99%+ | Very high | n/a |

### Theoretical minimum
Reducing rutile (TiO₂) to titanium metal requires **19.7 GJ/t**. Real production uses 15 to 35 times more because titanium cannot be reduced with carbon. Hot titanium also absorbs oxygen, nitrogen and carbon, which make it brittle, so every step must be sealed from air.

### Preindustrial methods
None. Titanium was identified in 1791 but was not isolated as pure metal until 1910 and the Kroll process came in 1940. Preindustrial production would require:
- A metal more reactive than titanium to strip the oxygen or chlorine (magnesium, sodium or calcium)
- Chlorine gas
- A fully sealed, airless vessel at red heat

Without those, titanium minerals could only be used as white or yellow pigment, or as heavy black sand.

## 5. Gold

### Where it is found
- **Quartz lode veins:** gold deposited by hot fluids in cracks, often with pyrite.
- **Placer deposits:** flakes, dust and nuggets concentrated by rivers in gravel bars and old stream beds. The main preindustrial source.
- **Epithermal deposits:** formed near the surface above volcanic systems (see Volcanoes).
- **Invisible gold:** microscopic particles in sedimentary rock (Carlin-type deposits in Nevada).
- **Byproduct:** recovered from copper ore and copper refining.

Native gold is almost always alloyed with silver. When the silver content exceeds about 20% the alloy is called electrum.

### Purity grades
| Grade | Gold content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Native gold | 70–95% | Silver is the main impurity |
| Doré bar | 60–90% | Raw mine output with silver |
| 18 karat | 75% | Jewelry |
| 22 karat | 91.7% | Traditional coinage |
| Good delivery bar | 99.5% | Bullion trading standard |
| Four nines (24 karat) | 99.99% | Investment bullion |
| Five nines | 99.999% | Electronics, research |

### Methods
**1. Gravity separation.** Panning, sluice boxes, rocker boxes, jigs, shaking tables and centrifugal concentrators. Gold is about 19 times denser than water and 7 times denser than sand.
- Result: native gold flakes and concentrate
- Energy: very low per tonne of gravel

**2. Mercury amalgamation.** Mercury dissolves gold into a soft paste. The paste is squeezed through cloth and heated to boil off the mercury. Toxic; banned in most industrial use but still common in small-scale mining.
- Result: sponge gold with silver
- Energy: low; high health and environmental cost

**3. Cyanide leaching.** Crushed ore is soaked in dilute sodium cyanide solution, which dissolves gold. The gold is absorbed onto activated carbon, stripped off and electroplated, then melted into doré.
- Result: doré bar
- Energy: ~100,000–300,000 GJ per tonne of gold (100–300 GJ per kg, estimate). Ore typically contains 1–5 g of gold per tonne, so nearly all the energy goes into blasting, hauling and grinding rock.

**4. Fire refining with fluxes.** Doré is melted with borax, silica and soda to slag off base metals.
- Result: removes copper, iron and lead

**5. Miller process.** Chlorine gas is bubbled through molten gold. Silver and base metals form chlorides that float off as slag.
- Result: 99.5–99.6%
- Energy: low relative to mining

**6. Wohlwill process.** Impure gold anodes are dissolved electrically in hydrochloric acid with gold chloride and pure gold plates onto the cathode.
- Result: 99.99%
- Energy: low relative to mining

**7. Aqua regia refining.** Gold is dissolved in a mixture of nitric and hydrochloric acid, silver chloride is filtered out and gold is precipitated with a selective reducing agent.
- Result: 99.99–99.999%
- Energy: low relative to mining

### Summary
| Method | Max purity | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| Gravity (placer) | 70–95% native gold | Very low |
| Mercury amalgamation | 70–95% | Low |
| Cyanide leaching (hard rock) | Doré | ~100–300 GJ per kg of gold |
| Miller process | 99.5% | Small relative to mining |
| Wohlwill electrolysis | 99.99% | Small relative to mining |
| Aqua regia | 99.999% | Small relative to mining |

### Theoretical minimum
Gold occurs as metal, so no chemical reduction is needed. The only minimum cost is separating dilute gold from rock: about **0.2 GJ/t** of gold for ore at 5 g/t. Real hard-rock mining uses roughly a million times more.

### Preindustrial methods
- **Panning and sluicing:** gravel washed in pans or wooden channels. Sheep fleeces laid in streams trapped fine gold, a possible origin of the Golden Fleece legend.
- **Hushing and hydraulic mining:** Romans released stored water from reservoirs to wash away whole hillsides (Las Médulas, Spain).
- **Stamp mills:** water-powered hammers crushed quartz ore for washing.
- **Cupellation:** gold was melted with lead in a porous dish made of bone ash. The lead oxidized and soaked into the dish, carrying base metals with it. Silver stays with the gold.
- **Salt cementation:** gold flakes were packed with salt and brick dust in sealed pots and heated for days. Silver converted to silver chloride and was absorbed into the packing. Used in Lydia (Sardis) around the 6th century BCE to make the first pure gold coins.
- **Nitric acid parting:** gold was alloyed with about three parts silver to one part gold so the acid could reach the silver, then soaked in nitric acid (aqua fortis), which dissolves silver and leaves gold. Medieval Europe onward.
- **Touchstone testing:** gold was rubbed on black stone and the streak was compared against reference needles of known purity.

## 6. Silver

### Where it is found
- **Argentiferous galena:** lead sulfide ore carrying silver. The main historic source (Laurion near Athens, Rio Tinto, Central Europe).
- **Native silver:** wire and sheet silver in veins (Kongsberg, Norway; Cobalt, Ontario).
- **Silver sulfides and chlorides:** acanthite (Ag₂S) and horn silver (AgCl) in vein ores and weathered zones (Potosí in Bolivia, Zacatecas in Mexico, Comstock Lode in Nevada).
- **Byproduct:** today most silver comes from lead, zinc, copper and gold mining.

### Purity grades
| Grade | Silver content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Coin silver | 90% | Historic US coinage |
| Sterling | 92.5% | Jewelry, tableware |
| Britannia | 95.8% | British hallmark standard |
| Fine silver | 99.9% | Bullion, good delivery bars |
| Four nines | 99.99% | Electronics, photography |
| Five nines and higher | 99.999%+ | Research, semiconductors |

### Methods
**1. Lead smelting + cupellation.** Silver-bearing lead ore is smelted into silver-rich lead. The lead is then melted in a shallow hearth or bone-ash dish with an air blast at 900–1,000°C. Lead oxidizes into litharge, which is skimmed or absorbed and a bright disc of silver remains.
- Result: 95–99%

**2. Pattinson and Parkes processes.** Pattinson: molten lead is cooled slowly and lead crystals that form are ladled off, leaving silver-enriched liquid. Parkes: zinc is stirred into molten lead; silver dissolves into the zinc, which floats as a crust. The zinc is distilled away and the silver is cupelled.
- Result: concentrates silver efficiently from low-grade lead

**3. Patio process.** Ground ore is mixed with salt, copper sulfate and mercury on stone courtyards and trampled by mules for weeks. Silver forms an amalgam and the mercury is boiled off. Used in Spanish America from 1554.
- Result: sponge silver
- Energy: low; very high mercury losses

**4. Cyanide leaching.** Same as gold.

**5. Anode slimes.** Silver collects in the sludge beneath copper refining tanks and is recovered with other precious metals.

**6. Electrolytic refining.** Impure silver anodes dissolve in silver nitrate solution and pure silver crystals grow on the cathode.
- Result: 99.9–99.99%

**Overall energy:** ~1,500–3,000 GJ per tonne of silver (estimate; depends heavily on how energy is divided among co-products).

### Summary
| Method | Max purity | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| Cupellation | 95–99% | Moderate (lead smelting dominates) |
| Parkes + cupellation | 99%+ | Moderate |
| Patio amalgamation | 95%+ | Low energy, high mercury cost |
| Electrolytic refining | 99.99% | Small relative to mining |
| Overall primary silver | 99.9%+ | ~1,500–3,000 GJ/t (estimate) |

### Theoretical minimum
Reducing silver sulfide to silver requires about **0.15 GJ/t**. Including separation from ore at typical grades, the total is about **0.3 GJ/t**. Silver compounds are weakly bonded; silver oxide decomposes on its own around 200–300°C.

### Preindustrial methods
- **Cupellation:** in use since at least 3000 BCE. Laurion's silver financed the Athenian fleet.
- **Liquation (Saigerprozess):** 15th-century Germany. Silver-bearing copper was melted with lead; when cooled and gently reheated, the lead drained out carrying the silver, which was then cupelled.
- **Patio process:** described above.
- **Native silver:** hammered and melted directly.
- **Assaying:** small samples were cupelled and weighed to judge ore value.

## 7. Metals Found in Volcanoes

### Where metals concentrate in a volcanic system
| Setting | Metals and materials |
|---|---|
| Fumaroles and gas vents | Native sulfur, sal ammoniac (ammonium chloride), metal chlorides and sulfides of copper, zinc, lead, cadmium, thallium, bismuth, arsenic, mercury, molybdenum. Rhenium sulfide at Kudryavy volcano (Kuril Islands), the only known rhenium mineral. Gold in measurable amounts at Galeras volcano (Colombia). |
| Epithermal veins (near surface, above magma) | Gold, silver, mercury, antimony, tellurium |
| Porphyry deposits (deep beneath volcanoes) | Copper, molybdenum, gold. The largest copper mines in the world (Andes) |
| Seafloor black smokers (volcanogenic massive sulfides) | Copper, zinc, lead, gold, silver, cobalt |
| Lava and basalt | Iron, magnesium, titanium, aluminum, calcium (in titanomagnetite, olivine, pyroxene, feldspar) |
| Layered magma intrusions | Chromium, platinum group metals, nickel, vanadium (Bushveld, South Africa) |
| Kimberlite pipes | Diamonds |
| Volcanic caldera clays and brines | Lithium (McDermitt caldera, Nevada; geothermal brines at Salton Sea) |
| Hot springs (Larderello, Tuscany) | Boric acid, the historic source of borax for glassmaking |
| Volcanic glass | Obsidian, pumice, perlite (natural glass) |

### Extraction and energy
| Material | Main method | Energy (GJ/t) |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfur | Collected or melted from fumarole crusts; Frasch process for buried deposits | ~0.1–0.5 (collection/melting, estimate) |
| Copper | Crushing, flotation, smelting to matte, converting to blister copper, electrorefining | ~30–65 |
| Zinc | Roasting sulfide, leaching in sulfuric acid, electrowinning | ~40–50 |
| Lead | Sintering, blast furnace smelting, refining | ~20–30 |
| Nickel | Smelting sulfide ore or acid leaching laterite | ~110–200 |
| Molybdenum | Flotation as byproduct of copper, roasting to oxide, reduction | ~100–300 (estimate) |
| Platinum group metals | Smelting, then complex chemical separation | ~100,000–250,000 GJ/t (estimate; very low ore grade) |
| Lithium (from clay) | Acid leaching, precipitation as carbonate | Not established at scale |
| Boric acid | Condensing steam vents and evaporating hot spring water | Low |

### Preindustrial volcanic resources
- **Sulfur:** gathered as yellow crusts around vents (Ijen in Indonesia is still mined by hand). Used for medicine, fumigation, gunpowder and bleaching.
- **Sal ammoniac:** collected from vents on Vesuvius and Etna. Used in metal soldering and alchemy.
- **Obsidian:** knapped into blades sharper than steel.
- **Boric acid:** collected from Tuscan steam vents from the 1700s onward.
- **Pumice:** abrasive and lightweight building aggregate. Roman concrete used volcanic ash (pozzolana).

## 8. Hawaii

Hawaii's islands are young basaltic shield volcanoes over a hot spot. This chemistry produces abundant iron, magnesium, titanium and aluminum but almost no gold, silver, copper deposits or quartz.

### Available materials
| Material | Location / form | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Basalt | Everywhere | Dense basalt was the principal tool stone (Mauna Kea adze quarry) |
| Aluminum (bauxite) | Highly weathered red soils on Kauaʻi, Maui and Oʻahu | Iron-rich "ferruginous bauxite"; studied but never mined |
| Titanium | Same weathered soils; titanium concentrates as other elements wash away | Several percent to over 10% TiO₂ in some soils |
| Iron | Weathered soils (iron oxides), magnetite grains in black sands | Low-grade by industrial standards |
| Olivine | Green sand at Papakōlea Beach; olivine crystals in lava | Gem form is peridot; a magnesium-iron silicate |
| Volcanic glass | Pele's hair (glass threads), Pele's tears (glass droplets), black sand at Punaluʻu | Natural glass |
| Sulfur | Sulphur Banks and vents at Kīlauea | Small deposits |
| Calcium carbonate | Coral reefs and white coral sand beaches | Source of lime |
| Clay | Weathered volcanic soils: kaolinite, halloysite, gibbsite | Iron-rich red clays |
| Manganese, cobalt, nickel | Ferromanganese crusts on surrounding seamounts; nodules in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone southeast of Hawaii | Deep-sea only |
| Quartz / silica sand | Essentially absent | Basaltic magma rarely forms quartz |
| Gold, silver, copper ores | Absent | No economic deposits |

### Purification of Hawaiian resources
**Aluminum from bauxite.**
- Bayer process: bauxite is digested in hot caustic soda at 150–250°C, which dissolves alumina; iron and titanium residue (red mud) is filtered off; pure aluminum hydroxide crystallizes out and is calcined to alumina.
- Hall–Héroult process: alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite at about 960°C and split by electrolysis. Smelting alone uses about 13–15 kWh per kg.
- Total energy: ~100–200 GJ/t of aluminum (estimate, including power generation)
- Theoretical minimum: **31 GJ/t**
- Hawaii's bauxite is unusually high in iron, which increases waste and cost.

**Titanium from soils.** Same route as Section 4. Concentrating titanium from soil would require removing iron and aluminum first.

**Iron from soils and sands.** Same route as Section 3. Magnetite can be pulled from black sand with magnets.

**Magnesium from olivine or seawater.**
- Seawater (Dow process): magnesium is precipitated from seawater with lime from burned coral, converted to magnesium chloride and split by electrolysis.
- Pidgeon process: magnesium oxide is reduced with silicon under vacuum at about 1,200°C and the magnesium vapor is condensed.
- Energy: ~100–350 GJ/t (estimate)
- Theoretical minimum: **24.8 GJ/t**

**Lime from coral.** Coral is burned at about 900°C to make quicklime (calcium oxide). Energy: ~4–5 GJ/t.

**Sulfur.** Collected from vents and melted to separate it from rock. Low energy.

**Silica for glassmaking.** Local supply is a problem. Options:
- Import quartz sand.
- Melt basalt directly: makes dark green to black glass (rock wool and basalt fiber are made this way).
- Use natural volcanic glass, which is dark from iron.
- Clear glass is not practical from local materials without removing iron.

### Preindustrial use in Hawaii
- Native Hawaiians had no metalworking before European contact. Tools were made of basalt, volcanic glass, bone, shell and wood.
- Dense basalt for adzes was quarried high on Mauna Kea.
- Iron was unknown until it arrived on driftwood and from ships and it was highly prized once available.
- Coral was burned for lime after contact for mortar and whitewash.

## 9. Comparison: Practical Energy vs Theoretical Minimum

| Material | Typical practical energy (GJ/t) | Theoretical minimum (GJ/t) | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silica (glass sand) | 0.2–0.5 | 0.0013 | ~150–400× |
| Silica (synthetic) | ~50–200 | 0.0013 | ~40,000–150,000× |
| Kaolin (processed) | ~1.5–6 | ~0.001 | ~1,500–6,000× |
| Iron/steel | 12–25 | 6.7–7.4 | ~2–3× |
| Aluminum | ~100–200 | 31 | ~3–6× |
| Magnesium | ~100–350 | 24.8 | ~4–14× |
| Titanium (ingot) | ~300–700 | 19.7 | ~15–35× |
| Silver | ~1,500–3,000 | ~0.3 | ~5,000–10,000× |
| Gold | ~100,000–300,000 | ~0.2 | ~1,000,000× |

**Pattern:** For oxide metals (iron, aluminum, magnesium, titanium), the minimum is set by the strength of the bond between metal and oxygen. Real processes come within a few times to a few dozen times of that minimum. For native or weakly bonded metals (gold, silver) and for already-oxidized minerals (silica, clay), chemistry costs almost nothing. Real energy is spent moving, crushing, heating and drying huge volumes of material to recover a small amount of product.
