## 1. Core Wear Mechanisms

Almost all deterioration comes from a short list of processes. Environment decides which ones dominate and how fast they run.

### 1.1 Physical (mechanical) processes

**Freeze-thaw**
- Water expands about 9% in volume on freezing.
- A pore that is more than roughly 91% full of water cannot accommodate that expansion. Ice growth plus hydraulic pressure from water pushed ahead of the ice front cracks the material.
- Damage tracks the number of freeze-thaw cycles that happen while the material is saturated. Absolute cold matters less. A climate that crosses 0°C (32°F) 50 to 100+ times per winter (US Mid-Atlantic and Midwest, Scotland, central Europe, southern Canada) destroys masonry faster than a climate that freezes solid in November and stays frozen until April.
- Symptoms: surface scaling, spalling, cracks parallel to the face, popouts, crumbling edges and corners.

**Salt crystallization**
- The most destructive single process for porous masonry in coastal and arid regions.
- Salt-laden water soaks into pores. As water evaporates, salt crystals grow inside the pores (subflorescence) or on the surface (efflorescence).
- Crystal growth pressure can exceed the tensile strength of most stone (typically 2 to 10 MPa).
- Sodium sulfate is the most aggressive common salt because it flips between an anhydrous form (thenardite) and a hydrated form (mirabilite) that occupies roughly three times the volume. Each humidity swing can trigger a new round of expansion.
- Sodium chloride (sea salt) is less aggressive per cycle but is supplied in enormous quantities near coasts. It is also hygroscopic: it pulls moisture from the air above about 75% relative humidity, so salty surfaces stay damp.
- Salt sources: sea spray, groundwater (rising damp), de-icing salts, Portland cement mortar (alkali sulfates), bird and bat guano, polluted air (sulfates and nitrates), soil, fertilizer runoff.
- Damage concentrates at the drying front, where evaporation happens. This creates powdering, flaking, contour scaling, honeycombing and cavernous hollows (tafoni).

**Thermal cycling**
- Daily heating and cooling makes minerals expand and contract.
- Mixed-mineral rocks suffer because each mineral expands at a different rate. Calcite is extreme: it expands about 26 × 10⁻⁶ per °C along one crystal axis and actually contracts slightly (about 6 × 10⁻⁶ per °C) along the perpendicular axes. Grain boundaries in marble slowly loosen as a result.
- Dark surfaces in direct sun reach 60 to 75°C (140 to 167°F) while the air is 30 to 35°C. A desert night or a cold rain on a hot wall can drop surface temperature 30 to 50°C in minutes to hours.
- Dry heating alone is a weak agent. Early 20th-century lab tests that heated and cooled granite thousands of times showed little damage. Add moisture or salt and the same cycling becomes highly effective.

**Wetting and drying**
- Clay minerals inside some stones (argillaceous sandstones, mudstones, some limestones) swell when wet and shrink when dry, fatiguing the rock.
- Wood swells and shrinks across the grain with moisture changes. This drives checking, cupping and fastener loosening.

**Abrasion**
- Wind-blown sand, waves throwing shingle, river and sea ice, foot and vehicle traffic, rain impact on soft surfaces.

**Stress release (unloading)**
- Rock that formed deep underground expands slightly when the overburden erodes away. It splits into sheets parallel to the surface. This is the origin of granite domes.

### 1.2 Chemical processes

**Dissolution**
- Rain absorbs carbon dioxide and is naturally mildly acidic (pH about 5.6).
- Calcite dissolves in it. Calcite appears in limestone, marble, lime mortar and calcite-cemented sandstone.
- Clean-air rain removes roughly 10 to 30 micrometers per year from exposed limestone and marble, or about 1 to 3 mm per century.

**Acid attack from pollution**
- Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from coal, oil and vehicles form sulfuric and nitric acid.
- Calcite reacts with sulfuric acid to form gypsum (calcium sulfate), which is roughly 150 times more soluble than calcite.
- Where rain washes the surface, gypsum dissolves and the stone recedes.
- Where the surface is sheltered from rain, gypsum accumulates, traps soot and forms hard black crusts. Crusts blister and detach, taking a layer of stone with them.
- Western Europe and North America saw peak damage from roughly 1850 to 1980. Clean air legislation (UK Clean Air Act 1956, US Clean Air Act 1970 and amendments) cut sulfur dioxide sharply. Stone decay rates in London and other cities have since fallen to a fraction of their mid-century values.

**Hydrolysis**
- Feldspars in granite and similar rocks react slowly with water and turn into clay (mostly kaolinite).
- As feldspar grains soften, quartz grains lose their grip and the rock crumbles into coarse sand called grus.

**Oxidation**
- Iron-bearing minerals (pyrite, biotite, magnetite, iron-rich clays) rust. Rust occupies more volume than the original mineral and stains the surface orange-brown.
- Pyrite also produces sulfuric acid when it oxidizes. Pyrite inclusions cause rust spots and pitting in slate, marble and some concrete aggregates.
- Embedded iron and steel (cramps, dowels, rebar, anchors) rust and expand two to six times in volume, splitting whatever surrounds them.

**Carbonation**
- Atmospheric CO₂ diffuses into concrete and converts calcium hydroxide to calcium carbonate, lowering alkalinity. Covered in `WeatheringCementConcrete.md`.

**Leaching**
- Water dissolves calcium hydroxide from concrete and mortar and redeposits it as white crust (efflorescence) or dripstone.

**Photodegradation**
- Ultraviolet light breaks chemical bonds in wood lignin, paint binders, sealants, plastics and rubber gaskets. Stone, concrete and glass themselves are largely immune, though their sealants and coatings are not.

### 1.3 Biological processes

- **Lichens**: secrete oxalic and other organic acids. Hyphae penetrate stone along grain boundaries, often several millimeters deep. Calcium oxalate patinas form beneath them. Some lichens etch pits into limestone and marble.
- **Mosses**: hold water against the surface, raising freeze-thaw and decay risk. Rhizoids loosen grains.
- **Algae and cyanobacteria**: green, black or red biofilms. Discolor surfaces and retain moisture. Dominant in humid tropics.
- **Fungi**: the primary destroyers of wood (`WeatheringGlassWood.md`).
- **Bacteria**: sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in sewers produce sulfuric acid that eats concrete crowns. Nitrifying bacteria produce nitric acid on stone.
- **Plants**: ivy, fig and tree roots wedge joints apart. Ivy also shields some walls from weather; whether it helps or harms depends on mortar condition.
- **Insects**: termites, carpenter ants, wood-boring beetles.
- **Marine borers**: shipworms and gribbles in wood, boring sponges and bivalves in limestone.
- **Birds and bats**: guano is acidic and rich in phosphate and nitrate salts.

## 2. Environment as a Multiplier

### 2.1 Salt water nearby

**Marine exposure zones** (from corrosion engineering, applies to every material):

| Zone | Location | Main attack | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submerged | Permanently underwater | Low oxygen slows corrosion. Marine borers attack wood. Slow sulfate and magnesium attack on concrete. Most stone stable. | Low to moderate (very high for untreated wood in warm water) |
| Tidal | Wetted and dried twice daily | Salt cycling, abrasion, borers, freeze-thaw in cold climates | High. Worst zone for timber piling. |
| Splash | Above high tide, hit by waves and spray | Constant salt wetting with abundant oxygen, heavy crystallization | Severe. Worst zone for steel and reinforced concrete. |
| Atmospheric marine | Inland from the shoreline | Wind-carried salt aerosol, crystallization, metal corrosion | Moderate to high, falls with distance |

**How far salt travels**
- Deposition falls off steeply with distance from the water.
- The first 100 to 300 m (330 to 1,000 ft) from a surf beach receive the heaviest load.
- Salt remains significant to about 1 to 3 km (0.6 to 2 miles) inland.
- On windy coasts with onshore prevailing winds, measurable salt reaches 10 to 50 km inland.
- Breaking surf generates far more aerosol than calm water. A house 100 m from a calm sheltered harbor can receive less salt than one 1 km from an exposed headland with heavy surf.
- Height matters. Upper floors of coastal buildings often see more salt than ground floors shielded by dunes or other buildings.

**Material-specific marine effects**
- Stone and brick: salt crystallization, honeycombing, powdering at the drying zone.
- Concrete: chloride-induced rebar corrosion is the dominant failure.
- Metal fasteners, cramps and anchors: corrosion several times faster than inland. A plain steel nail can rust through in a few years near surf.
- Glass: salt films stay damp and promote alkali etching. Frames and hardware corrode.
- Wood: salt actually suppresses fungal decay (brine is hostile to most rot fungi). Salt crystals still pry surface fibers apart, producing a fuzzy, hairy texture. Fasteners corrode and stain. In water, marine borers are the main threat.

**Freshwater lakes and rivers**: no salt crystallization from spray, no marine borers. Freeze-thaw and ice abrasion dominate in cold climates. Moisture drives wood decay.

### 2.2 Sunny vs shaded

**Sun drives**
- UV damage to wood, paint, sealants, gaskets, plastics.
- Thermal cycling and peak surface temperatures.
- Fast drying, which reduces biological growth and freeze-thaw saturation.
- Salt crystallization at or near the surface (rapid evaporation pulls the drying front close to the face).

**Shade drives**
- Persistent dampness.
- Moss, algae, lichen and mildew.
- Fungal decay in wood.
- More freeze-thaw damage because shaded masonry stays saturated through winter.
- Salts crystallize deeper below the surface, causing thicker scales to detach.

**Orientation** (Northern Hemisphere; reverse for Southern)
- South faces: most UV and heat cycling, driest.
- West faces: afternoon sun coincides with peak air temperature, producing the highest surface temperatures. Paint, sealant and coating failures often start here.
- East faces: morning sun hits surfaces still cold or frosted from the night, causing sharp thermal shock.
- North faces: coolest, dampest, greenest. Moss and algae, frost damage, slower drying, highest wood decay risk.

**Altitude and latitude**: UV intensity rises roughly 10% per 1,000 m of elevation and increases toward the equator. High desert and mountain sites degrade wood and coatings fastest.

### 2.3 Windy vs sheltered

**Wind drives**
- Wind-driven rain forced into joints, cracks and window perimeters. Exposed coastal facades take several times more water than sheltered ones.
- Faster evaporation, which concentrates salt crystallization.
- Sand and grit abrasion. Saltating sand grains rarely rise more than about 1 m (3 ft) above the ground, with peak abrasion around 20 to 50 cm. Wind-sanded columns, walls and fence posts are undercut near the base.
- Salt spray delivery from the coast.
- Mechanical loading: racking of timber frames, fatigue of fasteners, glass deflection, roof uplift.
- Drying, which limits mold and moss on exposed faces.

**Sheltered sites**
- Stay wet longer: more biological growth, more decay in wood.
- Collect pollutants: black crusts form in sheltered recesses of stone buildings.
- Collect stagnant salt: salts accumulate without being rinsed off.

**Windward vs leeward**
- Windward faces: rain penetration, salt and sand abrasion, cleaner surfaces.
- Leeward faces: dirt deposition, longer wetness, more biological colonization, soot accumulation.

### 2.4 Other modifiers

- **Humidity and rainfall**: hot-humid climates accelerate nearly every chemical and biological process. Hot-dry climates preserve wood and slow corrosion but intensify salt and thermal attack.
- **Freeze-thaw frequency**: the critical factor for porous masonry and concrete in temperate climates.
- **Pollution**: urban and industrial air multiplies stone decay rates several times over.
- **Ground contact**: rising damp, soil salts, termites, decay fungi.
- **Maintenance**: roof, gutter and flashing condition controls water entry. Most catastrophic building decay begins with a water management failure.

## 10. Reading Wear Patterns on a Structure

### 10.1 Water paths

- **Drip lines**: streaks and erosion below sills, cornices and copings. Projections without drip grooves send water running back along the underside, staining and eroding the wall below.
- **Run-off staining**: green streaks below copper flashing, orange streaks below iron, white streaks below new mortar or concrete.
- **Washdown pattern**: clean, eroded stone where rain hits. Dark, crusted stone where it does not. In polluted cities this often looks inverted from expectation: the sheltered parts are black and the exposed parts are pale.
- **Splashback zone**: the lowest 30 to 50 cm (12 to 20 in) of walls are stained, mossy, salt-damaged or rotting from rain bouncing off the ground.
- **Gutter failure signature**: localized vertical bands of decay, moss and mortar loss directly below a leaking or overflowing gutter.

### 10.2 Moisture from the ground

- **Rising damp tide line**: a horizontal band 0.5 to 1.5 m (1.5 to 5 ft) above ground where groundwater carrying salts evaporates. Shows as white efflorescence, powdering stone or brick, flaking paint and plaster. Damage peaks at the top edge of the band.
- **Ground-line decay** in wood posts.

### 10.3 Directional patterns

- Windward vs leeward: abrasion, rain penetration and salt on the windward side. Dirt, biology and black crusts on the leeward side.
- Sunny vs shaded faces: see Section 2.2.
- Desert undercutting: walls, columns and rock pillars narrowed at the base by sand abrasion within about 1 m of the ground. Combined with salt damage in the wet ground zone, this produces mushroom-shaped rocks and severely undercut ruins.

### 10.4 Geometry

- **Corners and edges (arrises) round first**: exposed to weather from multiple sides.
- **Projections fail before flat walls**: cornices, sills, string courses, finials, carved ornament and parapets take the most weather.
- **Upper surfaces of ledges (weatherings)**: collect water, snow and debris, eroding fastest.
- **Parapets and chimneys**: exposed on both faces and top. Among the first parts of a masonry building to need rebuilding.

### 10.5 Joints and inclusions

- Soft mortar recessed behind stone: normal sacrificial behavior.
- Hard mortar standing proud while stone erodes around it: incompatible repointing.
- Rust-stained cracks radiating from iron cramps, dowels and anchors.
- Rust spots or pits from pyrite in slate, marble or concrete aggregate.

### 10.6 Bedding and grain

- Differential erosion reveals bedding in sandstone and limestone as ridges and grooves.
- Face-bedded stone peels in sheets.
- Raised grain in weathered wood shows ring structure.

### 10.7 Biology as a moisture map

- Moss, algae and green slime show where surfaces stay wettest.
- Orange lichens (Xanthoria) often indicate nutrient-rich spots: bird perches, nitrogen-rich runoff.
- Black biofilms dominate humid tropics and sheltered damp walls.
- Plants growing from walls indicate failed mortar, trapped soil and persistent water.

## 12. Timelines

Typical progression in a moderate temperate climate unless noted. Real rates vary widely with quality, detailing and maintenance.

### 12.1 Granite (exterior, temperate)

| Age | Condition |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 years | Polish begins to dull. Fresh color. |
| 5 to 25 years | Soiling, early biological films on shaded faces. Polish noticeably duller. |
| 25 to 100 years | Gloss mostly gone on exposed polished faces. Lichens established. Rust spots from iron minerals. Lettering crisp. |
| 100 to 500 years | Slight rounding of sharp arrises. Coastal: granular roughening, early honeycombing. |
| 500 to 2,000+ years | Rounded edges, surface grain relief. Structure sound unless iron fixings or foundations fail. |

### 12.2 Limestone (exterior, clean temperate air)

| Age | Condition |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 years | Bright tooled surface, faint tool marks visible |
| 5 to 25 years | Graying, dirt patterns, first lichens. Tool marks softening. |
| 25 to 100 years | Arrises rounded. Fine carving softened. Shaded areas dark with biology or crusts. Some frost scaling on porous stone. |
| 100 to 300 years | Inscriptions blurred. Carved faces lose features. Soft beds recessed. Projecting moldings need replacement. |
| 300 to 1,000 years | Deep differential erosion. Ornament reduced to lumps. Extensive repairs or replacement stone typical. |

In a polluted 20th-century city, compress the 100 to 300 year stage into 50 to 150 years.

### 12.3 Marble

| Age | Condition |
|---|---|
| 0 to 2 years | Polish gone outdoors |
| 2 to 20 years | Sugaring starts on sun-exposed surfaces. Thin panels may begin bowing. |
| 20 to 50 years | Thin panels visibly bowed, cracking at anchors. Thick blocks show granular surface. |
| 50 to 150 years | Gravestone lettering faded (polluted wet climate). Sugary surfaces crumble at a touch. Black crusts in sheltered areas. |
| 150 to 500 years | Major loss of carved detail outdoors. Indoor marble essentially unchanged. |
| 500 to 2,500 years | In dry Mediterranean climates (Parthenon), massive blocks survive with rounded edges and honey-colored patina (calcium oxalate). |

### 12.4 Sandstone

| Age | Condition (well-cemented / poorly cemented or salt-exposed) |
|---|---|
| 0 to 10 years | Fresh / early salt bloom, first powdering at base |
| 10 to 50 years | Soiling and biology / contour scaling, face-bedded stone delaminating |
| 50 to 150 years | Arrises softened / severe delamination, honeycombing, carved detail lost |
| 150 to 500 years | Differential bedding relief / deep tafoni, structural blocks thinned |
| 500 to 2,000 years | Sheltered carving survives (Petra) / exposed faces sculpted into cellular forms |

### 12.5 Lime mortar

| Age | Condition |
|---|---|
| 0 to 1 year | Still carbonating and hardening |
| 1 to 25 years | Hard surface skin, minor weathering |
| 25 to 75 years | Joints recessing several millimeters on exposed faces |
| 50 to 100 years | Repointing due on exposed walls. Sheltered walls fine. |
| 100+ years | Deep open joints if neglected. Water entry, plant growth, loose stones. |

### 12.6 Reinforced concrete

| Age | Inland temperate | Coastal or de-iced |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1 year | Shrinkage hairline cracks, efflorescence | Same |
| 1 to 10 years | Weathered surface, slight darkening | Chlorides accumulating near surface. Hot coasts: early rust stains in poor concrete. |
| 10 to 25 years | ASR map cracking (if reactive aggregate). Carbonation 10 to 20 mm deep. | Rust stains, cracking over bars, first delamination at corners |
| 25 to 50 years | Carbonation reaching shallow bars. Isolated spalls. | Widespread spalling in splash zone. Major repair or replacement of decks. |
| 50 to 100 years | Spalling where cover was thin. Repairs typical. | Many unprotected structures replaced. Well-designed modern structures reaching design life. |
| 100+ years | Dense well-covered concrete can remain serviceable | Only high-specification or heavily maintained structures survive |

### 12.7 Roman unreinforced concrete

| Age | Condition |
|---|---|
| 0 to 100 years | Slow strengthening as pozzolanic reactions continue |
| 100 to 500 years | Exposed facing stones or bricks weather or are robbed. Core intact. |
| 500 to 2,000 years | Marine harbor cores still cohesive. Pantheon dome (about 125 AD) still standing. |

### 12.8 Glass

| Age | Modern float glass window | Medieval potash stained glass (outdoors, unprotected) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 years | Clean. Sealant and gasket aging begins. | Surface leaching begins |
| 10 to 25 years | Insulated glass seals may fail, fogging. Minor scratching. | Faint haze, early pitting |
| 25 to 50 years | Putty cracking (older windows), frames need repair | Pitting spreads, weathering crusts in polluted air |
| 50 to 200 years | Glass itself unchanged. Frames and glazing compounds replaced several times. | Opaque outer surface, browning of manganese, paint loss |
| 200 to 800 years | Old crown and cylinder glass still in service if frames maintained | Corrosion up to 1 to 2 mm deep. Images barely readable from outside. Protective glazing needed. |

### 12.9 Untreated wood, above ground

| Age | Full sun, dry climate | Shaded, wet climate |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 2 months | Yellowing, darkening | Yellowing, first mildew spots |
| 2 to 12 months | Silver-gray | Dark gray to black, green algae near ground |
| 1 to 5 years | Checking, raised grain, cupping | Moss on horizontal surfaces, soft spots at joints and ends (non-durable species) |
| 5 to 20 years | Deep checks, 1 to 2 mm erosion on soft woods | Decay in joints, ends and lower boards. Non-durable boards need replacing. |
| 20 to 50 years | Ridged weathered surface, still structurally sound | Durable heartwood weathered but sound. Non-durable wood largely failed. |
| 50 to 100+ years | Desert timbers essentially intact apart from surface erosion | Only durable species, well-detailed and maintained, survive |

### 12.10 Wood in ground contact

| Age | Non-durable untreated | Durable heartwood | Pressure-treated (UC4A or better) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 years | Ground-line decay begins | Sound | Sound |
| 3 to 7 years | Post rotted through at ground line | Minor surface softening | Sound |
| 7 to 15 years | Gone | Ground-line decay advancing | Sound, surface weathered |
| 15 to 30 years | | Many posts failed (except locust, Osage orange) | Sound to minor softening |
| 30 to 50 years | | Very durable species still standing | Some failures, especially in wet, warm soils |

### 12.11 Marine timber piling

| Age | Untreated, warm water | Untreated, cold water | Treated (UC5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1 year | Shipworm larvae settle, tunnels begin | Light attack | Sound |
| 1 to 3 years | Pilings riddled, failures begin | Gribble necking in tidal zone | Sound |
| 3 to 10 years | Destroyed | Significant section loss | Surface wear in tidal zone |
| 10 to 25 years | | Failed | Sound to moderate wear |
| 25 to 50+ years | | | Approaching replacement in warm waters, still serviceable in cold |

## 13. Abandoned Structure Decay Sequence

How an unmaintained building comes apart. The roof controls everything: once it fails, water reaches materials that would otherwise last centuries.

| Time since abandonment | Typical changes |
|---|---|
| Months 0 to 6 | Gutters clog with leaves. Broken windows let in rain, birds and animals. Mildew indoors. |
| Years 1 to 3 | Gutters overflow, soaking walls below. Roof leaks start at flashings and valleys. Paint peeling. Vegetation in gutters and cracks. |
| Years 3 to 10 | Roof sheathing and ceilings rot under leaks. Plaster collapses. Floors soften under water paths. Ivy and saplings on walls. |
| Years 10 to 25 | Wood-framed roofs collapse. Interior wood floors fall through. Wood-frame houses lose structural integrity. Trees growing inside. |
| Years 25 to 50 | Wood-frame buildings reduced to foundations, chimneys and debris mounds. Masonry buildings roofless with internal floors gone. Concrete structures show rebar corrosion, spalling, plants rooted in cracks. |
| Years 50 to 100 | Masonry walls lose tops and corners. Parapets and gables fall. Steel-framed and reinforced concrete buildings show major corrosion, partial collapses. |
| Years 100 to 300 | Masonry stands as ruins: walls without roofs, filled with soil and vegetation. Reinforced concrete heavily degraded or collapsed in wet climates. |
| Years 300 to 1,000+ | Low masonry walls, foundations and mounds. Stone survives best, especially massive granite and well-built unreinforced masonry. Glass shards survive in soil. Dry deserts preserve wood and mudbrick far longer. |

**Climate effects on this sequence**
- Hot humid: compress the timeline roughly by half. Vegetation and termites dominate.
- Hot dry: stretch it greatly. Wood and even adobe can persist for centuries if protected from rain.
- Cold freeze-thaw: masonry and concrete degrade faster, wood decays somewhat slower.
- Coastal: steel and reinforced concrete degrade much faster. Salt crystallization accelerates masonry decay.

## 14. Case Studies

**Cleopatra's Needle, New York City**
Red Aswan granite obelisk, carved around 1450 BC. Stood in Heliopolis and Alexandria for over 3,000 years. Moved to Central Park in 1881. Within a few decades its surface flaked and inscriptions on some faces faded noticeably. Contributing factors: salts absorbed during centuries near the Alexandria waterfront, New York's frequent freeze-thaw cycles and humidity, coal-era pollution. A paraffin wax coating applied in the 1880s to protect it may have trapped moisture and salts. The London twin, also on a damp, polluted riverside, shows similar weathering.

**Great Sphinx of Giza**
Carved from bedrock limestone of varying hardness. Middle layers deeply recessed. Rising groundwater and humidity from the expanding city and irrigation feed salts into the stone. Twentieth-century repairs with incompatible mortars accelerated decay. Wind-blown sand abrasion and periodic burial by sand both shaped its condition. Much of its history the body lay buried, which protected it.

**St Paul's Cathedral, London**
Portland limestone. Lead plugs set in the balustrade in the early 1700s stood about 20 mm proud by the 1980s, equal to roughly 8 mm per century of stone loss during the coal era. Rates fell sharply after clean air legislation.

**Aon Center, Chicago**
Thin Carrara marble panels on an 83-story tower. Bowing from thermal hysteresis appeared within about 15 years. Full granite recladding in the early 1990s at roughly 80 million dollars.

**Parthenon, Athens**
Pentelic marble, about 2,400 years old. Surfaces developed a golden calcium oxalate patina. The greatest damage came from a 1687 gunpowder explosion, later removal of sculpture, rusting iron clamps installed in early 20th-century restoration and late 20th-century air pollution.

**Petra, Jordan**
Sandstone tomb facades about 2,000 years old. Exposed faces show honeycomb weathering, salt crystallization and wind erosion. Sheltered locations within narrow canyons preserve carving far better. Flash floods and rising water tables add to salt damage.

**Roman harbors (Caesarea Maritima, Portus Cosanus and others)**
Unreinforced Roman marine concrete still cohesive after about 2,000 years in seawater, strengthened by minerals growing within it.

**San Francisco Bay shipworm invasion, 1917 to 1921**
Shipworms spread rapidly through the bay, destroying untreated timber wharves and ferry slips within a few years. Prompted wider adoption of creosote-treated and concrete pilings.

**Vasa warship, Stockholm**
Oak warship that sank in 1628 in cold, low-salinity Baltic water where shipworms are scarce. Raised in 1961 largely intact. Since then, sulfur compounds absorbed from the seabed have oxidized into acid inside the wood, an ongoing conservation challenge.

**Norwegian stave churches**
Pine timber buildings from the 12th and 13th centuries, kept alive by tar coatings, raised sills and continuous maintenance. Of perhaps over a thousand originally built, fewer than 30 survive.

**Ancestral Puebloan structures, US Southwest**
Wooden roof beams from roughly 800 to 1300 AD survive in dry rock shelters and pueblos. Dry climate prevents decay; UV and sand only affect exposed surfaces. These beams are the foundation of North American tree-ring dating.

**Medieval stained glass, York Minster and Chartres**
Potash glass weathered heavily over centuries: pitting, crusts and browning. Major restorations add external protective glazing to stop further rain and condensation contact.

**Morandi Bridge, Genoa (collapsed 2018)**
Reinforced and prestressed concrete bridge from the 1960s in a coastal, industrial environment. Investigations focused heavily on corrosion of steel tendons inside concrete-encased stays, a failure mode difficult to see from outside.
