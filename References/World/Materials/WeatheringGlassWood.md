## 7. Glass

### 7.1 Composition matters

| Glass type | Era | Composition | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roman soda-lime | 1st century BC onward | Soda (from mineral natron), lime, silica | Good |
| Medieval northern European potash ("forest glass") | About 10th to 16th centuries | Potash from wood and fern ash, high lime, low silica (often near 50%) | Poor. Weathers heavily outdoors. |
| Venetian cristallo | 15th century onward | Purified soda, silica | Moderate to good |
| Unstable 17th-century glass | Late 1600s | Excess alkali, too little lime | Very poor ("crizzling") |
| Modern soda-lime float | 1950s onward | About 72% silica, 13 to 15% soda, about 10% lime | Very good |
| Borosilicate | 20th century | Silica plus boron oxide | Excellent chemical and thermal resistance |

### 7.2 Chemical weathering

**Stage 1: ion exchange (leaching)**
- Water penetrates the surface. Sodium or potassium ions swap with hydrogen ions from water.
- A hydrated, silica-rich gel layer forms on the surface.
- Over centuries this layer can split into many microscopically thin laminations. Light interfering between them produces the rainbow iridescence on excavated Roman glass. The laminations flake off as shiny scales.

**Stage 2: network dissolution**
- When water sits on glass without draining, leached alkali raises its pH. Above about pH 9, the silica network itself dissolves.
- Glass sheets stored stacked in damp conditions develop permanent stains and etching within weeks to months.
- Alkaline runoff from new concrete, mortar or masonry etches windows below it, a common defect on new buildings.

**Weathering crusts and pitting** (mainly medieval potash glass)
- Crusts of gypsum, syngenite and calcite form on outer surfaces in polluted air.
- Pitting (crater corrosion) eats into the surface. Some medieval panels have lost more than 1 mm of thickness and become opaque from the outside.
- **Browning**: manganese in the glass oxidizes to dark brown compounds, obscuring painted figures.
- Great cathedral windows (York Minster, Chartres, Canterbury, Cologne) have been fitted since the 1970s and 1980s with external protective glazing, with the historic glass hung in ventilated space just inside.

**Crizzling ("sick glass")**
- Unstable 17th-century glass develops fine networks of cracks and sweats droplets of alkaline liquid in humid air. Museums control humidity to slow it.

### 7.3 Solarization (sun-driven color change)

- Manganese was added as a decolorizer to clear glass in the United States from roughly 1880 to 1915. UV gradually oxidizes it, turning the glass pale lilac to deep purple over years to decades. Bottles lying in desert sun ("desert amethyst glass") show the strongest effect.
- Selenium-decolorized glass (used after manganese supplies were disrupted around World War I) turns amber or straw yellow in sun.
- Modern float glass with low manganese shows little solarization.

### 7.4 Mechanical wear

- **Sandblasting**: desert winds frost and pit windows and windshields. Lower panes and vehicle windshields suffer most.
- **Sea glass**: broken glass tumbled by waves and leached by seawater develops a frosted surface. Fully frosted, smooth sea glass usually takes 20 to 50 years in the surf.
- **Scratching**: from cleaning grit, wiper blades, windblown debris.
- **Impact**: hail and windborne debris in storms. Hurricane-zone codes require laminated or impact-rated glazing.

### 7.5 Thermal stress breakage

- When the center of a pane heats in sun while edges stay cool inside the frame, the edges are pulled into tension.
- Ordinary annealed glass can crack when the temperature difference reaches roughly 30 to 40°C (54 to 72°F).
- Highest risk: tinted or body-absorbing glass, partially shaded panes (tree or overhang shadow), dark blinds or films close to the glass, low winter sun on cold mornings.
- Cracks typically start at the edge, at right angles to it, then wander across the pane.
- Heat-strengthened and tempered glass tolerate far larger temperature differences.

### 7.6 Nickel sulfide inclusions

- Microscopic nickel sulfide stones in tempered glass slowly change crystal form after installation, expanding by a few percent.
- Tempered panes can shatter spontaneously months to years later with no external cause. The break pattern shows a characteristic "butterfly" at the origin.
- Heat soak testing during manufacture reduces the risk.

### 7.7 Seals, coatings and glazing compounds

- **Insulated glass units (double and triple glazing)**: edge seals typically last 10 to 25 years. Sun-baked frames and frames holding water fail sooner. Failure shows as fogging, condensation and mineral deposits between panes.
- **Linseed oil putty**: hardens, shrinks and cracks over 20 to 50 years. Panes rattle and leak.
- **Silicone sealant**: commonly 20+ years. Polysulfide and polyurethane sealants usually less.
- **Low-E and reflective coatings**: soft coatings corrode if exposed at failed edge seals. Hard coatings scratch.
- **Frames**: steel frames rust and crack glass as they expand. Aluminum frames corrode in marine air. Wood frames rot at bottom rails and sills.

### 7.8 The myth of flowing glass

- Old windows are often thicker at the bottom. This comes from manufacturing, not flow.
- Crown glass (spun from a molten disk) and cylinder glass (blown and flattened) were uneven. Glaziers often set the heavy edge down for stability, though not always; some old panes are thicker at the top or sides.
- At room temperature the viscosity of window glass is so high that measurable sagging would take far longer than the age of the universe.
- Roman glass, ancient Egyptian glass beads and centuries-old telescope lenses show no flow.

### 7.9 Glass by environment

| Environment | Main wear |
|---|---|
| Coastal | Salt film stays damp, causing alkali etch and haze. Frame and hardware corrosion. Seal degradation. |
| Sunny | Thermal stress breakage, UV degradation of seals and gaskets, solarization of older glass |
| Windy and sandy | Sandblast frosting and pitting, debris impact, deflection under gusts |
| Humid sheltered | Condensation, mold on frame residues, long wetness on sills |
| Polluted urban | Crusts on historic glass, grime films, acidic deposits |
| Freeze-thaw | Frame damage, putty and sealant cracking |

**Rates**
- Modern float glass in a window: negligible surface loss over centuries if kept draining and clean.
- Medieval potash glass outdoors: corrosion layers 0.1 to 2 mm deep over 600 to 800 years.
- Buried Roman glass: weathering layers from micrometers to over a millimeter depending on soil moisture and chemistry.

## 8. Untreated Wood

### 8.1 Wood structure that controls weathering

- **Cellulose**: long strong fibers. Resists UV fairly well.
- **Lignin**: about 20 to 30% of wood. Binds the fibers together. Highly sensitive to UV.
- **Earlywood vs latewood**: each annual ring has a soft, light earlywood band and a dense, dark latewood band.
- **Heartwood vs sapwood**: heartwood often contains natural extractives that resist decay. Sapwood almost never does.
- **End grain**: absorbs water many times faster than side grain, making board ends, post tops and joints the first places to decay.

### 8.2 Surface weathering (no rot)

**Color sequence**
1. **Weeks 1 to 8**: light woods yellow and darken to tan or brown as UV breaks down lignin. Dark woods (walnut, redwood) often lighten.
2. **Months 2 to 12**: rain washes out broken-down lignin, leaving loose cellulose fibers. In sunny, dry climates the surface turns **silver-gray**. In damp climates, mildew fungi (especially Aureobasidium pullulans) colonize the surface, turning it **dark gray to nearly black**.
3. **Years 1 to 2**: color stabilizes. Further change is slow.

**Depth of damage**
- UV penetrates only about 75 micrometers. Visible light degrades slightly deeper, to around 200 micrometers.
- Discoloration extends 0.5 to 2.5 mm.
- The weathered gray layer is thin. Planing or sanding 1 to 2 mm reveals fresh wood.

**Surface erosion rates**
- Most softwoods: about 6 mm (1/4 in) per century.
- Low-density woods such as western red cedar: up to about 12 mm per century.
- Dense hardwoods: about 3 mm per century.
- Hot, sunny, sand-blasted desert locations erode faster.

**Physical wear patterns**
- **Raised grain ("corduroy")**: soft earlywood erodes faster than dense latewood, leaving ridges.
- **Proud knots**: knots are denser than surrounding wood and stand above the eroded surface.
- **Checking**: cracks along the grain from repeated wetting and drying. Worst on sun-baked faces and flat-sawn boards. Checks let water in, inviting decay.
- **Cupping and warping**: boards curl across their width. Flat-sawn boards cup away from the heart side.
- **Splitting at nails**: shrinkage and fastener corrosion split board ends.
- **Iron stains**: tannins in oak, cedar, redwood and chestnut react with iron fasteners, producing blue-black streaks.

### 8.3 Decay (rot)

**Conditions required**
- Moisture content above about 20% for extended periods. Fiber saturation is about 28 to 30%. Optimum fungal activity is well above that.
- Oxygen.
- Temperature roughly 10 to 35°C (50 to 95°F).
- Food (the wood itself).

**Consequences**
- Wood kept dry (under 20% moisture) can last essentially indefinitely. Roof timbers in sound buildings routinely last centuries.
- Wood kept fully waterlogged without oxygen also survives for centuries: Venetian foundation piles, Viking ships buried in clay, the Swedish warship Vasa (sank 1628, raised 1961, preserved in the cold brackish Baltic where shipworms are rare). Anaerobic bacteria still degrade waterlogged wood slowly, softening outer layers.
- Wood that alternates between wet and dry, or stays damp with air available, decays fastest.

**Rot types**

| Type | Appearance | Typical targets |
|---|---|---|
| Brown rot | Brown, shrunken, cracks into cubes, crumbles to powder | Softwoods. Most building decay in North America and Europe. |
| Dry rot (Serpula lacrymans) | Brown rot with white cottony mycelium and thick strands. Spreads through masonry to reach new wood. | Damp, poorly ventilated buildings in Britain and Europe |
| White rot | Bleached, spongy, stringy, fibrous | Hardwoods, some softwoods |
| Soft rot | Surface softening, fine cracking, gray-brown, in very wet conditions | Cooling towers, ground contact, wood repeatedly wetted |

**Where decay starts**
- Ground line of posts (soil surface down to about 30 cm below): air and moisture both present.
- End grain of posts, rafters and beams embedded in damp masonry.
- Sill plates and bottom plates on masonry.
- Bottom rails of windows and doors.
- Horizontal surfaces and joints that trap water (deck boards over joists, mortise and tenon joints).
- Behind trim and at flashing failures.
- The bottom 30 to 50 cm of siding, within rain splashback range.

### 8.4 Insects

- **Subterranean termites**: build mud tubes up foundations. Hollow out wood along the grain, leaving a thin outer shell. Warm and temperate regions.
- **Drywood termites**: live entirely inside wood, push out small hard pellets. Warm coastal climates (Florida, Southern California, Hawaii, tropics).
- **Carpenter ants**: excavate smooth galleries in already damp or decayed wood. Leave sawdust piles.
- **Powderpost beetles**: tiny round exit holes and fine flour-like powder. Mainly hardwood sapwood.
- **Old house borer and similar long-horned beetles**: oval exit holes, galleries packed with frass in softwood framing.

### 8.5 Marine borers

- **Shipworms (Teredo and relatives)**: bivalve mollusks that bore tunnels inside wood, lining them with shell. Tunnels can exceed 1 m in length while the surface shows only pinholes. Pilings look sound until they snap.
- **Gribbles (Limnoria)**: tiny crustaceans that burrow at the surface in vast numbers. They erode pilings most heavily in the tidal zone, producing an hourglass or "necked" shape.
- **Sphaeroma**: larger isopods boring in brackish and warm waters.
- Borers are absent in freshwater and scarce in very cold or low-salinity water. Warm marine water is the worst case.
- Untreated softwood pilings in warm seawater can fail within 1 to 3 years.
- **San Francisco Bay, 1917 to 1921**: an invasion of the shipworm Teredo navalis destroyed wharves, piers and ferry slips around the bay, causing tens of millions of dollars of damage at the time.

### 8.6 Salt effects on wood

- Salt inhibits most decay fungi, so salt-saturated wood rots slowly.
- Salt crystallizing in surface cells pries fibers apart, producing a hairy, fuzzy surface (defibration). Common on seawall timbers, coastal decks and salt storage buildings.
- Hygroscopic salt keeps wood damp and accelerates fastener corrosion. Corroding iron then degrades the wood around it (dark, soft, brittle rings around nails and bolts).

### 8.7 Natural durability of heartwood

| Class | Examples | Untreated ground contact life (approximate) |
|---|---|---|
| Very durable | Black locust, Osage orange, ipe, teak, greenheart, ironbark, old-growth redwood heartwood, Pacific yew | 25 to 50+ years |
| Durable | White oak, western red cedar, eastern red cedar, bald cypress (old-growth), black walnut, chestnut | 10 to 25 years |
| Moderately durable | Douglas fir heartwood, larch, second-growth redwood and cedar | 5 to 15 years |
| Non-durable | Pine, spruce, fir, hemlock, birch, maple, poplar, beech, all sapwood | 1 to 5 years |

Above-ground exposed wood lasts several times longer than ground contact. Wood that sheds water, stays off the ground and dries quickly can last 50 to 150+ years even untreated.

### 8.8 Untreated wood by environment

| Environment | Main wear | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Hot dry desert | UV, checking, sand erosion, very little rot | Wood survives centuries. Ancestral Puebloan roof beams from roughly 800 to 1300 AD remain in place and are used for tree-ring dating. |
| Hot humid tropics | Rapid fungal decay, termites, mold | Non-durable wood fails in a few years outdoors |
| Temperate rainy (Pacific Northwest, Britain, Scandinavia coasts) | Decay, moss, mildew, especially on shaded faces | Gray-black weathering. Decay at joints and ground line. |
| Cold dry continental | Slow decay, UV and checking in sunny areas | Long life above ground |
| Coastal marine air | Salt defibration, fastener corrosion, reduced rot | Fuzzy gray surfaces, rust stains, loose fasteners |
| Marine water (tidal, submerged) | Shipworms, gribbles | Fast failure in warm water, slower in cold |

**Orientation on a wooden building (Northern Hemisphere)**
- South and west faces: silver-gray, deeply ridged grain, heavy checking, paint failure first.
- North faces: dark gray to black, mildew, moss and lichen, soft spots near the base, slower erosion.
- Bottom boards: decay and splashback staining.
- Roof surfaces: fastest erosion and checking. Shingles on north slopes grow moss and rot. South slopes curl, split and erode thin.

### 8.9 Wood shingles and shakes

- Untreated cedar shingles: 15 to 30 years on roofs, 30 to 50+ years on walls (walls shed water faster and receive less direct sun).
- Shade, overhanging trees and humid climates shorten roof life. Moss holds water and lifts shingle edges.

## 9. Treated, Coated and Modified Wood

### 9.1 Surface coatings

| Finish | Typical service life before refinishing (exterior, vertical) | Failure pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Quality acrylic latex paint | 7 to 12 years | Chalking, fading, eventual cracking along the grain |
| Oil-based paint (older) | 5 to 10 years | Embrittles, alligators (cracks into squares), peels in layers |
| Solid-color stain | 5 to 8 years | Fades, wears thin, some peeling |
| Semi-transparent penetrating stain | 3 to 5 years | Fades and erodes. No peeling. |
| Clear film (varnish, spar urethane) | 1 to 3 years | UV passes through and degrades wood beneath. Film lifts, cracks and flakes, leaving gray patches. |
| Water repellent only | 1 to 2 years | Wood grays normally underneath |
| Pine tar (traditional) | 3 to 10 years depending on exposure | Darkens, erodes, needs periodic recoating |

Horizontal surfaces (decks, rails, sills) wear two to three times faster than vertical surfaces. Paint fails first on sun-facing walls and wherever moisture pushes from behind (behind gutters, below windows, over bathrooms and kitchens with poor vapor control).

### 9.2 Preservative treatments

**History**
- **Creosote** (from the 1830s onward): railroad ties, utility poles, marine pilings. Black, oily, strong smell. Bleeds in hot sun.
- **Pentachlorophenol**: utility poles, heavy timber. Now largely phased out.
- **Chromated copper arsenate (CCA)**: from the 1930s. Green tint. Residential use ended in the United States at the end of 2003. Still used for poles, pilings and marine structures.
- **Alkaline copper quaternary (ACQ), copper azole (CA), micronized copper (MCA)**: replaced CCA for residential lumber.
- **Borates**: very effective against fungi and insects but leach out in rain. Used indoors and in protected framing.
- **Copper naphthenate**: brush-on treatment for field cuts.

**Use categories (American Wood Protection Association)**
- UC1 and UC2: interior, dry or occasionally damp
- UC3A and UC3B: exterior above ground (coated or uncoated)
- UC4A: ground contact, general use
- UC4B: ground contact, heavy duty or critical
- UC4C: extreme ground contact (tropical, wet soil)
- UC5A, UC5B, UC5C: marine. Higher numbers for warmer waters with greater borer pressure.

**Service lives**

| Item | Typical life |
|---|---|
| Treated fence post, ground contact | 25 to 50 years |
| Treated utility pole | 40 to 60+ years |
| Creosoted railroad tie | 25 to 40 years (shorter in hot, humid climates) |
| Treated deck framing | 25 to 40+ years (decking surface often replaced sooner from weathering) |
| Marine piling, CCA or creosote | 25 to 50+ years in cold water, less in warm water |
| Dual-treated marine piling (CCA plus creosote) | 40 to 75 years in high-borer areas |

**Wear patterns specific to treated wood**
- Fresh green or brown tint fades to the same gray as untreated wood within 6 to 12 months of sun. Treatment prevents rot and insects, not UV weathering.
- Surface still checks, cups and erodes. Deep checks in large timbers expose untreated interior wood, which can rot even inside treated members. Incising (small slits cut before treatment) improves penetration.
- Field cuts and drilled holes not re-treated become decay entry points.
- Copper-based treatments corrode ordinary steel fasteners. Hot-dip galvanized (G185 or better) or stainless steel fasteners are needed. Type 316 stainless is standard for marine exposure.
- Creosote bleeds and becomes sticky in hot sun.
- Fire-retardant-treated plywood used as roof sheathing in the 1980s in the United States lost strength in hot attics as acidic salts degraded the wood, leading to widespread roof replacement.

### 9.3 Modified wood

- **Thermally modified wood** (heated to roughly 180 to 230°C): improved decay resistance and dimensional stability. More brittle. Weathers to gray like untreated wood.
- **Acetylated wood** (brand example: Accoya): chemically modified to resist moisture uptake. Very stable, highly durable. Still turns gray in sun.
- **Charred wood (yakisugi, also called shou sugi ban)**: traditional Japanese surface charring. The char layer resists UV, insects and some decay. Char erodes gradually over decades. Charred cladding commonly lasts 50 to 80+ years with re-oiling.

### 9.4 Historic durability strategies

- **Norwegian stave churches**: Urnes stave church dates to around 1130. Survival due to tar coatings renewed for centuries, raised sills on stone foundations and steep overhanging roofs.
- **Ise Grand Shrine, Japan**: deliberately rebuilt every 20 years for over 1,300 years, transferring craft skill across generations rather than expecting the timber to last.
- **Hōryū-ji, Japan**: wooden buildings dating to around the 7th century, among the oldest surviving wooden structures, kept sound by deep roofs, stone bases and continuous maintenance.
- **Covered bridges**: a roof protects the structural truss. Covered timber bridges last 100 to 200 years. Uncovered timber trusses of the same era typically decayed in 10 to 20 years.
- **Design over chemistry**: overhangs, raised foundations, sloped tops on posts, drip edges and ventilation often matter more than species or treatment.
