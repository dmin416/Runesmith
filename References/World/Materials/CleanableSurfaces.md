## 1. Surface Science Fundamentals

### 1.1 The five properties that decide cleanability

| Property | Why it matters | Hygienic target |
|---|---|---|
| Porosity / water absorption | Pores hold water, urine, oils and microbes where cleaning cannot reach | 0% (glass, metal, fluoropolymer) or at most 0.5% (impervious ceramic) |
| Surface roughness (Ra) | Bacteria are 0.5 to 2 µm; scratches and pits at that scale shelter them | Ra 0.8 µm or lower (food/pharma hygienic design); 0.2 to 0.4 µm premium; electropolished steel under 0.25 µm |
| Surface energy | Low-energy surfaces resist wetting and adhesion of soils | Under about 25 mN/m for true non-stick |
| Durability | A surface eroded by cleaners or abrasion becomes rough then porous | Must survive bleach, acids, alkalis and scrubbing for decades |
| Geometry | Seams, corners, overlaps, fastener heads and joints trap soil | Internal radii 3 mm minimum (EHEDG); coved floor-to-wall junctions 25 mm radius or larger; continuous seams |

Geometry and durability usually matter more than chemistry. A perfectly smooth surface with a grout line or a caulk joint every 300 mm is only as clean as its joints.

### 1.2 Surface energy and contact angle

Young's equation: γ_SV = γ_SL + γ_LV · cos θ

- θ is the contact angle of a liquid drop on the surface.
- θ under 90° = wetting (hydrophilic); θ over 90° = non-wetting (hydrophobic); over 150° with sliding angle under 10° = superhydrophobic.
- A liquid spreads on a solid when the liquid's surface tension is lower than the solid's critical surface tension (Zisman). Water is 72.8 mN/m at 20 °C; oils are 25 to 35 mN/m. That is why very few surfaces repel oil.

| Material | Critical surface tension / surface energy (mN/m) | Water contact angle |
|---|---|---|
| PTFE (Teflon) | 18 to 20 | 108 to 115° |
| FEP / PFA | 17 to 19 | 110 to 115° |
| Silicone (PDMS) | 20 to 24 | 100 to 110° |
| PVDF | 25 | 80 to 90° |
| Polypropylene | 29 to 31 | 95 to 100° |
| Polyethylene | 31 | 95 to 100° |
| Polystyrene | 33 | 85 to 90° |
| PMMA (acrylic) | 39 to 41 | 65 to 75° |
| PVC | 39 | 80 to 85° |
| Epoxy (cured) | 43 to 47 | 70 to 80° |
| Nylon 6,6 | 42 to 46 | 65 to 70° |
| Clean glass | above 70 (very high) | under 20° |
| Clean metals | hundreds to over 1000 mJ/m² | near 0° when pristine; 60 to 90° once airborne organics adsorb |

### 1.3 Roughness and wetting regimes

- **Wenzel regime**: liquid fills the texture. Roughness amplifies the native behavior: hydrophilic becomes more wetting, hydrophobic becomes more repellent.
- **Cassie-Baxter regime**: liquid sits on trapped air pockets. Produces superhydrophobicity.
- **Lotus effect**: hierarchical micro bumps (10 to 20 µm) topped with nano wax crystals. Drops roll and collect dirt. Weakness: mechanical abrasion, oils and surfactants collapse the air layer. Superhydrophobic coatings are rarely durable enough for floors or toilets.

### 1.4 Ceramic porosity classes (ASTM C373 water absorption)

| Class | Water absorption | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Impervious | 0.5% or less | Porcelain tile, vitreous china sanitary ware, sintered stone |
| Vitreous | 0.5 to 3% | Some floor tiles |
| Semi-vitreous | 3 to 7% | Older floor tile |
| Non-vitreous | over 7% | Wall tile bodies (glaze carries all protection), terracotta |

Natural stone (typical absorption): granite 0.1 to 0.5%; slate 0.1 to 0.4%; marble 0.1 to 0.5% but dissolves in acids including urine and vinegar; limestone 0.5 to 10%; travertine 2 to 5% plus open voids; sandstone 2 to 10%. Unsealed concrete 4 to 8%.

### 1.5 Biofilm formation

1. Conditioning film: proteins and organics adsorb within seconds to minutes.
2. Reversible attachment of cells.
3. Irreversible attachment within hours; cells secrete extracellular polymeric substance (EPS).
4. Mature biofilm: commonly 10 to 1000 times more tolerant of disinfectants than free cells.

Mineral scale (calcium carbonate from hard water; calcium phosphate, struvite and uric acid from urine) roughens surfaces then hosts biofilm. The strongest hygiene strategy is smooth + impervious + scale-resistant + seamless, with frequent mechanical cleaning. Biocidal additives are secondary.

## 3. Non-Stick and Anti-Fouling Technologies

| Technology | Mechanism | Durability | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluoropolymer (PTFE, PFA, FEP) | Lowest common surface energy (18 to 20 mN/m); C-F bonds | Moderate; soft and scratchable | Cookware, industrial chutes, release surfaces |
| Silicone / foul-release | Low energy plus low modulus so soils peel | Moderate | Molds, marine hulls, gaskets |
| Sol-gel ceramic (silica-based) | Hard glassy film, moderately low energy | Hard but loses release properties faster than PTFE | "Ceramic" cookware; architectural easy-clean |
| Fluorosilane easy-clean | Covalently bonded monolayer on glass or ceramic, nanometers thick | Years on shower glass | Shower glass, tile, sanitary ware |
| Nano-glaze ceramics | Ultra-smooth fired glaze closing micro-irregularities | Permanent (part of glaze) | Toilets, urinals, basins |
| Photocatalytic TiO2 | UV light creates superhydrophilic surface plus oxidizes organics | Permanent; needs UV-A | Exterior glass, lit toilet bowls |
| Superhydrophobic (lotus) | Air pockets in hierarchical texture | Poor under abrasion and oil | Lab demos, some exterior coatings |
| SLIPS / liquid-infused surfaces | Porous or textured solid holding a lubricant film; inspired by pitcher plants | Lubricant depletes; replenishable | Containers, medical tubing, toilet research |
| LESS (liquid-entrenched smooth surface) | Grafted molecular layer holding a thin lubricant | Reported to reduce adhesion of feces enough to cut flush water substantially (Penn State, 2019) | Toilets (research to commercial) |
| Hydrophilic anti-fouling (PEG, zwitterionic polymers) | Tightly bound water layer blocks protein adsorption | Moderate | Medical devices, membranes |
| Microtopography (Sharklet type) | Diamond micro-pattern inspired by shark skin disrupts bacterial attachment | Permanent texture | Hospital touch surfaces |
| Seasoned carbon steel / cast iron | Polymerized oil film | Renewable | Cookware |
| Hard anodized aluminum | Hard oxide layer, often PTFE impregnated | High | Cookware, machine parts |
| Nitriding, DLC, PVD ceramics | Very hard low-friction layers | Very high | Faucets, tools, molds |
| Electropolishing | Removes micro-peaks; chromium-rich passive film | Permanent | Stainless sanitary fixtures |

### 3.1 PTFE facts

- Coefficient of friction 0.04 to 0.10, among the lowest of any solid.
- Continuous service to 260 °C. Decomposition accelerates above about 350 °C; fumes cause polymer fume fever in humans and are lethal to pet birds.
- Applied over grit-blasted metal with a primer; reinforced grades include ceramic or metal particles.
- Production historically used PFOA, phased out in the US by 2015; replacements include other fluorinated processing aids. Fluoropolymers fall under the broad PFAS definition being considered for restriction in the EU; several US states restrict PFAS in cookware.
- Creeps (cold flows) under sustained load.
- Too soft for floors, toilets or anything scrubbed with abrasives.

## 4. Toilets

### 4.1 Where toilets get dirty

1. Under the rim (urine scale, biofilm, hidden from cleaning)
2. Waterline ring (hard water mineral plus biofilm)
3. Trapway interior (unglazed or partially glazed in cheap models)
4. Exterior trap curves and bolt caps
5. Seat hinges and the seat-to-bowl gap
6. Floor around the base (grout absorbs urine)
7. Air: flush aerosol plume

### 4.2 Body material ranking with unlimited manufacturing

| Rank | Material | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Vitreous china with nano-smooth, fully fired glaze inside and outside, including trapway | Proven, impervious glaze, scale resistant, warm look, quiet | Chip exposes body (0.5% absorption) |
| 2 | Full-body glass-ceramic or cast borosilicate | Zero porosity through entire thickness, chip-tolerant hygienically, optically perfect surface | Complex trapways very hard to cast and anneal; impact brittle; extremely expensive |
| 3 | Electropolished 316L stainless or titanium | Unbreakable, no chips, seamless welds, bleach-tolerant (titanium) | Cold touch, water spotting, scratches dull it, noisier |
| 4 | Full-body porcelain stoneware | Impervious through thickness | Harder to form than vitreous china |
| 5 | Solid surface or polymer | Renewable by sanding | Scratches; not suitable for bowl interior long term |

Optimal combination: vitreous or full-body porcelain body, nano-glaze, photocatalytic layer activated by a UV lamp in the lid, an anti-adhesion molecular layer in the bowl, electrolyzed water mist after each use. Commercial examples of these individual features exist: TOTO CeFiONtect (ultra-smooth glaze), TOTO Actilight (UV plus photocatalytic glaze), TOTO EWATER+ (hypochlorous acid mist), Duravit HygieneGlaze and American Standard EverClean (antimicrobial glazes), Villeroy & Boch CeramicPlus (easy-clean glaze).

### 4.3 Glaze science

- Glaze is a glass layer roughly 0.3 to 1 mm thick fused at 1200 to 1250 °C onto the clay body.
- Main components: silica (glass former), alumina (viscosity and hardness), alkali and alkaline earth fluxes, zirconium silicate (white opacifier).
- Standard glazes have microscopic undulations, pinholes and crawling defects. Premium glazes add finer milling, controlled firing and ion-barrier layers to reduce surface irregularity at the nanometer scale.
- Glaze is harder than most abrasive cleaners but pumice stones and metal scrapers can still scratch it; scratches seed scale.

### 4.4 Bowl geometry

- **Rimless (open rim)**: removes the hidden rim channel, the single largest hygiene improvement in modern toilets.
- **Fully glazed trapway**: smoother path, less clogging and staining.
- **Skirted / concealed trapway**: smooth exterior, no curves to clean.
- **Wall-hung on an in-wall carrier**: floor underneath fully exposed and cleanable; tank concealed in the wall; height adjustable. Use a carrier rated for at least 500 lb (227 kg); heavy-duty models reach about 1000 lb.
- **Elongated bowl**: larger target, better hygiene.
- **Large water surface**: soils land in water instead of dry ceramic. Siphonic bowls have large water spots; European washdown bowls are smaller; old "shelf" bowls are the worst design.
- **Trapway diameter**: 2 to 2-1/8 in (51 to 54 mm) resists clogging.
- **Seat height**: 17 to 19 in (432 to 483 mm) to top of seat for accessible "comfort height."

### 4.5 Flush technology

| Type | How it works | Water | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gravity siphonic | Water fills trapway, starts siphon | 0.8 to 1.28 gpf (3 to 4.8 L) | Quiet, large water spot | Can clog at very low volumes |
| Gravity washdown | Water pushes waste over weir | 4 to 6 L | Robust, simple | Smaller water spot, more bowl soiling |
| Cyclonic / tornado | Angled jets swirl water around open rim | 1.0 to 1.28 gpf | Excellent bowl scouring | Proprietary |
| Pressure-assisted | Air-pressurized tank vessel | 1.0 to 1.6 gpf | Very powerful, clog resistant | Loud |
| Flushometer valve | Direct line pressure, no tank | 1.1 to 1.6 gpf | Commercial durability, rapid reuse | Needs 1 in supply and high flow |
| Vacuum | Differential pressure pulls waste | about 0.5 to 1.2 L | Minimal water; any pipe direction | Pump, noise, maintenance |
| Dual flush | Two volumes | e.g. 4 / 2.6 L | Savings | Small partial volume may leave residue |

Performance metrics:
- **MaP (Maximum Performance) test**: grams of soybean-paste test media removed in one flush. 1000 g is the test maximum. 800 g plus is excellent.
- **US EPA WaterSense**: 1.28 gpf (4.8 L) or less and at least 350 g MaP.
- US federal maximum: 1.6 gpf (6 L).

### 4.6 Aerosol plume

- A University of Colorado Boulder study (2022) visualized a commercial lidless toilet throwing an aerosol plume about 1.5 m high within 8 seconds of flushing.
- Mitigations: lid closed before flush (ideally automatic), lower turbulence flush designs, continuous exhaust ventilation near or behind the toilet, UV or electrolyzed water treatment of the bowl, ceiling-level exhaust in bathrooms.

### 4.7 Seats

| Seat material | Notes |
|---|---|
| Duroplast (urea-formaldehyde thermoset) | Hard, scratch resistant, non-porous, glossy; best standard seat |
| Polypropylene | Flexible, cheap, scratches, dulls |
| Wood (molded or solid) | Paint chips expose porous core; avoid in sanitary use |
| Heated bidet seat | Electronics; choose stainless nozzle, self-cleaning cycle, UV or electrolyzed water nozzle cleaning |

Hinges: 304 or 316 stainless, quick-release for full removal and cleaning. US plumbing codes require open-front seats on public water closets.

### 4.8 Installation hygiene

- Sealed waste connection: wax ring or wax-free rubber/foam gasket; flange set flush with finished floor.
- Base perimeter sealed with sanitary (fungicide) silicone so urine cannot wick under the toilet. Many installers leave a small rear gap to reveal leaks.
- Wall-hung eliminates this entirely.
- Floor under and around toilet: impervious porcelain or seamless resin, not cementitious grout.

### 4.9 Best toilet specification

- Wall-hung, rimless, elongated, fully glazed including trapway
- Vitreous china or porcelain stoneware with nano-smooth glaze plus photocatalytic or anti-adhesion layer
- Cyclonic or siphonic flush, 1.0 to 1.28 gpf, MaP 800 g or higher
- Concealed in-wall carrier and tank with front access panel
- Integrated bidet with stainless self-cleaning nozzle, warm water, air dryer, deodorizer
- Auto-close lid before flush; UV lamp and electrolyzed water mist cycle
- Duroplast seat, quick-release stainless hinges
- Dedicated continuous exhaust near the fixture

## 5. Urinals

### 5.1 Urine chemistry and why urinals stink

- Urine: about 95% water; urea 9 to 23 g/L; chloride, sodium, potassium, phosphate, sulfate, creatinine, uric acid; fresh pH about 6.
- Bacterial urease converts urea to ammonia and CO2. pH rises to about 9.
- At high pH, **struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate) and **calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite)** precipitate as hard scale in traps and drain lines. Uric acid crystals add more.
- Ammonia is the main odor. Scale roughens surfaces, traps more urine, more urease bacteria grow, cycle repeats.
- Ammonia attacks copper and brass. Use PVC, ABS, HDPE, polypropylene or stainless drain lines.

### 5.2 Urinal types

| Type | Water | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Legacy flushing | 1.0 gpf (3.8 L) | US federal maximum |
| WaterSense flushing | 0.5 gpf (1.9 L) or less | Standard modern |
| Ultra-low ("pint") | 0.125 gpf (0.47 L) | Needs proper bowl design to scour |
| Waterless, barrier-fluid cartridge | 0 | Low-density oil layer floats on urine; cartridge replacement required |
| Waterless, mechanical seal | 0 | Silicone membrane or duckbill closes after flow |
| Hybrid | Periodic automatic flush (e.g. every 24 to 72 h) | Clears drain line scale; solves main waterless weakness |
| Urine-diverting (NoMix) | 0 to minimal | Collects urine for nutrient recovery |

Waterless drain-line rules:
- Slope 2% (1/4 in per ft) minimum; steeper is better.
- Plastic drain material.
- Place downstream of other fixtures where possible so flush water dilutes.
- Periodic enzymatic or acid descaling.

### 5.3 Splash physics

- A urine stream breaks into droplets (Plateau-Rayleigh instability) after traveling roughly 15 to 20 cm. Droplets splash far more than an intact stream.
- Research from the BYU Splash Lab and the University of Waterloo found splashback falls dramatically when the stream strikes the surface at a glancing angle below about 30°.
- The Waterloo "Nauti-loo" design (2022) uses a nautilus-shell curve so the stream meets the wall at a shallow angle from a wide range of heights and positions.
- Superhydrophobic surfaces **increase** droplet rebound. Smooth hydrophilic or liquid-infused surfaces reduce rebound. A urinal surface should be smooth and wetting for splash control plus scale resistant.
- Aim targets (the fly etched in Amsterdam Schiphol urinals) have reported spillage reductions commonly cited at 50 to 80%. Place the target low and toward the back where the impact angle is shallowest.

### 5.4 Mounting

- Standard rim height 24 in (610 mm).
- US accessible urinal: rim no higher than 17 in (432 mm), elongated rim at least 13.5 in (343 mm) deep.
- Floor-to-rim "stall" urinals catch drips but put a cleaning seam at the floor unless fully sealed.

### 5.5 The zone around the urinal

The floor within about 1 m (3 ft) of a urinal is typically the dirtiest surface in a restroom.
- One-piece impervious wall panel behind and below the urinal (sintered stone slab, glass, stainless or solid surface) extending to an integral coved base.
- Floor: seamless resin or large-format porcelain with epoxy grout, sloped to a floor drain.
- Privacy screens: phenolic compact laminate, HDPE or stainless, wall-hung with no floor contact.

### 5.6 Best urinal specification

- Vitreous china with nano-smooth glaze or electropolished 316L stainless
- Deep bowl with shallow-angle (nautilus-type) back profile to eliminate splash
- Hybrid operation: ultra-low 0.125 gpf or waterless with automatic periodic line flush
- Sensor activation (no touch), concealed flush valve with access panel
- Integrated aim target at low back position
- Plastic drain line at 2% plus slope, cleanout access
- Seamless impervious wall panel and coved floor, floor drain, continuous exhaust

## 6. Walls

### 6.1 Wet-zone wall surfaces ranked

| Rank | Surface | Joints | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Back-painted tempered glass or low-iron glass panels | Few, siliconed | Zero porosity, easy-clean coatings available |
| 2 | Sintered stone or gauged porcelain panels (3.5 to 6 mm, up to 1600 × 3200 mm) | Very few | Impervious, heat proof, thin and light |
| 3 | Solid surface, thermoformed and seam-glued | Invisible seams | Fully seamless, coved corners possible, repairable |
| 4 | Electropolished stainless panels with welded seams | None if welded | Institutional; cold aesthetic |
| 5 | Large-format porcelain tile with epoxy grout | Some | Excellent if grout is epoxy |
| 6 | Tadelakt (burnished lime plaster sealed with olive soap) | None | Beautiful, seamless, water resistant; needs maintenance; not impervious |
| 7 | Small ceramic tile with cementitious grout | Many | Grout absorbs and stains |
| 8 | FRP panels | Trim joints | Cheap; scratches and yellows |

### 6.2 Tile and grout

- Gauged porcelain tile panels: ANSI A137.3 and A108.19. Require large-format mortar with near-full coverage.
- Epoxy grout (ANSI A118.3): non-porous, stain proof, chemical resistant. Harder to install.
- Urethane grout: good stain resistance, easier than epoxy.
- Cementitious grout (ANSI A118.6/A118.7): porous unless sealed; sealers wear off.
- Joint width 1/16 in (1.5 mm) with rectified tile minimizes grout area.
- Change of plane (corners, wall-to-floor, wall-to-tub) uses sealant, never grout. Silicone movement joints at all perimeters and every 8 to 12 ft (2.4 to 3.6 m) interior per TCNA EJ171.

### 6.3 Waterproofing behind the finish

- Liquid-applied or sheet membranes meeting ANSI A118.10.
- Steam showers need a membrane at 0.5 perm or lower (vapor retarder), sloped ceiling to shed condensation.
- Substrates: cement board, foam tile backer boards, glass-mat gypsum. Paper-faced gypsum never goes behind wet tile.
- Mold resistance test ASTM D3273: score 10 = no growth. Glass-mat gypsum typically scores 10.

### 6.4 Dry interior wall finishes

| Venetian / marmorino plaster | high | good | good when waxed | Polished lime |

Paint washability: ASTM D2486 scrub resistance; ISO 11998 class 1 = under 5 µm film loss after 200 scrub cycles (best). Higher gloss means less micro-porosity and better cleanability.

## 7. Breathability, Vapor Control and Ventilation

### 7.1 What "breathable" actually means

- A wall should not exchange air. Air leakage carries vastly more moisture than diffusion. Classic Building Science Corporation example: a 1 in² hole can move about 30 quarts (28 L) of water into a wall over a heating season, while diffusion through an entire 4 × 8 ft sheet of painted gypsum moves about 1/3 quart.
- "Breathable" correctly means **vapor permeable**: water vapor can diffuse through materials so the assembly dries.
- The rule: **airtight envelope + vapor-open materials in the drying direction + mechanical ventilation.** "Build tight, ventilate right."

### 7.2 Units

| Unit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| US perm | 1 grain/(h·ft²·inHg). 1 US perm = 57.2 ng/(s·m²·Pa) |
| μ (mu) | Vapor diffusion resistance factor relative to still air (air = 1) |
| sd value (m) | Equivalent air layer thickness = μ × material thickness. sd 0.5 m or less is vapor open; over 1500 m is a vapor barrier |

### 7.3 Vapor retarder classes (US codes)

| Class | Perm | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| I (vapor barrier) | 0.1 or less | Polyethylene, glass, sheet metal, foil facing |
| II | over 0.1 to 1.0 | Kraft facing, some vapor-retarder paints, plywood when dry |
| III | over 1 to 10 | Latex paint on gypsum |
| Vapor open | over 10 | Housewraps (typically 10 to 60 perm), mineral wool, unpainted gypsum |

| Still air | 1 |
| Mineral wool / fiberglass | 1 to 1.3 |
| Cellulose | 1 to 2 |
| Wood fiber insulation board | 3 to 5 |
| Clay plaster | 5 to 10 |
| Lime plaster | 6 to 10 |
| Gypsum board | 8 to 10 |
| AAC | 5 to 10 |
| Brick | 5 to 15 |

| EPS | 20 to 100 |
| Concrete | 70 to 150 |
| XPS | 80 to 250 |
| OSB | 150 to 300 (falls when humid) |

### 7.5 What the breathability of a house is supposed to be

1. **Airtightness** (blower door at 50 Pa):
   - US IECC 2021: 3.0 ACH50 or lower in climate zones 3 to 8; 5.0 ACH50 or lower in zones 0 to 2.
   - Passive House: 0.6 ACH50 or lower.
   - Phius: 0.06 CFM50 per ft² of envelope area.
   - Excellent practical target: 1.0 ACH50 or lower.
2. **Vapor profile**: the warm side should be more vapor resistant than the cold side so moisture that enters can exit. German practice: interior layer at least 5 to 10 times more vapor resistant (sd) than the exterior layers in heating climates.
3. **Smart (variable-perm) vapor retarders**: about 0.5 to 1 perm when dry (winter) rising above 10 perm when humid (summer), letting walls dry inward in summer. Products include polyamide and hygrovariable membranes.
4. **Climate rules**:
   - Cold (zones 5 to 8): interior Class II or III or smart retarder; exterior vapor open; or enough continuous exterior insulation to keep sheathing warm (IRC Table R702.7 ratios).
   - Mixed (zone 4): smart retarder; avoid Class I on either side.
   - Hot humid (zones 1 to 3): no interior polyethylene or vinyl wallpaper; allow inward drying; strong dehumidification.
5. **Hygric buffering** (NORDTEST Moisture Buffer Value, g/(m²·%RH)):

| MBV class | Value | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Negligible | 0 to 0.2 | Glass, metal, epoxy |
| Limited | 0.2 to 0.5 | Painted gypsum |
| Moderate | 0.5 to 1.0 | Bare gypsum, lime |
| Good | 1.0 to 2.0 | Wood, lime plaster thick coats |
| Excellent | 2.0 or more | Clay plaster, earth |

A strong design: impervious surfaces in wet zones, buffering surfaces (clay, lime, wood) in bedrooms and living areas.

### 7.6 Ventilation

- ASHRAE 62.2 whole-house continuous rate: **Q (cfm) = 0.03 × floor area (ft²) + 7.5 × (bedrooms + 1)**. Example: 2000 ft², 3 bedrooms = 60 + 30 = 90 cfm.
- Bathrooms: 50 cfm intermittent or 20 cfm continuous minimum. Better: 80 to 110 cfm with humidity control.
- Kitchens: 100 cfm intermittent minimum; range hoods vented outdoors.
- HRV (heat recovery ventilator): transfers heat only. Best in cold dry climates.
- ERV (energy recovery ventilator): transfers heat and some moisture. Best in humid or very dry climates.
- Recovery efficiency 75 to 95% for good units.

## 9. Grounding, Static and Electrical Safety

### 9.1 Electrical safety grounding

- US NEC: GFCI protection on all bathroom receptacles; within 6 ft of sinks, tubs and showers in other areas; all kitchen countertop receptacles.
- Metal water piping bonded to the electrical service grounding system (NEC 250).
- Many countries require supplementary equipotential bonding in bathrooms (IEC 60364-7-701) linking metal pipes, tubs and accessible metal parts.
- Stainless and metal fixtures that may contact electrical systems are bonded.
- Bidet seats and electronic toilets require a GFCI-protected dedicated receptacle.

### 9.2 Static electricity and ESD floors

- Indoor RH below about 30% sharply increases static buildup. Maintaining 40% RH or higher reduces shocks.

| Floor class | Resistance to ground | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Conductive | 2.5 × 10⁴ to 1 × 10⁶ Ω | Explosive handling, electronics manufacturing |
| Static dissipative | 1 × 10⁶ to 1 × 10⁹ Ω | Electronics, labs, data centers |
| Insulative (standard floors) | over 10¹⁰ Ω | Most homes |

ANSI/ESD S20.20 requires floor systems under 1 × 10⁹ Ω combined with ESD footwear. Conductive epoxy floors use carbon fibers or carbon black; ESD vinyl uses conductive chips or carbon networks; copper grounding strips tie them to building ground.

### 9.3 "Earthing" health products

- Practice: skin contact with the ground or with conductive mats and sheets wired to the ground pin of an outlet.
- Evidence: published studies on inflammation, sleep, cortisol, blood viscosity and pain are small, mostly short, often unblinded and frequently authored by people tied to the product industry. No large independent randomized trials establish health effects.
- Physics: the body already equilibrates with its electrical environment through ordinary contact; grounding removes static charge and changes AC-induced body voltage measured from nearby wiring.
- Safety: consumer mats include a series resistor (commonly about 100 kΩ to 1 MΩ) so a fault cannot drive large current through the body. A miswired outlet or a direct wire to ground without a resistor removes that protection.
- Building implication: bare-foot contact with concrete slabs, stone and earthen floors is also a conductive path to earth, more so when the slab sits directly on soil without a vapor barrier. Modern slabs have polyethylene vapor barriers and sealers that make them poor conductors.

### 9.4 EMF considerations in wiring design

- Magnetic fields fall off rapidly with distance; keeping main panels, meters and large transformers away from bedroom walls reduces exposure.
- Proper wiring (no neutral-to-ground faults, no shared neutrals between circuits) prevents net current on water pipes that creates elevated magnetic fields throughout a house.
- Metal-clad (MC) cable or conduit reduces electric field emission compared with unshielded cable.

### 10.2 Why some surfaces feel cold: thermal effusivity

Effusivity e = √(k · ρ · c), units W·s^0.5/(m²·K).

When skin (temperature T_s, effusivity e_s) touches a surface (T_m, e_m), the instant contact temperature is:

**T_contact = (e_s·T_s + e_m·T_m) / (e_s + e_m)**

| Material | Effusivity (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Cork | 120 |
| Oak | 450 to 500 |
| Polypropylene / thermoset seat | 500 to 700 |
| Human skin | about 1000 to 1500 |
| Glass | 1450 |
| Porcelain | 1700 |
| Concrete | 1850 |
| Granite | 2500 to 3000 |
| Stainless steel | 8000 |
| Aluminum | 22,000 |

Worked example (skin 33 °C, e_s = 1200; room surfaces 20 °C):
- Oak floor: (1200·33 + 488·20)/(1688) = **29.2 °C** felt
- Porcelain floor: (1200·33 + 1690·20)/(2890) = **25.4 °C** felt
- Stainless toilet: (1200·33 + 8000·20)/(9200) = **21.7 °C** felt

This is why thermoset seats replace ceramic seats, why stainless toilets feel harsh and why radiant heating pairs well with porcelain floors.

### 11.4 Genuine antimicrobial surfaces

- **Copper alloys**: the US EPA registered copper alloys (roughly 60% copper or more) in 2008 with public health claims of killing over 99.9% of specific bacteria within 2 hours of contact. Mechanism: copper ions damage membranes, generate reactive oxygen species and degrade DNA. Lacquer or wax coatings block the effect; tarnish reduces it only moderately. Ammonia and urine corrode copper, so it suits door hardware and touch surfaces rather than urinal interiors.
- **Silver ions**: effective in glazes and coatings at low dose; slower than copper.
- **Photocatalytic TiO2**: oxidizes organics under UV-A; indoor light is usually too weak without a UV source or visible-light doped catalyst.

## 14. Glues, Adhesives, Grouts and Sealants

### 14.1 Adhesion science

- An adhesive must wet the surface: adhesive surface tension lower than substrate surface energy.
- Low-energy plastics (PTFE, PE, PP, silicone) resist bonding. Remedies: corona, flame or plasma treatment; sodium-naphthalene etching for PTFE; primers; specialty low-surface-energy acrylic adhesives.
- Glass and ceramics bond best with silane coupling agents that chemically link inorganic surfaces to organic adhesives.
- Metals bond best after abrasion, degreasing and primer.
- Joint design: shear and tension preferred; peel and cleavage are weak.

| PVD coatings (ZrN, TiN, CrN) | Extremely hard colored finishes on faucets | Thin; base metal quality still matters |
| DLC (diamond-like carbon) | Very hard, low friction, black | Cost |

### 15.4 Galvanic corrosion

Metals in electrical contact with an electrolyte (water, urine) corrode the less noble metal.

Approximate order (most noble to least): titanium, stainless (passive), silver, copper alloys, nickel, tin, lead, carbon steel, cast iron, aluminum, zinc, magnesium.

Rules: isolate dissimilar metals with nylon or EPDM washers; keep the anode (less noble) larger in area than the cathode; never fasten aluminum with copper or bare stainless in wet service without isolation.
