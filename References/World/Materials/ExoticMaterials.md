## Carbon allotropes

**Carbyne.** A one-dimensional chain of carbon atoms joined by alternating single and triple bonds. Calculations put its tensile strength at roughly twice that of graphene. Its stiffness is about three times that of diamond. In 2016 researchers grew chains of over 6,000 atoms by protecting them inside double-walled carbon nanotubes. In open air the chains cross-link with each other violently, so bulk carbyne may never exist.

**Graphene.** A single layer of carbon atoms. It is the strongest material ever measured (about 130 GPa tensile strength, roughly 200 times steel by weight). It also conducts heat and electricity extremely well. Defect-free sheets are impermeable to H₂ (`PermeationBarriers.md`). The main obstacle is making large sheets without defects.

**Carbon nanotubes.** Graphene rolled into tubes. They are the standard candidate for space elevator cables. Real bulk fibers lose most of the theoretical strength because of defects and weak bonding between tubes.

**Lonsdaleite (hexagonal diamond).** Found in meteorite impact sites. Simulations predict it is up to 58% harder than cubic diamond. Natural samples are too small and impure to confirm this.

**Q-carbon.** Made in 2015 by melting amorphous carbon with lasers and quenching it. It is reported to be harder than diamond, ferromagnetic at room temperature and to glow when heated.

**Diamond nanothreads.** Ultra-thin sp³ carbon threads made from compressed benzene. They are predicted to approach nanotube strength while being more flexible.

## High-pressure exotic phases

**Metallic hydrogen.** Hydrogen squeezed until it behaves as a liquid or solid metal. It was predicted in 1935 and needs roughly 400 to 500 GPa (around 4 to 5 million atmospheres). Two predicted properties make it remarkable:
- It may be a room-temperature superconductor.
- If it stays metastable after the pressure is released, it would be the most powerful chemical rocket fuel possible, with a specific impulse of about 1,700 seconds. The best chemical rockets today reach about 450.

A 2017 Harvard claim of producing it remains disputed. Jupiter and Saturn almost certainly contain huge oceans of it.

**Polymeric nitrogen (cubic gauche nitrogen).** Normal nitrogen is held together by an extremely strong triple bond. Under about 110 GPa and 2,000 K the atoms rearrange into a lattice of single bonds. When that lattice collapses back into N₂ gas it releases roughly 5 times the energy of TNT by mass with clean nitrogen as the only product. It was first synthesized in 2004. It does not yet survive at normal pressure, though teams keep trying to stabilize it.

**Metallic oxygen and other metallized gases.** Oxygen becomes metallic and superconducting at very low temperatures above about 96 GPa. Most elements turn metallic if compressed hard enough.

**Superhydrides.** Compounds like lanthanum hydride (LaH₁₀) superconduct at about 250 K (around -23 °C) under 170 GPa. These are the closest verified approach to room-temperature superconductivity. Several claims of true room-temperature superconductors (including LK-99 in 2023) have collapsed under replication.

## Extreme alloys and structures

**CrCoNi and other high-entropy alloys.** Alloys mixing several metals in near-equal amounts. In 2022 CrCoNi was measured as the toughest material ever recorded, and it gets tougher as it gets colder.

**Metallic glasses.** Metals cooled so fast that they never form crystals. They can be about 2 to 3 times stronger than steel while flexing like springs.

**Ultra-high-temperature ceramics.** Tantalum hafnium carbide melts near 3,990 °C. Hafnium carbonitride is predicted to melt above 4,100 °C, which would be the highest of any known compound.

**Wurtzite boron nitride.** Predicted to be harder than diamond under certain loads and far more resistant to oxidation at high temperatures.

**Aerogels and metamaterials.** Graphene aerogel is among the lightest solids made (lighter than air by volume when evacuated). Metamaterials gain properties from structure rather than chemistry, such as negative refraction for invisibility-style cloaking.

## Purely theoretical or astrophysical

**Superheavy "island of stability" elements.** Nuclear models predict that elements near proton number 114 to 126 and neutron number 184 could have half-lives far longer than their neighbors. Some might last long enough to have usable chemistry.

**Nuclear pasta.** Matter in the crust of neutron stars where nuclei squeeze into sheets and rods. A 2018 simulation estimated it is about 10 billion times stronger than steel, which would make it the strongest material in the universe.

**Neutronium.** The degenerate neutron matter of neutron star interiors, with a teaspoon weighing around a billion tons. It cannot exist without the gravity holding it together.

**Strange matter.** A hypothetical form of quark matter containing strange quarks. Some theories say it could be more stable than ordinary nuclei, with the unsettling implication that contact could convert normal matter into more strange matter.

**Antimatter.** Not a structural material but the densest possible energy storage. Matter and antimatter annihilation converts 100% of mass to energy, about 1,000 times more than fission.

**Exotic matter with negative mass or energy density.** Required by Alcubierre warp drives and traversable wormholes. Small negative energy densities do appear in the Casimir effect. Bulk negative mass has never been observed.
