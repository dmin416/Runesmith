## 3. Natural Rock

Outcrops, cliffs, boulders and fieldstone. Wear patterns relevant to building on rock, using fieldstone or describing ruins in landscape.

### 3.1 Characteristic forms

- **Exfoliation and sheeting**: granite domes (Half Dome, Stone Mountain, Sugarloaf Mountain) shed curved sheets centimeters to meters thick through stress release.
- **Spheroidal weathering**: jointed blocks of granite or basalt round off from the corners inward because corners are attacked from three sides and edges from two. Rounded corestones sit within decomposed rock. When the soft rock washes away, piles of rounded boulders (tors) remain.
- **Granular disintegration**: granite crumbles into grus. Slopes below granite outcrops are often carpeted with coarse angular sand.
- **Weathering rinds**: basalt and andesite clasts develop altered outer rinds that thicken over time. Rind thickness (millimeters over tens of thousands of years) is used to date glacial deposits.
- **Desert varnish**: a dark coating of manganese and iron oxides bound with clay. It grows extremely slowly, roughly 1 to 40 micrometers per thousand years. Fresh rock is pale. Varnished rock turns orange-brown, then dark brown to black over thousands of years. Petroglyphs pecked through varnish slowly darken again; the degree of re-varnishing helps estimate their age.
- **Tafoni and honeycomb**: cavernous hollows from salt weathering, common on coastal sandstone and granite (Corsica, Sardinia, the California coast, the Mediterranean).
- **Karst and solution features**: limestone develops grooves (rillenkarren), runnels, solution pans and pits in rainy climates. Exposed limestone pavements form clints and grikes.
- **Ventifacts and yardangs**: wind-polished and faceted stones and streamlined ridges in sandy deserts.

### 3.2 Coastal rock

- **Notches**: tropical limestone coasts develop undercut notches at sea level from dissolution and bioerosion (snails, urchins, boring sponges grazing the rock). Notch deepening runs roughly 0.5 to 1 mm per year.
- **Wave-cut platforms and sea caves** form where joints and soft beds concentrate wave energy.
- **Cliff retreat rates** vary enormously with rock type:
  - Glacial clay cliffs (Holderness, England): about 1 to 2 m per year
  - Chalk cliffs (southern England, Normandy): about 0.2 to 0.5 m per year, often in sudden collapses
  - Sandstone: centimeters to tens of centimeters per year
  - Granite and basalt: millimeters per year or less
- **Biological zonation on coastal rock** (temperate Atlantic coasts): a black band of tar-like lichen (Verrucaria) at the top of the splash zone, an orange band (Xanthoria, Caloplaca) above it, then gray-green shrubby lichens (Ramalina) higher still. Pale bare rock lies below in the tidal zone. The bands show exactly how far spray reaches.

### 3.3 Lichen growth as a clock

Lichens colonize fresh rock within years to decades. Map lichen (Rhizocarpon geographicum) grows very slowly, often well under 1 mm in diameter per year after an initial establishment period. Large lichen colonies on stone indicate decades to centuries of undisturbed exposure.

## 4. Building Stone by Type

### 4.1 Granite (and diorite, gabbro, other coarse igneous rock)

**Composition**: quartz, feldspar, mica. Porosity usually under 1 to 2%.

**Durability**: the most weather-resistant common building stone.

**Wear patterns**
- Polished surfaces lose gloss over decades outdoors as feldspar surfaces etch. Honed and flamed finishes change less visibly.
- Rust spots and streaks from oxidizing biotite or pyrite.
- Granular disintegration where salt and moisture attack grain boundaries. Edges become rough and sandy.
- Flaking and sheet spalling in salty or fire-damaged stone.
- Lichen colonization on unpolished faces in clean, humid air.

**Fire**: quartz undergoes a sudden crystal change at 573°C (1,063°F) with a jump in volume. Granite cracks and spalls badly in fires despite its weather durability.

**Marine**: salt penetrates microcracks. Coastal granite develops honeycombing and roughened surfaces. Granite seawalls, breakwaters and lighthouses still routinely last centuries.

**Rates**: surface loss usually under 1 mm per century in most climates. Lettering on granite gravestones typically remains crisp after 100 to 150 years.

**Examples**
- Aberdeen, Scotland ("the Granite City"): 19th-century granite buildings still sharp-edged, darkened in places by soot and biological growth.
- Smeaton's Tower, Eddystone Lighthouse (1759): granite tower dismantled in 1877 because waves were undermining the rock it stood on. The tower itself was sound and was rebuilt on Plymouth Hoe, where it still stands.
- Cleopatra's Needle, New York (see `WeatheringWear.md` Case Studies): Egyptian granite that survived over 3,000 years in Egypt flaked noticeably within decades in New York's freeze-thaw climate.

### 4.2 Basalt

**Composition**: dense, fine-grained volcanic rock, sometimes full of gas bubbles (vesicular).

**Wear patterns**
- Dark gray surfaces weather to brown or reddish rinds as iron oxidizes.
- Spheroidal rounding of blocks.
- Vesicular basalt traps water and salts in its holes and weathers faster.
- Very resistant to abrasion. Used for cobbles, millstones and breakwater armor.

### 4.3 Tuff and other volcanic ash stone

**Composition**: compacted or welded volcanic ash. Porous, soft, easy to carve.

**Wear patterns**
- Rapid rounding of carved detail.
- Wind-sculpted hollows and fluting.
- Salt crystallization and lichen damage in coastal locations.
- Soft beds erode faster than welded beds, producing layered, stepped profiles.

**Examples**
- Cappadocia, Turkey: carved tuff dwellings and fairy chimneys. Erosion visible within human lifetimes.
- Rapa Nui (Easter Island) moai: carved mostly from lapilli tuff. Facial detail is softening from rain, salt spray, wind and lichens, with significant loss over the last few centuries.
- Roman tuff walls in central Italy: faces recessed between harder stone or brick courses.

### 4.4 Sandstone

**Composition**: quartz (sometimes feldspar) grains bound by a natural cement. The cement controls durability:

| Cement type | Color clues | Durability | Weak point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Siliceous (quartz) | Pale, very hard | Excellent | Hard to carve |
| Ferruginous (iron oxide) | Red, brown, yellow | Good | Iron can leach, causing staining |
| Calcareous (calcite) | Buff, gray | Fair | Dissolves in acid rain |
| Argillaceous (clay) | Varies | Poor | Swells when wet, crumbles |

**Wear patterns**
- **Contour scaling**: a surface crust 2 to 20 mm thick detaches as a single shell following the carved shape rather than the bedding. Caused by salts or pollutants concentrating in an outer zone with different properties than the interior.
- **Delamination**: stone laid with its bedding planes parallel to the wall face ("face-bedded") peels off in sheets along the beds. Stone laid in its natural bed (beds horizontal) resists far better.
- **Honeycombing and tafoni**: networks of cells and hollows from salt, common near coasts and in deserts.
- **Granular disintegration ("sanding")**: grains rub off at a touch. Sand accumulates on ledges and at the wall base.
- **Differential erosion**: harder beds stand proud while softer beds recede, producing a ribbed or ledged surface.
- **Iron banding and liesegang rings** become more pronounced as surrounding material erodes.

**Rates**: highly variable. Well-cemented siliceous sandstone may lose under 1 mm per century. Soft sandstone in salt environments can lose 5 to 50 mm per century. Active tafoni in aggressive salt settings deepen by several millimeters to centimeters per decade.

**Examples**
- New York brownstone: Triassic sandstone from Connecticut was often face-bedded on 19th-century row houses. Fronts delaminated within 50 to 100 years. Many were resurfaced with tinted cement stucco, which itself traps moisture and fails.
- Petra, Jordan: facades carved roughly 2,000 years ago. Exposed faces show honeycombing from salt and wind. Al-Khazneh (the Treasury) is better preserved because the narrow canyon approach shelters it.
- Angkor Wat, Cambodia (12th century): sandstone attacked by salts (partly from bat guano), biological films and tree roots. Laterite cores behind the facing also deteriorate.
- Sydney, Australia: Hawkesbury sandstone buildings in coastal salt air show extensive salt decay, honeycombing and powdering at the base.
- Red sandstone cathedrals of the Rhine (Strasbourg, Mainz): constant replacement of eroded carved stone through permanent cathedral workshops.

### 4.5 Limestone

**Composition**: calcite. Varieties include oolitic limestone (Portland, Bath, Indiana), fossiliferous limestone, chalk and travertine.

**Wear patterns**
- **Dissolution and rounding**: carved detail softens. Arrises (sharp edges) round first. Inscriptions blur.
- **Rain-washed vs sheltered contrast**: washed zones are pale, clean and eroded. Sheltered zones (under cornices, inside porches, behind projecting moldings) collect black gypsum crusts that blister and spall.
- **Frost damage** in porous oolitic stones: surface scaling, cracked and missing projections.
- **Differential erosion of beds**: soft beds recede, hard beds stand proud.
- **Lichen etching** in clean rural air.
- **Karst-style fluting** on exposed tops of walls, parapets and copings.

**Rates**
- Clean air: roughly 1 to 3 mm per century.
- Heavily polluted 20th-century cities: around 8 mm per century or more. At St Paul's Cathedral in London, lead plugs set flush in the stone balustrade around 1718 stood about 20 mm proud by the 1980s as the surrounding limestone dissolved away.

**Examples**
- **Great Sphinx of Giza**: carved directly from bedrock around 2500 BC. The rock layers weather at very different rates: harder beds at the head and lowest body, softer beds in the middle body deeply recessed and rounded. Salts drawn up from groundwater and dissolved by dew and rising humidity cause ongoing flaking. Twentieth-century repairs using gypsum and cement mortars accelerated damage and had to be removed.
- Bath, England: honey-colored oolitic limestone. Frost and pollution damage, ongoing stone replacement.
- Indiana limestone: widely used across North America (Empire State Building, Pentagon, many state capitols). Performs well in clean air, darkens with soot and biological growth.

### 4.6 Marble

**Composition**: metamorphosed limestone, a mosaic of interlocking calcite (or dolomite) crystals.

**Wear patterns**
- **Loss of polish** within a few years outdoors.
- **Sugaring**: grains loosen along boundaries due to thermal cycling and dissolution. The surface looks and feels like a sugar cube and crumbles under a fingernail.
- **Bowing of thin panels**: panels thinner than about 3 to 4 cm (1.25 to 1.5 in) bow outward or dish inward from thermal hysteresis. Each heating cycle leaves a tiny permanent expansion. Strength can drop 40 to 50%.
- **Gypsum black crusts** in sheltered, polluted locations.
- **Rusty staining** from iron cramps and pyrite inclusions.
- **Iron cramp splitting**: rusting iron dowels and cramps crack blocks open.
- **Biological films** (black and orange) in humid climates.

**Rates**: gravestone studies show roughly 1 mm per century in dry, clean rural settings, rising to 10 mm or more per century in heavy 20th-century industrial air. Lettering on marble gravestones becomes hard to read after about 100 years in polluted, wet climates and may remain legible for 200+ years in dry, clean ones.

**Examples**
- **Aon Center, Chicago** (completed 1973): clad in thin Carrara marble panels. Panels bowed visibly within about 15 years. The entire building was reclad in granite in the early 1990s at a cost of roughly 80 million dollars.
- **Finlandia Hall, Helsinki** (1971): Carrara marble panels bowed in the cold climate. Replaced in the late 1990s with new marble, which began bowing again.
- **Parthenon, Athens** (5th century BC): Pentelic marble survived 2,400 years comparatively well. The worst damage came from a 1687 explosion, from iron clamps added during early 20th-century restoration that rusted and split blocks and from 20th-century air pollution. The original Caryatids were moved indoors.
- **Taj Mahal, Agra** (17th century): white Makrana marble yellowing and browning from airborne particulates (soot, dust, brown carbon). Cleaned repeatedly with clay poultices (multani mitti).

### 4.7 Slate

**Composition**: fine-grained metamorphic rock that splits into thin, strong sheets. Low porosity.

**Wear patterns**
- Edge flaking and delamination in lower-grade slates.
- Rust spots from pyrite. Calcite-bearing slates soften and lose strength.
- Surface softening and white bloom on poor slates.
- **Nail sickness**: iron nails rust through long before quality slate fails. Slates slip and drop from the roof. Copper or stainless nails outlast iron by many decades.

**Lifespans**
- Premium Welsh, Vermont and Spanish slates: 100 to 200 years.
- Lower grades: 50 to 80 years.
- Iron-nailed roofs: often need refixing after 30 to 60 years regardless of slate quality.

### 4.8 Other stones

- **Quartzite**: extremely hard and durable. Weathers mainly by fracture along joints.
- **Soapstone**: soft but dense and chemically inert. Resists acids and heat well. Scratches and wears down under traffic.
- **Travertine**: limestone deposited by springs, full of natural voids. Voids collect dirt and water. Freeze-thaw can pop surfaces in cold climates. Rome's Colosseum is travertine; its pockmarked appearance comes mostly from medieval removal of iron clamps rather than weather.

### 4.9 Stone durability ranking (general exterior use)

| Rank | Stone | Typical surface loss per century (temperate, clean air) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Quartzite, granite, basalt | under 1 mm |
| 2 | Siliceous sandstone, quality slate | under 1 mm to 2 mm |
| 3 | Dense limestone, marble (thick sections) | 1 to 3 mm |
| 4 | Ferruginous and calcareous sandstone | 2 to 10 mm |
| 5 | Oolitic and porous limestone | 3 to 15 mm |
| 6 | Clay-cemented sandstone, tuff, chalk | 10 to 50+ mm |

Pollution, salt or heavy freeze-thaw can multiply these figures several times.

## 6. Brick and Terracotta

Fired clay masonry, included because it shares most wear patterns with stone and mortar.

- **Underfired soft bricks**: porous, weak. Erode, powder and spall from frost and salts. Common in 18th and 19th-century interior wythes later exposed by demolition.
- **Hard-fired bricks**: dense fire skin protects the face. Once the skin is lost, the softer interior erodes quickly.
- **Efflorescence**: white salt bloom, often in the first year of a new building ("new building bloom") as construction moisture dries out. Persistent efflorescence means an ongoing water source.
- **Spalling**: brick faces pop off from freeze-thaw or salts, especially near the ground, under leaking gutters and on parapets.
- **Glazed terracotta**: the glaze crazes over decades. Water enters behind it through cracks and failed joints. Iron anchors inside rust and expand, cracking pieces off. Many early 20th-century terracotta facades needed major anchor replacement after 60 to 100 years.
- **Coastal brick**: salt decay concentrated in the lowest courses and parapets.
- **Lifespan**: well-fired brick with appropriate mortar commonly lasts 100 to 500+ years.
