## 1. Glass

**What it is:** An amorphous solid with no orderly crystal pattern. Common glass is about 70% silica (SiO₂) with soda (sodium oxide) to lower the melting point and lime (calcium oxide) for durability. Mohs hardness about 5.5.

### Types and applications
- **Soda-lime:** windows, bottles, jars. Cheapest.
- **Borosilicate:** lab glassware, cookware, lamp covers. Resists thermal shock.
- **Fused silica (quartz glass):** UV optics, furnace tubes, semiconductor equipment. Handles 1,000°C+ and passes UV.
- **Aluminosilicate:** phone screens, chemically strengthened covers.
- **Lead/crystal:** decorative ware, high sparkle from high refractive index.
- **Optical (crown and flint):** camera, telescope and microscope lenses.
- **Tempered/laminated:** safety windows, windshields.

### Raw materials in nature
- **Silica sand:** riverbeds, beaches, dunes, weathered sandstone. White sand low in iron makes clear glass; iron-rich sand makes green or brown glass.
- **Soda flux:**
  - Natron, a natural soda mineral crusting the shores of evaporating desert lakes (Wadi Natrun in Egypt was the classic source).
  - Ash of salt-marsh plants such as saltwort and glasswort, called barilla.
- **Potash flux:** ash of beech, oak or bracken fern. Produces the greenish "forest glass" of medieval Europe.
- **Lime:** limestone, chalk, crushed seashells. Often enters by accident with the sand or ash.
- **Lead oxide (for crystal):** smelted from galena, a heavy gray lead ore found in hydrothermal veins.
- **Borax (for borosilicate):** crusts on desert dry lakes (Tibet, Death Valley).
- **Decolorizer:** manganese dioxide (pyrolusite), a black mineral known as "glassmaker's soap." It cancels the green tint of iron.
- **Colorants:** cobalt (deep blue), copper (green or red), gold (ruby red), iron (green or amber), tin or antimony (opaque white).

### Processing (narrative)
The glassmaker begins at the river, washing sand in baskets until the clay and silt run off and only bright grains remain. Marsh plants are cut in late summer, dried and burned in pits; the gray cake of ash left behind is the flux. Sand, ash and a pinch of crushed shell or chalk are measured by weight, since too little flux leaves a stony mass and too much makes glass that weeps and clouds in damp air.

The mixture is first baked at a dull red heat into **frit**, a crumbly half-reacted cake that is crushed and sorted. The frit then goes into thick clay crucibles set inside a beehive furnace of brick. Bellows or a tall chimney draft drive the fire past 1,100°C and the furnace is fed wood or charcoal day and night. A foul scum called **gall** rises to the surface and is skimmed away. When the melt runs clear and free of bubbles the batch is ready.

To blow a vessel the worker dips a long iron pipe into the melt, turns it to gather a glowing glob, rolls it on a flat stone (the **marver**) and breathes into it. Tongs and wooden paddles shape the bubble. An iron rod called the **pontil** is stuck to the base so the neck can be opened and finished.

For flat glass there are two old methods:
- **Crown glass:** a blown bubble is opened and spun rapidly on the pontil until centrifugal force flings it into a wide flat disc. The thick center knob is the "bullseye."
- **Cylinder glass:** a long cylinder is blown, its ends are cut off, it is slit lengthwise and laid in an oven where it slumps flat.

Every finished piece must go into the **annealing oven** (lehr) and cool over hours or days. Glass cooled too fast holds hidden stress and shatters at a touch or a change in weather.

Modern window glass is made by pouring the melt onto a bath of molten tin, where it floats and spreads into a perfectly flat sheet. Fused silica needs 1,700°C+ and requires electric arc or oxyhydrogen flame; in a preindustrial setting it would demand exceptional or magical heat.

## 2. Quartz

**What it is:** Crystalline silicon dioxide (SiO₂). Same chemistry as silica glass but the atoms sit in an ordered hexagonal lattice. Mohs 7. Piezoelectric: squeezing it produces a small electric charge.

### Applications
- Oscillators in watches, radios and computers.
- Polarizing optics and waveplates (it splits light into two rays).
- Lenses and prisms that pass ultraviolet.
- Jewelry, carvings, abrasives, raw feedstock for fused silica.

### Raw materials in nature
- **Hydrothermal veins:** white quartz veins cutting through granite, schist and slate. Clear crystals grow in open pockets in these veins.
- **Pegmatites:** coarse granite bodies that host giant crystals.
- **Alpine clefts:** cracks in mountain rock lined with crystals. Alpine crystal hunters (strahlers) searched cliff faces for them.
- **Geodes:** hollow stone nodules lined with crystals.
- **River gravels:** rounded pebbles of clear quartz washed from eroded veins.
- **Famous sources:** Arkansas (Hot Springs), Brazil (Minas Gerais), the Alps, Madagascar.

### Processing (narrative)
Quartz cannot be melted and recast as a crystal, so it is shaped by removal. The crystal hunter reads the mountain for white veins and rusty stains, then follows them to a hollow that rings when struck. The pocket is opened with hammer and chisel. Crystals are packed in clay and moss for the descent, since a single blow can fracture them.

In the workshop the cutter studies each crystal against the light and knocks off cloudy or cracked portions (cobbing). Slices are cut with a copper or iron wire or a thin metal blade fed with wet abrasive grit: crushed corundum, emery or diamond dust. Iron and bronze alone will not scratch quartz; the grit does the cutting. Rough shapes are ground on sandstone wheels, then on finer stones and finally polished on leather or felt charged with tin oxide, rouge or powdered pumice. A good lens takes days of patient grinding.

For use in oscillators the crystal must be sliced at an exact angle to its internal axes (the "AT-cut" is standard). Modern quartz is grown rather than mined: seed plates hang in a steel pressure vessel filled with alkaline water and crushed quartz, held near 350°C at over 1,000 atmospheres for weeks while new crystal slowly builds on the seeds.

## 3. Diamond

**What it is:** Pure carbon in a cubic lattice where every atom bonds to four neighbors. Mohs 10. Best thermal conductor of any bulk material. Graphite is the same element in a soft black layered form. Fantasy hard insulating heat conductor built on this: `MaterialConsiderations.md`.

### Applications
- Cutting, drilling, grinding and polishing tools.
- Heat spreaders for high-power electronics.
- Windows for high-power lasers, infrared and X-rays.
- Anvils for extreme pressure experiments.
- Jewelry. Future high-power semiconductors.

### Raw materials in nature
- **Kimberlite and lamproite pipes:** carrot-shaped volcanic plugs that erupted from 150+ km deep through ancient continental cores. Found in South Africa, Botswana, Siberia, northern Canada and Australia.
- **Alluvial deposits:** diamonds weathered out of pipes and carried by rivers into gravel beds. Historic India (the Golconda region) was the world's only major source until the 1700s; Brazil followed. West African and Namibian river and beach gravels are rich today.
- **Indicator minerals:** prospectors trace pipes upstream by following deep-red pyrope garnet, emerald-green chrome diopside and black ilmenite grains in the gravels.

### Processing (narrative)
At the surface a kimberlite pipe weathers into soft **yellow ground**; beneath it lies harder **blue ground**. Early miners spread blue ground in open fields and let rain and sun crumble it for months. River miners dig gravel from old channels and wash it in rocking cradles or circular pans, letting light sand spill over while heavy minerals settle.

The concentrate is passed over a **grease table**: water carries ordinary minerals across but diamond repels water and sticks fast in the grease. The grease is scraped off and boiled away, leaving the stones. Final picking is done by hand and eye.

Only diamond cuts diamond. The cutter first finds the crystal's natural planes and **cleaves** it with a single sharp tap on a steel blade set in a scored groove; a wrong angle shatters the stone. Shaping is done by **bruting**, rubbing one diamond against another to grind the girdle round. Facets are polished on a **scaife**, a spinning cast-iron wheel charged with diamond dust and olive oil. Hardness varies with direction in the crystal, so the polisher must find the soft grain or the wheel does nothing.

Industrial diamond is crushed into grit and set in metal for saw blades and drill bits. Synthetic diamond is made by squeezing graphite with a molten metal catalyst at about 5 GPa and 1,500°C, or by depositing carbon atom by atom from methane gas in a plasma (CVD).

## 4. Silicone

**What it is:** A synthetic polymer with a silicon-oxygen backbone (–Si–O–Si–O–) and organic groups (usually methyl) attached. Stable from about -60°C to 230°C+, flexible, water repellent and chemically inert. Does not occur in nature.

### Types and applications
- **Fluids/oils (PDMS):** lubricants, hydraulic and heat-transfer fluids, defoamers, cosmetics, hair products. Water clear.
- **Gels:** very soft, clear. Electronics potting, scar sheets, wound dressings, cushioning pads.
- **HCR/HTV rubber (high consistency, heat cured):** gaskets, seals, hoses, keypads, extruded tubing.
- **LSR (liquid silicone rubber):** injection molded. Baby bottle nipples, medical parts, seals, wearables. Optical grades are water clear and used for LED lenses.
- **RTV-1 (one-part sealant):** bathroom caulk, window and aquarium sealant. Acetoxy cure smells like vinegar; neutral cure is safer on metal.
- **RTV-2 mold rubber:**
  - Tin cure: cheap, tolerant of contamination, slightly hazy, shrinks over time.
  - Platinum cure: clearest, food and skin safe, dimensionally stable. Used for food molds, prosthetics, special effects, clear molds.
- **Fluorosilicone:** resists fuel and oil. Aerospace and automotive seals.
- **Addition silicones (PVS/VPS):** dental impression material, very accurate and stable.
- **Resins:** high-heat paints, masonry waterproofing, electrical varnish.
- **Pressure-sensitive adhesives:** medical tapes, high-temp tapes, release liners.
- **Foams/sponges:** insulation, gasketing, cushioning.

Clarity: fumed silica filler adds strength but haze. Optical and platinum-cure grades stay clearest.

### Raw materials in nature
- **Quartz or high-purity silica sand:** the silicon source.
- **Carbon:** charcoal, coke or wood chips to strip oxygen from the quartz.
- **Methanol:** originally distilled from wood ("wood alcohol"), now made from natural gas.
- **Hydrochloric acid / chlorine:** from salt (rock salt deposits or evaporated seawater).
- **Copper:** catalyst for the key reaction.
- **Platinum or tin compounds:** catalysts for curing into rubber.

### Processing (narrative)
Silicone is a product of advanced chemistry and every step depends on the one before.

First, clean quartz and charcoal are fed into an electric arc furnace and heated near 2,000°C. The carbon tears the oxygen away and molten silicon metal pools at the bottom, gray and glittering. It is cast, cooled and ground to fine powder.

Second, wood alcohol and acid from salt are combined into **methyl chloride**, a sweet-smelling gas. In a heated reactor near 300°C the gas streams through the silicon powder mixed with copper. The copper coaxes the silicon to grab methyl groups and chlorine, producing a soup of liquid **chlorosilanes**. These are separated by careful distillation in tall columns; the prized fraction is dimethyldichlorosilane.

Third, the purified liquid is mixed with water. It hydrolyzes violently, releasing acid fumes and the silicon atoms link through oxygen bridges into long chains. Short chains are thin oil; long chains are a thick gum.

Finally the gum is compounded and cured. Mixed with fumed silica for strength, it is crosslinked by heat with peroxide, by a platinum catalyst that zips vinyl and hydride groups together, or by a tin catalyst that reacts with moisture. Platinum cure yields the clearest, purest rubber.

In a preindustrial setting, silicone requires either a leap in chemistry or a magical equivalent (for example, a rune that forces quartz to "unknot" into living thread). See Section 7 for natural substitutes.

## 5. Chitin and Chitosan (clear insect material)

**What it is:** Chitin is a tough polysaccharide (a long sugar chain related to cellulose) forming the base of insect and crustacean exoskeletons. Combined with proteins it makes cuticle, clear in wing membranes and eye lenses, hardened and pigmented in beetle shells. Chitosan is chitin chemically modified to dissolve in weak acid.

Related clear insect materials:
- **Wing membranes:** two layers of thin cuticle supported by veins (dragonflies, cicadas).
- **Eye lenses:** each compound-eye facet is capped with a clear cuticle lens.
- **Resilin:** a transparent, rubbery protein in wing hinges and jumping joints that stores and releases energy with very little loss.

### Applications
- Wound dressings, dissolvable sutures, drug delivery.
- Water purification (binds metals and dyes).
- Biodegradable films, food coatings, seed and fruit coatings.
- Cosmetics, paper strengthening, agriculture.

### Raw materials in nature
- **Crustacean shells:** crab, shrimp, lobster, crayfish. Main commercial source.
- **Squid pens:** the clear internal "quill" of squid. Very pure, flexible, translucent chitin.
- **Insects:** shed exoskeletons, wings, farmed larvae (black soldier fly, mealworm). Large insects or monster carapaces in fiction.
- **Fungi:** mushroom stalks and mold cell walls.

### Processing (narrative)
Shells are scrubbed and boiled to loosen flesh, then dried in the sun and pounded into flakes. The flakes soak in sour vinegar or dilute acid for a day or more; bubbles rise as the chalky mineral dissolves and the shells turn pale, soft and leathery. They are rinsed and then simmered in strong lye leached from wood ash, which strips away the clinging proteins and fats. Laid out on racks in bright sun or washed with peroxide, the remaining mass bleaches to an off-white. This is raw chitin: tough, insoluble and fibrous.

To make clear sheets the chitin is cooked again, long and hot, in very concentrated lye (near boiling). This changes it into **chitosan**. Washed clean, chitosan dissolves in vinegar into a thick, honey-like syrup. Poured thin onto a level pane of glass or polished stone and left to dry, it becomes a flexible, transparent film that can be peeled away like a sheet of mica. Several films pressed together with heat form a thicker, tougher pane.

Natural wings can also be used directly: harvested, flattened under weight, edged in metal or wood and treated with oil or resin to keep them from cracking.

Sources in insects harden cuticle through **sclerotization**, a natural tanning process using quinones, which darkens and stiffens it. Clear cuticle is the untanned form.

## 6. Other Materials from the "Best" List

### Sapphire (corundum, aluminum oxide)
- **Uses:** watch crystals, camera and scanner covers, armored windows, LED substrates.
- **Sources:** alluvial gravels and metamorphosed limestone (Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Kashmir, Thailand, Madagascar, Montana). Gray, coarse corundum and emery (corundum mixed with iron oxide) occur in larger masses.
- **Processing:** natural stones are cut like other gems with diamond abrasive. Synthetic sapphire is grown by dropping fine aluminum oxide powder through an oxyhydrogen flame (the Verneuil method, from 1902), where it melts and builds into a pear-shaped crystal boule. Larger boules are pulled slowly from a melt.

### Acrylic (PMMA) and Polycarbonate
- **Uses:** acrylic for signs, aquariums, displays and light guides; polycarbonate for riot shields, safety glasses, greenhouses and bulletproof layers.
- **Sources:** petroleum and natural gas chemicals.
- **Processing:** liquid monomers are polymerized with a catalyst or heat into clear plastic, then cast into sheets or melted and molded.

## 7. Preindustrial Clear and Flexible Substitutes

Useful when silicone, plastics or high-temperature glass are out of reach.

- **Horn:** cattle or sheep horn soaked, boiled until soft, split and pressed flat between heated plates, then scraped thin. Translucent amber sheets used for lantern panes ("lanthorns") and hornbook covers.
- **Mica (muscovite):** split from pegmatite books into thin, clear, heat-proof sheets. Used for stove and furnace windows and lantern panes. Historically called "isinglass."
- **Isinglass (fish):** collagen from dried fish swim bladders (sturgeon originally). Soaked into a clear jelly used for glue and clarifying drinks.
- **Gelatin:** boiled from hide and bone. Dries into clear, brittle sheets that soften with heat or water.
- **Natural rubber (latex):** sap tapped from rubber trees (Hevea, Castilla). Amber translucent, elastic. Hardened and stabilized by heating with sulfur (vulcanization, 1839).
- **Gutta-percha:** hardened sap of Palaquium trees. Molded when hot, firm when cool. Used for early cable insulation and dental fillings.
- **Oiled parchment or paper:** scraped skin or paper rubbed with oil or wax to become translucent. Cheap window covering.
- **Selenite (gypsum):** clear crystal that splits into thin panes. Soft and water sensitive.
- **Amber and copal:** fossil and semi-fossil tree resin. Melted resins make clear varnishes and small cast pieces.

## 8. Best Translucent Material by Purpose

| Purpose | Best Material |
|---|---|
| Cheap everyday clarity | Soda-lime glass |
| Lightweight, very clear (92% light transmission) | Acrylic (PMMA) |
| Impact and shatter resistance | Polycarbonate |
| Thermal shock (cookware, lab) | Borosilicate glass |
| Extreme heat or UV transmission | Fused silica |
| Scratch resistance at practical cost | Sapphire |
| Absolute hardness and heat conduction | Diamond |
| Precision optics | Optical glass |
| Flexible, soft and clear | Platinum-cure silicone or optical LSR |
| Flexible and heat resistant | Silicone |
| Clear molds and food contact rubber | Platinum-cure silicone |
| Encapsulating LEDs and electronics | Optical silicone gel or LSR |
| Biodegradable clear film | Chitosan |
| Precise frequency control | Crystalline quartz |
| Preindustrial heat-proof window | Mica |
| Preindustrial cheap translucent pane | Horn or oiled parchment |
| Preindustrial flexible clear-ish material | Natural rubber or isinglass |
