## Skatole vs. Bacteria Through Plastic

Only scent molecules get through. Bacteria cannot pass through intact plastic film at all.

Skatole moves through polyethylene by **permeation** rather than through holes. The molecule dissolves into the polymer surface and wiggles between the tangled polymer chains, slipping through tiny transient gaps that open and close as the chains vibrate. Those gaps are on the order of a nanometer or less. Skatole is a small molecule (about 131 daltons) under a nanometer across, so it fits. Thin grocery bags are especially leaky to nonpolar organic molecules like skatole because they dissolve readily into polyethylene.

A typical bacterium is around 1,000 to 2,000 nanometers long, roughly a thousand times larger than skatole. It can't dissolve into the polymer or fit between chains, so intact film is a complete barrier. Even viruses at 20 to 300 nanometers are blocked.

Bacteria get through plastic only via physical defects:

- Pinholes and punctures
- Tears
- Bad heat seals
- Knotted or folded openings

Genuinely porous materials like paper, cloth and some foams are different. Motile bacteria can swim or grow through their open channels. Some microbes can slowly degrade plastics, but that takes months to years and works by eating the material rather than passing through it.

## Breathable and Waterproof at the Same Time

A material can be fully waterproof and highly breathable because water vapor and liquid water behave very differently.

### Microporous Membranes

Expanded PTFE (Gore-Tex style) is full of pores around 0.2 micrometers wide.

- A single water vapor molecule is about 0.3 nanometers, so it passes easily.
- A raindrop is millions of molecules held together by surface tension. The material is strongly water-repellent, so liquid can't force its way into pores that small without significant pressure.
- 0.2 micrometers is the standard pore size for sterilizing filters, so these membranes also block most bacteria.

### Monolithic Membranes

Solid hydrophilic polyurethane or polyester films have no pores at all. They work the same way skatole moves through a plastic bag: water molecules dissolve into the film on the humid side, diffuse through the polymer chains and evaporate off the drier side. Liquid water and bacteria have nothing to pass through.

### Physical Limits

- **Breathability depends on a humidity difference.** Vapor only moves from the wetter side to the drier side. In hot, humid rain the gradient nearly vanishes and even a perfect membrane barely breathes.
- **Vapor-breathable is not the same as air-permeable.** Monolithic films pass no air. Microporous films pass very little. Neither feels airy like mesh.
- **Thinner means faster but weaker.** Vapor transmission scales inversely with thickness, so a thin membrane needs a backing fabric for durability.
- **Contamination degrades microporous types.** Skin oils and detergents lower surface tension, which lets water wick into the pores.

## Materials That Block Skatole

Skatole is a nonpolar aromatic molecule. Nonpolar polymers absorb it readily and leak it. Dense, polar or crystalline materials and inorganic layers block it.

| Material | Barrier Quality | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Glass | Complete | No measurable organic vapor permeation. Seal quality is the weak point. |
| Metal (cans, steel, aluminum) | Complete | Same as glass. Lids and gaskets are the leak path. |
| Aluminum foil laminate | Excellent | Thicker foil is essentially pinhole-free. Thin foil can have pinholes and creases that crack. |
| Metallized PET (Mylar-type) | Very good | Thin aluminum coating on polyester. Flexing creates micro-cracks over time. |
| PVDC (Saran-type) | Very good | Classic odor barrier used in ostomy pouches. Performs well wet or dry. |
| EVOH | Very good when dry | Common in multilayer food and ostomy films. Loses barrier in high humidity, so it is usually sandwiched between moisture-resistant layers. |
| Polyamide (nylon) film | Good | Polar polymer that resists aromatic absorption. Used in vacuum-seal and smell-proof bags. |
| PET (polyester) | Good | Much better than polyethylene. Used in odor-resistant pouches. |
| Liquid crystal polymer | Excellent | High-end barrier film. Expensive. |
| Activated carbon layer | Absorbs rather than blocks | Traps skatole until saturated. Often added to vented pouches and odor-proof bags. |
| Polyethylene (grocery bags, trash bags) | Poor | Nonpolar. Skatole dissolves in and passes through. |
| Polypropylene | Poor to fair | Slightly better than polyethylene. Still leaks aromatics. |
| Natural rubber, silicone | Poor | Very high organic vapor permeability. |

## Materials That Block Hydrogen

Hydrogen (H2) is the smallest molecule and permeates almost every polymer far faster than larger gases. Atomic hydrogen can even diffuse through some metals. Effective barriers are mostly inorganic.

| Material | Barrier Quality | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Graphene (defect-free) | Complete | Impermeable to H2 molecules. Real sheets are limited by defects and grain boundaries. |
| Glass (soda-lime, borosilicate) | Excellent at room temperature | Permeation becomes measurable at high temperature. Fused silica is more permeable than ordinary glass. |
| Aluminum and aluminum foil | Excellent | Very low hydrogen permeability. The natural oxide layer helps. Foil is limited by pinholes. |
| Copper, gold | Excellent | Used as hydrogen barrier coatings. |
| Austenitic stainless steel (304, 316) | Very good | Much lower permeability than ferritic or carbon steel. Preferred for hydrogen piping. |
| Ceramic coatings (aluminum oxide, silicon oxide, chromium oxide) | Very good | Thin layers applied to steel or polymer to cut permeation by large factors. |
| Carbon steel | Fair | Hydrogen diffuses through as atoms and can cause hydrogen embrittlement. |
| Palladium | None | Hydrogen passes through it readily. Palladium is used as a hydrogen purification membrane. |
| EVOH (dry) | Good for a polymer | Among the best polymer hydrogen barriers. Degrades when humid. |
| PVDC | Good for a polymer | Low gas permeability overall. |
| Polyamide (nylon) | Fair | Used as liners in some hydrogen tanks where slow permeation is acceptable. |
| PET | Fair | Better than polyolefins. |
| HDPE | Poor | Used in Type IV hydrogen tank liners only because some permeation is tolerated. |
| Butyl rubber | Fair | Good general gas barrier. Still leaks hydrogen faster than larger gases. |
| Silicone, natural rubber | Very poor | Among the most gas-permeable materials. |

## Materials That Block Both

For a container or bag that holds both skatole and hydrogen, the reliable choices are:

1. **Glass with a metal or glass seal**
2. **Metal containers** (aluminum or austenitic stainless steel) with low-permeability gaskets
3. **Aluminum foil laminates** (foil sandwiched between PET and polyamide or polyethylene sealing layers)
4. **Multilayer films with EVOH or PVDC** for a flexible polymer option, with a moisture-resistant outer layer to protect the barrier core

In every case the material itself usually outperforms the closure. Seals, zippers, folds and heat-seal seams are where leaks actually happen.
