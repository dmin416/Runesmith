## 5. Cement, Mortar, Stucco and Concrete

### 5.1 Historical context

- **Lime mortar**: used for thousands of years. Hardens slowly by absorbing CO₂ from the air.
- **Roman concrete** (roughly 2nd century BC to 5th century AD): lime mixed with volcanic ash (pozzolana) and rubble. No steel reinforcement.
- **Portland cement**: patented in 1824 in England. In wide use by the late 1800s.
- **Reinforced concrete**: spread rapidly from the 1890s to 1910s.
- **Air-entrained concrete**: adopted widely in North America from the late 1930s and 1940s for freeze-thaw resistance.
- **Modern high-performance concrete**: since the 1980s and 1990s, with low water-cement ratios and supplementary materials (fly ash, slag, silica fume).

### 5.2 Lime mortar

- Soft, porous, flexible. Designed to be sacrificial.
- Moisture and salts leave a wall through the mortar joints rather than through the stone or brick. The joints erode first while the masonry units survive.
- Joints recede gradually. Repointing is typically needed every 50 to 100 years in moderate climates and every 25 to 50 years in exposed coastal or freeze-thaw sites.
- **Hard Portland cement mortar on soft historic masonry** reverses this. Water and salts are forced through the stone or brick instead. Edges of units spall and crumble while the hard mortar stands proud like a grid. This is one of the most common visible defects on 19th-century buildings repointed in the 20th century.

### 5.3 Portland cement concrete: deterioration mechanisms

#### Carbonation
- Fresh concrete is highly alkaline (pH 12.5 to 13.5). This protects embedded steel with a passive oxide film.
- CO₂ diffuses inward and converts calcium hydroxide to calcium carbonate, dropping pH below about 9. Once this front reaches the steel, the protection is lost.
- Carbonation depth roughly follows **depth = k × √time**:

| Concrete quality | k (mm per √year) | Time to reach 25 mm (1 in) | Time to reach 50 mm (2 in) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense, high quality | 1 to 2 | 156 to 625 years | 625+ years |
| Average | 3 to 4 | 39 to 69 years | 156 to 278 years |
| Poor, porous | 5 to 8 | 10 to 25 years | 39 to 100 years |

- Carbonation runs fastest at 50 to 70% relative humidity. Sheltered outdoor surfaces (balcony soffits, underside of canopies) often carbonate faster than rain-washed surfaces. Rain-washed concrete carbonates more slowly but stays wetter, so corrosion moves faster once the front arrives.
- Dry indoor concrete carbonates readily but the steel rarely corrodes much because there is too little moisture.
- Field test: phenolphthalein sprayed on a fresh break turns pink where concrete is still alkaline and stays colorless where it has carbonated.

#### Chloride-induced corrosion
- Chlorides from seawater, sea spray or de-icing salt diffuse into concrete.
- Once chloride at the steel exceeds roughly 0.2 to 0.4% by mass of cement, the passive film breaks down locally, causing pitting corrosion. This happens even in fully alkaline concrete.
- Rust occupies two to six times the volume of the original steel.
- Sequence of visible damage:
  1. Rust stains bleeding through the surface
  2. Hairline cracks running along the line of reinforcing bars
  3. Delamination (concrete sounds hollow when tapped)
  4. Spalling, exposing corroded bars
  5. Section loss in bars, eventual structural weakness
- Corners and edges fail first because chloride and CO₂ reach the steel from two directions.
- Pitting can cut bars severely with little surface warning. Prestressing strands and post-tensioned tendons are especially vulnerable because they carry high stress in small cross-sections.

#### Freeze-thaw
- **Scaling**: surface flakes off in thin layers, worst with de-icing salts.
- **D-cracking**: closely spaced cracks along joints in pavements, caused by porous aggregates.
- **Popouts**: conical pieces pop out above porous aggregate particles.
- Air entrainment (4 to 8% tiny air bubbles) gives freezing water room to expand and largely prevents damage.

#### Alkali-silica reaction (ASR)
- Certain silica-rich aggregates react with alkalis in cement to form a gel that swells as it absorbs water.
- Visible as **map cracking** (random polygonal crack networks), often with dark staining or white gel exuding from cracks. Pavements and slabs may show joint closure and buckling.
- Typically appears 5 to 15 years after construction and can progress for decades.
- Examples: Mactaquac Dam in New Brunswick, Canada (built 1960s) suffered such severe ASR expansion that major parts required ongoing slot cutting and planned replacement. Seabrook Station nuclear plant in New Hampshire has documented ASR in some structures.

#### Sulfate attack
- Sulfates from soil, groundwater or seawater react with cement compounds to form expansive ettringite and gypsum.
- Concrete swells, cracks, softens and loses cohesion, often starting at edges and corners.
- Seawater contains sulfates. Its chlorides partly suppress the expansion. Marine sulfate attack is usually slower than in sulfate-rich soils.
- **Delayed ettringite formation** affects some steam-cured precast elements years after manufacture.

#### Leaching and efflorescence
- Water percolating through cracks dissolves calcium hydroxide. White crusts, drips and small stalactites form under bridges, parking decks and retaining walls.
- Long-term leaching increases porosity and weakens the paste.

#### Abrasion and erosion
- Spillways, channels and pavements lose surface paste, exposing aggregate.
- High-velocity water causes cavitation pitting.
- Sand-laden wind etches desert concrete near ground level.

#### Biogenic acid
- In sewers, bacteria convert hydrogen sulfide gas into sulfuric acid on pipe crowns. Unprotected concrete sewers can lose several millimeters per year.

#### Thermal and shrinkage cracking
- Drying shrinkage and daily thermal movement cause cracks where joints are absent or poorly placed.
- Concrete expands roughly 10 to 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, similar to steel. This compatibility is part of why reinforced concrete works.

#### UV
- Cement paste is essentially immune to UV.
- Joint sealants, coatings and waterproofing membranes degrade in sun. Failed sealants let water and chlorides in.

### 5.4 Concrete by environment

| Environment | Dominant mechanism | Typical unprotected service life before major repair |
|---|---|---|
| Dry inland, indoors | Carbonation (little corrosion) | 100+ years |
| Temperate inland, outdoors | Carbonation, freeze-thaw | 50 to 100+ years |
| Cold climate with de-icing salts | Chloride corrosion, scaling | 20 to 50 years (bridge decks, parking garages) |
| Temperate marine atmospheric | Chloride corrosion | 30 to 60 years |
| Marine splash and tidal | Chloride corrosion, abrasion | 15 to 40 years |
| Hot humid coastal (Persian Gulf, Gulf of Mexico, tropics) | Chloride corrosion at high temperature | 10 to 30 years |
| Hot dry desert inland | Thermal cracking, abrasion, salt from soil | 50+ years |
| Submerged seawater | Slow sulfate and magnesium attack | 50 to 100+ years |

Chemical reactions and diffusion roughly double in rate for every 10°C rise. Hot coastal climates combine high salt with high temperature and humidity, the worst case for reinforced concrete. Much concrete built during the 1970s Persian Gulf construction boom showed severe corrosion within 10 to 15 years.

Modern marine specifications target 50 to 100+ years using 50 to 75 mm (2 to 3 in) of cover over steel, low water-cement ratios, slag or fly ash, corrosion inhibitors, epoxy-coated or stainless reinforcement and sometimes cathodic protection.

### 5.5 Visual pattern of a marine concrete structure

- **Below low tide**: often in surprisingly good condition, with marine growth.
- **Tidal zone**: abrasion, barnacles, softened paste, exposed aggregate.
- **Splash zone**: the worst band. Heavy rust staining, spalling, exposed bars, delaminated corners.
- **Upper structure**: rust streaks below cracks. Seaward faces worse than landward. Underside of decks and beams worse than top surfaces (salt collects without being rinsed by rain).

### 5.6 Roman marine concrete

- Harbor structures across the Mediterranean (for example at Caesarea Maritima and Portus Cosanus) survived roughly 2,000 years in seawater.
- Seawater percolating through lime and volcanic ash grows minerals such as aluminous tobermorite and phillipsite in pores and cracks. The material becomes denser and more cohesive over time.
- Research published in 2023 showed that lumps of unmixed lime in Roman concrete act as a reservoir: water entering cracks dissolves the lime and redeposits calcium carbonate, sealing the crack.
- The decisive factor: no steel. Without reinforcement to rust and expand, there is nothing inside the concrete to split it apart.
- Roman concrete was weaker in compression than modern concrete and took far longer to harden. Its durability came from chemistry and the absence of rebar, not raw strength.

### 5.7 Stucco and render

- **Cement stucco**: hairline and map cracking within the first years, efflorescence, dirt streaking under sills, delamination where water gets behind. Typically 50 to 80 years with maintenance.
- **Lime render**: softer, breathable, needs periodic limewash. Erodes gradually rather than cracking off in sheets.
- **Common failure**: water entering at window perimeters and roof edges, then trapped behind the stucco. Hidden framing or masonry decays while the surface looks intact except for staining and bulges.
