# Printer Worm

A biological fabricator that synthesizes liquid precursors in internal glands, then extrudes, sprays or casts them into solid materials. Every material below has a real biological analog. Entries marked **(magic)** extend real chemistry past what any known animal does.

The **(magic)** rubber rows here are the worm making polyisoprene itself.

## Fabrication anatomy

| Organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Feed gut | Digests protein, sugars, minerals and trace metals. Excess is stored in the fat body as reserves. |
| Gland bank | Separate reservoirs keep reactive components apart: proteins, enzymes, oxidizers, mineral ions, waxes, terpenes. |
| Mixing chamber | Combines two or more streams just before exit so curing starts at the nozzle instead of inside the body. |
| Spinneret / nozzle | Variable aperture. Controls shear, draw speed, pH, ion content and water removal. Different nozzles for fiber, bead, spray and sheet. |
| Multi-nozzle head | Co-extrusion of core and sheath fibers, side-by-side fibers and continuous material gradients. |
| Hemolymph pump | Hydraulic pressure drives extrusion. Spiders already extend their legs with hemolymph pressure. |
| Sensory nozzle | Chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors give feedback on viscosity, temperature and cure state. |
| Thermal gland | Exothermic bombardier-style reaction supplies controlled heat for melting, molding and vulcanizing. |
| Recycling glands | Proteases, chitinases and degumming enzymes dissolve old prints for reuse. |

## Curing toolbox

Every material sets through one or more of these mechanisms.

| Mechanism | How it works | Speed | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enzymatic dityrosine cross-linking | Peroxidase plus hydrogen peroxide turns tyrosine residues into radicals that pair into dityrosine and trityrosine bonds. | Seconds to minutes | Resilin, abductin analogs, gelatin rubber |
| Photo cross-linking | A photosensitizer such as riboflavin activates under light and triggers the same tyrosine bonds. | Seconds under bright light | Resilin, collagen gels, fine detail work |
| Quinone tanning (sclerotization) | Phenoloxidase converts catechols into quinones that bind proteins to each other and to chitin. | Minutes to hours | Cuticle, armor plates, egg cases |
| Metal coordination | DOPA and histidine residues grab iron, zinc, calcium or copper ions to form strong reversible bonds. | Seconds | Byssal threads, sandcastle cement, self-healing coatings |
| Shear and pH self-assembly | Liquid crystalline dope is acidified, swapped from sodium to potassium ions, dewatered and stretched so proteins lock into beta sheets. | Instant on draw | All silks |
| Coacervation | Oppositely charged proteins separate into a dense liquid phase that flows into gaps then hardens. | Seconds to minutes | Underwater cements |
| Disulfide bonding | Cysteine residues oxidize into sulfur bridges. | Minutes to hours | Keratin, hagfish threads |
| Lysyl oxidase cross-linking | Copper enzyme links lysines into desmosine bridges. | Hours | Elastin, collagen |
| Thermal set | Melt and cool. | Seconds | Waxes, shellac, suckerin, gutta-percha |
| Dehydration | Water removal locks structure in place. | Seconds to hours | Silk films, mucus coatings, paper |
| Biomineralization | Amorphous mineral precursors are laid onto a protein template then crystallize. | Hours to days (faster with magic) | Shell, bone, enamel, glass, metal oxides |
| Terpene polymerization | Volatile terpenes evaporate while heavier terpenoids oxidize into a tacky solid. | Minutes on air exposure | Nasute termite glue, polyisoprene rubber |

## Resilin

### Chemistry

- The precursor is pro-resilin, a water-soluble protein built from repeating motifs rich in glycine, proline and tyrosine.
- In fruit flies the gene has three main regions: an elastic N-terminal repeat block, a chitin-binding domain in the middle and a second elastic repeat block at the C-terminal end.
- The chains are intrinsically disordered random coils with almost no crystalline regions. This is why the material behaves like a near-ideal rubber.
- Cross-links are dityrosine and trityrosine bonds formed between tyrosine side chains.
- Dityrosine fluoresces blue under UV light, so cured resilin glows under a blacklight. Useful for checking print quality.

### Natural properties

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Resilience (energy returned) | About 92 to 97%. Working figure elsewhere is about 95%. |
| Extension before breaking | Up to about 300% for recombinant resilin |
| Stiffness (hydrated) | Roughly 0.6 to 2 MPa, soft rubber range |
| Stiffness (dry) | Rises to the GPa range, glassy and stiff |
| Fatigue life | Insect wing hinges flex hundreds of millions of times over a lifetime |
| Water requirement | Must stay hydrated to stay rubbery |

### Production process

1. **Synthesis.** Silk-gland-style cells build pro-resilin and store it as a concentrated aqueous solution. Neutral pH, antioxidants and the total absence of oxidizer keep it liquid indefinitely.
2. **Catalyst storage.** A second reservoir holds peroxidase and hydrogen peroxide. Bombardier beetles already store hydrogen peroxide safely in a sealed gland. An alternate reservoir holds a photosensitizer for light curing.
3. **Mixing.** Both streams meet in the mixing chamber. The ratio sets cure speed and cross-link density.
4. **Deposition.** Bead extrusion for springs and straps, spray for coatings, pour into molds for blocks and pads, injection into a hollow chitin shell for hinges.
5. **Gelation.** The liquid reaches its gel point within seconds to minutes depending on enzyme load.
6. **Post-cure.** Remaining tyrosines continue linking for some time after deposition. Final properties settle once this finishes.
7. **Hydration lock.** A wax coating or a hydrogel sheath keeps water in so the part stays rubbery in dry air.

### Tuning controls

| Variable | Effect |
|---|---|
| Cross-link density | More links make it stiffer with less stretch. Fewer links make it softer and stretchier. |
| Protein concentration | Higher concentration gives a stiffer, tougher part. |
| Water content | Wetter is softer and more resilient. Drier is stiffer and more brittle. |
| Plasticizers | Glycerol or trehalose keeps it flexible even when dry. |
| Chitin blending | A continuous gradient from pure resilin into hard chitin removes any weak joint between soft and hard parts. Locust hinges and flea leg pads work this way. |
| Fiber reinforcement | Silk fibers embedded in resilin give a spring that is stiff in one direction and stretchy in another. |
| Hybrid proteins | Blocks of silk, elastin or collagen spliced into resilin chains add strength, cell adhesion or controlled breakdown. |

### Resilin products

- **Springs:** leaf, coil, torsion and pad springs.
- **Catapult mechanisms:** latch-and-release jumpers modeled on fleas and froghoppers that store energy slowly then release it in milliseconds.
- **Hinges:** flexures that bend millions of times without wearing out.
- **Gaskets, seals and valves.**
- **Shock mounts, soles and padding.**
- **Slingshot and launcher bands.**
- **Bouncing balls** with very high rebound.
- **Acoustic membranes:** cicada sound organs use resilin to snap and vibrate.
- **Tendons and artificial muscle anchors** for printed living tissue.

### Natural rubber process (magic)

Insects already build isoprenoids for hormones, pheromones and defensive sprays. Extending that pathway produces long polyisoprene chains.

1. **Latex gland** secretes tiny rubber particles suspended in watery serum.
2. **Coagulation.** The formic acid gland drops the pH and the particles clump into solid raw rubber. Real latex is coagulated with formic acid.
3. **Compounding.** A sulfur reservoir plus zinc oxide (zinc comes from the same metabolism that hardens mandibles) is mixed in.
4. **Vulcanization.** The thermal gland heats the rubber so sulfur bridges form between chains. Raw sticky rubber becomes durable elastic rubber.
5. **Shaping.** Extrude as tubing, press into sheets, coat fabric or cast in molds before the heat cure.
6. **Choosing the isomer.** cis chains make soft natural rubber. trans chains make hard gutta-percha.

### Choosing a rubber

- **Maximum bounce:** resilin.
- **Compression load:** abductin.
- **Long cyclic stretching:** elastin.
- **Absorb a hit without rebound:** flagelliform silk.
- **Reshape later:** suckerin or gutta-percha.
- **Recycle and reprint:** velvet worm slime.
- **Self-repair:** byssal thread.
- **Dry environments without coatings:** vulcanized natural rubber.
- **Cheap bulk:** gelatin rubber.

## Silks

Protein silks. Ned's silk is cellulose and stays in `BiologicalSilks.md`. Do not replace that lock with this table.

**Shared process:** Silk proteins are stored as a liquid crystalline dope at very high concentration. Along the narrowing spinning duct the gland drops the pH, swaps sodium for potassium ions, pulls out water and stretches the stream. These changes force the proteins into aligned beta sheets and the liquid becomes a solid fiber. Draw speed controls strength. Faster drawing gives stronger, stiffer fibers.

## Minerals

**Shared process:** Ions are concentrated inside sealed vesicles and laid down as an amorphous paste such as amorphous calcium carbonate or amorphous calcium phosphate. This paste can be shaped like clay. A protein or chitin template then guides crystallization into the final form. ## Living tissue

**Process:** Cells are suspended in a collagen or silk hydrogel bio-ink and extruded layer by layer. Channels are left open or filled with dissolvable mucus to become blood vessels. The print is then bathed in hemolymph so cells can grow, fuse and mature.

| Tissue | Uses |
|---|---|
| Muscle | Actuators that contract on signal |
| Nerve | Wiring for sensors and control |
| Skin and mucosa | Self-healing outer layers |
| Cartilage and bone grafts | Medical repair |
| Seeded scaffolds | Silk or collagen frames that cells grow over. Replacement organs, living architecture that heals damage |
| Symbiotic cultures | Glowing bacteria, cellulose bacteria, fungal mycelium bricks |

## Costs and limits

- **Protein:** silk, resilin, collagen and keratin all need large amounts of dietary protein. Heavy printing requires heavy eating or long refill times.
- **Minerals and metals:** shell, glass and hardened edges need calcium, silica, iron, copper or zinc in the diet.
- **Water:** most cures release or consume water. Dry environments slow aqueous printing.
- **Heat:** thermal molding and vulcanization draw on the bombardier reaction, which consumes stored chemicals.
- **Cure time:** enzymatic and photo cures are fast. Biomineralization is slow without magic acceleration.
- **Gland capacity:** reservoir size limits how much can be printed in one session.
