## 5. Hard in Summer, Tough in Rain

Four real mechanisms combine to produce this behavior.

### 5.1 Hydration Plasticization

Water acts as a plasticizer for proteins. Dry collagen and dry resilin are glassy and hard. Wet collagen and wet resilin are flexible and rubbery. Summer drives out water, so the material stiffens. Rain soaks the surface, so the material becomes tough and impact-absorbing instead of soft and muddy.

### 5.2 Heat-Triggered Collapse (LCST Behavior)

Elastin-like polypeptides (repeating Val-Pro-Gly-X-Gly units, studied extensively by Dan Urry) dissolve in cool water and then collapse into dense aggregates above a transition temperature. Heat drives water out of the oily parts of the chains so they pack tighter. The transition point can be tuned anywhere from near freezing to near boiling by changing the sequence. The synthetic polymer PNIPAM does the same at about 32°C.

Built into CR binder, this produces a material that **tightens and stiffens as temperature rises**. This is the exact opposite of asphalt.

### 5.3 Crosslinks Prevent Dissolving

A covalently crosslinked network can swell but cannot dissolve, the same way a rubber band soaks in water without melting away. Crosslink density sets the balance:

- Too few crosslinks: swells into mush.
- Too many crosslinks: brittle and prone to cracking.
- Correct density: swells a few percent in rain and stays intact.

### 5.4 Hydrophobic Skin

Insect cuticle is waterproofed by a thin outer wax layer. Lotus leaves shed water through microscopic surface texture. CR binder can bloom a waxy skin to the surface as it cures, shedding most rain before it soaks in.

### 5.5 Comparison with Asphalt

| Condition | Asphalt (real) | CR binder road (suggested canon) |
|---|---|---|
| Hot summer | Softens, ruts, bleeds tar | Hardest state |
| Cold winter | Brittle, thermal cracking | Stiff; frost risk if wet (see Infrastructure 9.4) |
| Rain | Water strips binder from stone, starting potholes | Surface hydrates into a tough rubbery skin; wet adhesion like mussel glue |
| Heavy loads | Slow deformation over time | Sacrificial bonds absorb impact, small cracks partially close |

### 5.6 Behavior by Condition

| Condition | Surface state | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hot and dry | Glassy, hardest | Maximum compressive strength, no dust |
| Mild and dry | Hard | Normal service |
| Light rain | Top few mm hydrate | Rubbery skin gives wet traction; bulk stays stiff |
| Prolonged soaking | Slight swelling | Requires expansion joints on rigid grades |
| Freezing while wet | Frost risk | Antifreeze protein domains solve it (Infrastructure 9.4) |

### 5.7 Silk Components in Sun and Rain

- **Dragline supercontraction:** rain tightens dragline cables and reinforcement. Used deliberately, structures with dragline fibers clamp tighter when wet. Uncontrolled, it can crack brittle surrounding material. Permanent works use minor ampullate silk (no supercontraction) or dragline pre-shrunk by soaking under tension during manufacture.
- **Aggregate glue:** real spider glue peaks in stickiness at the humidity of the spider's habitat. Desert and rainforest species tune it differently. Construction tapes and traps can be tuned the same way.
- **Cribellate fibers:** grip best when dry; suited to indoor fasteners and dry-climate climbing gear.
- **Tubuliform and minor ampullate silk:** change little with humidity; the stable backbone for outdoor fabric.
- **Hydrophobin skin:** forms at the surface during curing and keeps rain from reaching the silk at all.
- **Cold:** silk stays tough far below freezing, so silk reinforcement offsets the cold-weather stiffening of binder-dominant grades.

## 6. Cure Triggers and Processing

### 6.1 Cure Triggers

| Trigger | Real analog | Best uses |
|---|---|---|
| Drying | Hide glue, gelatin, casein | Glues, paints, sheets |
| Sunlight or UV | Riboflavin-UV and ruthenium photo-crosslinking | Roads, roofs, surface seals |
| Calcium or mineral contact | Alginate gels instantly in calcium chloride solution | Concrete, mortar, underwater casting |
| Low heat (60-150°C) | Thermosetting resins, baking | Bricks, tiles, windows |
| pH shift | Microbial cement; casein curdling in acid | Soil stabilization |
| Air exposure | Quinone tanning of insect cuticle | Large castings that cure from the outside inward |
| Mana pulse | None | Instant controlled set by a trained worker |
| Pressure | Compaction, sintering | Rammed earth, pressed tiles |
| Shear and drawing | Spider and silkworm spinning | Fibers, cables, fabrics |
| Seawater ions | Sandcastle worm and caddisfly cements | Underwater masonry and bonding |
| Air-water interface | Hydrophobin self-assembly | Water-repellent skins |
| Enzyme cascade trigger | Blood clotting | Instant set across large pours |
| Stretching | Hagfish threads converting from coil to sheet | Strengthening gels into fibers |
| Heat with moisture | Suckerin thermoplastic molding | Remoldable parts |

### 6.2 Processing Methods

- **Casting** in wooden, clay or fabric molds.
- **Slip-forming**: a moving form that extrudes continuous curbs, ditch liners or walls.
- **Spraying** onto slopes, tunnel walls or ditches in the style of shotcrete.
- **Ramming and compaction** for earth walls, road bases and blocks.
- **Extrusion** through dies for pipes and fibers (real collagen sausage casings are extruded this way).
- **Pressing and laminating** for tiles, panels and armor.
- **Foaming** by whipping air into dilute binder for insulation and floats.
- **Dipping and brushing** for sealing timber, rope, cloth and stone.
- **Layered deposition**: thin layers built up one at a time, each cured by sunlight or a mana pulse before the next.

### 6.3 Stock Solutions (suggested canon)

| Stock name | Binder in water | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| Neat | Undiluted secretion | Windows, seals, armor |
| Heavy | 1 part to 2-4 parts water | Glue, sealant, protein glass |
| Working | 1 part to 10-20 parts water | Concrete, mortar, road surfaces |
| Spray | 1 part to 50-200 parts water | Soil stabilization, dust control, erosion control |
| Dope | 30-50% silk protein in water, as stored in spider glands | Spinning fibers, drawing cables |
| Thread gel | 1 part to 10,000-25,000 parts water | Instant gel barriers, Tier 3 feedstock |

## 7. Master Material List

Mix percentages are binder as a share of total dry solids, Tier 1 unamplified (suggested canon, scaled from the best real biopolymer additives). Divide by the amplification factor where seeding magic applies.

### Group A: Earthworks and Infrastructure

#### 7.1 Stabilized Soil and Road Base

- **Replaces:** cement or lime soil stabilization (real practice uses 3-10% cement or 3-8% lime).
- **Mix:** native soil or crushed stone with 0.5-1% binder at spray strength, compacted at optimum moisture.
- **Mechanism:** protein coats and bridges soil grains; collagen templates calcium carbonate at grain contacts.
- **Real precedent:** biopolymers such as xanthan gum at 0.5-2% of soil mass produce large gains in soil strength in published studies. Microbial cement turns sand into sandstone.
- **Magic:** templated nucleation plus mana energy replaces bacterial metabolism.
- **Limits:** clay-rich soils need more binder; organic topsoil must be stripped first.

#### 7.2 Road Wearing Surface (Asphalt Substitute)

- **Replaces:** asphalt concrete (real bitumen content about 4-7%).
- **Mix:** well-graded crushed stone and sand with 2-4% binder at 70:30 collagen to resilin. Sun-cured.
- **Mechanism:** heat-stiffening plus rain-toughening (Section 5).
- **Properties:** hard and smooth when dry; tough and grippy when wet; does not rut in heat.
- **Magic:** crosslink cascade triggered by sunlight; waxy surface bloom sheds water.
- **Limits:** needs a well-drained base like any road.

#### 7.3 Gravel Road Binder and Dust Control

- **Replaces:** oil, calcium chloride and lignosulfonate dust suppressants (all real).
- **Mix:** spray-strength binder applied to compacted gravel, 0.2-0.5%.
- **Mechanism:** binds fine particles to the surface so traffic does not lift them.
- **Real precedent:** lignosulfonates (a papermaking byproduct) are sprayed on real gravel roads for exactly this purpose.
- **Limits:** must be renewed every season or two on busy roads.

#### 7.4 Rammed Earth and Stabilized Earth Blocks

- **Replaces:** fired brick, cement-stabilized earth.
- **Mix:** sandy clay subsoil with 1-2% binder, rammed in forms or pressed into blocks.
- **Real precedent:** rammed earth walls survive centuries in dry climates. Historical mortars across many cultures used blood, egg white, animal glue and casein as protein additives. Chinese builders used sticky rice in lime mortar.
- **Magic:** pressure triggers crosslinking; wall surfaces become water-shedding.
- **Limits:** thick walls required; still vulnerable to standing water at the base without a raised plinth.

#### 7.5 Canal, Pond, Cistern and Reservoir Liners

- **Replaces:** puddled clay, concrete, stone lining.
- **Mix:** 3-5 cm shell of sand with 3-5% binder at 50:50, sprayed or troweled.
- **Mechanism:** dense low-permeability layer; resilin content lets it flex as soil settles instead of cracking.
- **Magic:** calcium-triggered set allows application underwater.
- **Limits:** burrowing animals; root penetration if left unprotected.

#### 7.6 Levees, Embankments and Slope Armor

- **Replaces:** riprap, gabions, concrete slope paving.
- **Mix:** spray binder over seeded soil for light protection; bound stone mats for heavy flow.
- **Real precedent:** hydroseeding uses guar-based tackifiers to hold seed and soil on slopes until plants root.
- **Magic:** surface crust strong enough to resist flood scour while letting grass push through.
- **Limits:** a levee is only as strong as its weakest section; seepage under the structure still needs cutoff walls.

#### 7.7 Culverts and Drainage Pipes

- **Replaces:** fired clay pipe, concrete pipe, corrugated metal.
- **Mix:** sand with 3-4% binder at 70:30, cast around an inflatable bladder or a collapsible reed core.
- **Properties:** smooth bore (flow roughness similar to concrete); flexible enough to tolerate settling.
- **Real precedent:** flexible pipes are designed to deflect slightly and rely on compacted backfill for support (real engineering practice limits deflection to about 5%).
- **Limits:** backfill compaction matters as much as the pipe itself.

### Group B: Structural Masonry

#### 7.8 Concrete Substitute

- **Replaces:** Portland cement concrete (real cement content about 10-15% of the mix by mass).
- **Mix:** gravel, sand and limestone dust with 1-3% binder, calcium-triggered set.
- **Mechanism:** binder bonds aggregate and templates calcium carbonate growth in the pores, densifying over the first weeks.
- **Properties (suggested canon):** Tier 1 roughly 15-30 MPa compressive strength, similar to ordinary concrete. Amplified nacre-structured grades approach bone at 100+ MPa.
- **Real precedent:** microbially grown bricks meet real masonry unit standards. Nacre and bone show what organized protein-mineral composites can reach.
- **Magic:** no kiln, no carbon dioxide release, no 28-day wait (sets in hours with a mana pulse).
- **Limits:** slow creep under constant heavy load; long-span beams still need reinforcement.

#### 7.9 Mortar and Grout

- **Replaces:** lime mortar (real binder about a quarter of the mix by volume), cement mortar.
- **Mix:** sand with 2-5% binder.
- **Mechanism:** strong adhesion to stone; resilin content gives the joint slight flex so walls tolerate settling and earthquakes.
- **Real precedent:** protein-enhanced historical mortars; flexible lime mortars outlast rigid cement mortars in old masonry.
- **Limits:** joints should stay weaker than the stone so repairs are possible.

#### 7.10 Unfired Bricks

- **Replaces:** kiln-fired brick.
- **Mix:** sand or crushed stone with 2-3% binder, pressed and sun-cured or low-heat cured.
- **Real precedent:** bioMASON grows bricks from sand and bacteria at room temperature.
- **Magic:** a brickyard needs molds, sunlight and the binder; the fuel-hungry kiln disappears.
- **Limits:** quality control depends on consistent aggregate.

#### 7.11 Harbor Accretion Stone (Tier 2)

- **Replaces:** quarried breakwater stone, poured marine concrete.
- **Process:** a binder-coated framework of timber, iron or woven reed is placed in seawater. The binder pulls calcium and magnesium out of the passing water and grows a mineral shell, thickening for months.
- **Real precedent:** Biorock structures grow this way using electric current.
- **Properties:** grows thicker with time; self-repairs when damaged as long as seawater flows.
- **Limits:** slow; needs moving water; cold seas and low-salinity water grow more slowly.

#### 7.12 Structural Beams and Posts (Fiber Composite)

- **Replaces:** large timber, glulam.
- **Mix:** straw, reed, bamboo or flax fiber with 5-10% binder, pressed in long molds.
- **Real precedent:** soy-protein adhesives bond commercial plywood; casein glue was used in early aircraft frames; real particleboard and fiberboard rely on similar principles.
- **Magic:** collagen-fiber alignment during pressing produces wood-like grain along the beam's length.
- **Limits:** must be kept from rot like timber; fire behavior similar to wood.

#### 7.13 Tensile Reinforcement (Collagen Rebar)

- **Replaces:** iron or steel reinforcing bar.
- **Form:** extruded collagen fiber cables, neat or heavy stock, aligned and stretched while curing.
- **Real precedent:** sinew bowstrings and sinew lashing; tendon tensile strength rivals steel per unit weight; sinew shrinks as it dries, tightening around joints.
- **Advantage:** does not rust, so it never expands and cracks the surrounding masonry (a major failure mode of real reinforced concrete).
- **Limits:** lower stiffness than steel, so structures flex more under load.

### Group C: Transparent Materials

#### 7.14 Quartz-Matched Glass Composite (Windows)

- **Replaces:** soda-lime glass.
- **Mix:** finely ground quartz sand (very fine and evenly sized) with 10-20% binder at 90:10, cast in flat molds and cured under pressure with no air bubbles.
- **Scientific basis (real):** transparency in a composite depends on refractive index matching. When two materials bend light by the same amount, the boundary between them disappears. Quartz has a refractive index of about 1.54-1.55. Dry dense protein falls around 1.54-1.6. Real "transparent wood" works by filling wood with a polymer index-matched to cellulose.
- **Magic:** binder tunes its own refractive index to match the sand exactly.
- **Properties:** tougher than glass because the protein phase stops cracks; shatters into blunt fragments.
- **Limits:** any mismatch or trapped air produces haze; proteins slowly yellow in sunlight, so old windows develop an amber tint.

#### 7.15 Protein Glass Sheets

- **Replaces:** small glass panes, lantern windows.
- **Mix:** neat binder at 90:10, poured thin and dried flat.
- **Real precedent:** lanterns used thinly shaved horn as translucent panes before cheap glass ("lanthorn" is the old spelling). Silk fibroin films reach over 90% visible light transmission in modern research.
- **Properties:** light, unbreakable in ordinary use, slightly flexible.
- **Limits:** softer surface than glass, so it scratches; best for lanterns, small windows and goggles.

#### 7.16 Cold-Cast Biosilica Glass (Tier 2 or 3)

- **Replaces:** blown and plate glass.
- **Real basis:** diatoms build glass shells from dissolved silica at sea temperature using proteins called silaffins (Kröger et al., 1999). Sponges use an enzyme called silicatein to assemble glass spicules. Both make glass without heat.
- **Magical extension:** binder carries silicatein-like activity, partially dissolving sand grains and redepositing the silica as continuous glass between them.
- **Properties:** true glass hardness with protein-enhanced toughness.
- **Limits:** high-magic grade; slow cure in thick sections.

### Group D: Elastic Materials

#### 7.17 Seals, Gaskets and Hoses

- **Replaces:** natural rubber, leather gaskets.
- **Mix:** neat binder at 20:80, cast or extruded.
- **Properties:** high resilience; holds shape for hundreds of millions of cycles.
- **Limits:** pure resilin has modest tensile strength, so hoses need woven fiber reinforcement.

#### 7.18 Tires, Belts and Shock Mounts

- **Replaces:** rubber tires, leather drive belts, spring mounts.
- **Mix:** 20:80 binder reinforced with collagen fiber cords, the same way real tires use fabric or steel cords.
- **Additives:** carbon black (soot) or iron oxide for UV protection and wear resistance, as in real tires.
- **Advantage:** very low energy loss means wagon wheels roll easier and run cooler.

#### 7.19 Springs and Energy Storage

- **Replaces:** steel springs, sinew torsion bundles in siege engines, bow limbs.
- **Real precedent:** fleas store energy in resilin pads and release it in under a millisecond to jump.
- **Uses:** crossbows, catapults, clockwork, door closers, vehicle suspension.
- **Limits:** must stay hydrated for best elasticity; dry resilin turns glassy and stiff.

#### 7.20 Expansion Joints and Bridge Bearings

- **Replaces:** steel and rubber bridge bearings, joint fillers.
- **Form:** thick 20:80 pads under beams and strips between slabs.
- **Function:** lets structures expand, contract and shift without cracking.

### Group E: Adhesives and Coatings

#### 7.21 Wood and Stone Adhesive

- **Replaces:** hide glue, resin glues.
- **Mix:** heavy stock, applied warm or cold.
- **Real precedent:** hide glue (pure collagen) has been used for thousands of years. Joints made with it often break through the wood before the glue fails. Luthiers still use it.
- **Advantage over real hide glue:** crosslinked grade does not soften in heat or humidity.
- **Option:** a reversible grade that releases with steam for repairable joinery.

#### 7.22 Paint, Primer and Gesso

- **Replaces:** oil paint, limewash.
- **Mix:** working stock with chalk, clay or mineral pigment.
- **Real precedent:** gesso is literally animal glue (collagen) plus chalk or gypsum. Casein paint and egg tempera are protein paints that have lasted centuries.
- **Properties:** durable, breathable, water-resistant once cured.

#### 7.23 Sealant, Caulk and Roof Coating

- **Replaces:** pitch, tar, oakum, lead flashing.
- **Mix:** heavy stock at 50:50 with fine sand or fiber filler.
- **Uses:** roof seams, ship seams, window frames, barrel joints.
- **Real precedent:** mussel adhesive proteins bond to wet surfaces underwater.

#### 7.24 Plaster and Stucco

- **Replaces:** lime and gypsum plaster.
- **Mix:** fine sand and chalk with 1-3% binder.
- **Real precedent:** animal glue was added to historical plasters to control setting time.
- **Properties:** crack-resistant and washable; exterior stucco sheds rain.

### Group F: Sheets and Fibers

#### 7.25 Membranes and Leather Substitute

- **Replaces:** leather, oiled canvas, tarpaulin.
- **Form:** neat binder cast in sheets or soaked into woven cloth.
- **Real precedent:** parchment and vellum are dried collagen sheets; tanning makes leather rot-resistant.
- **Uses:** tents, waterproof covers, buckets, bellows, boots.

#### 7.26 Paper, Pasteboard and Parchment

- **Replaces:** rag paper, vellum.
- **Mix:** plant pulp with 1-3% binder as sizing.
- **Real precedent:** European papermakers sized paper with gelatin for centuries so ink would not bleed.
- **Properties:** strong, water-resistant, long-lived.

#### 7.27 Rope, Cordage and Textile Fiber

- **Replaces:** hemp rope, sinew, leather thong.
- **Form:** extruded collagen filaments, drawn and twisted.
- **Real precedent:** extruded collagen casings; regenerated protein fibers; tendon.
- **Feature:** lashing shrinks and tightens as it dries, like real sinew.

#### 7.28 Roof Tiles and Shingles (Nacre Structure)

- **Replaces:** fired clay tile, slate.
- **Mix:** fine clay platelets layered with binder in thin pressed stacks, imitating nacre's "brick and mortar" structure.
- **Real precedent:** layered clay and polymer films have reached tensile strengths around 400 MPa in the lab (Podsiadlo et al., 2007).
- **Properties:** thin, light, very tough against hail.

### Group G: Lightweight Materials

#### 7.29 Insulation Foam

- **Replaces:** straw, wool, cork insulation.
- **Process:** whip dilute binder into a stiff foam (like meringue) and cure.
- **Real precedent:** protein-based firefighting foams made from hydrolyzed hoof and horn.
- **Properties:** insulating value comparable to modern foams (thermal conductivity around 0.03-0.05 W/m·K); fire-resistant when mineral-filled.

#### 7.30 Lightweight Blocks and Floats

- **Replaces:** cork floats, pumice, aerated concrete.
- **Mix:** foam plus sand or ash, cast in blocks.
- **Uses:** pontoons, floating docks, buoys, lightweight upper walls.

### Group H: Specialty Materials

#### 7.31 Fibered Ice (Pykrete Analog)

- **Real precedent:** Project Habakkuk (1942-43) investigated aircraft carriers built from pykrete, ice frozen with about 14% wood pulp. It proved much stronger and tougher than plain ice and melted far slower because the fiber insulated the surface.
- **CR version:** water with a small fraction of binder and straw, frozen in place.
- **Uses:** winter roads, river crossings, temporary bridges and fortifications.
- **Tier 3 upgrade:** water-locked grade that no longer melts at all.

#### 7.32 Watercrete (Tier 3 Hydrate Stone)

- **Mix:** 60-90% water held by concentrated binder.
- **Properties (suggested canon):** ranges from hard rubber to soft stone depending on concentration.
- **Advantage:** lowest material cost of anything on this list; buildable anywhere water exists.
- **Weakness:** reverts toward liquid if the magic sustaining it is dispelled. Excellent for temporary works or siege engineering. Dangerous for anything permanent.

#### 7.33 Armor Plate

- **Form:** layered nacre-structure plates of mineral and 90:10 binder, backed by a 20:80 elastic layer.
- **Real precedent:** nacre's layered structure resists crack growth far beyond its mineral alone; insect cuticle combines hard outer layers with elastic inner layers.
- **Properties:** lighter than steel per unit of protection against blades and blunt impact (suggested canon).

#### 7.34 Self-Healing Crack Sealer

- **Form:** spray stock applied to old stone, concrete or binder structures.
- **Real precedent:** self-healing bacterial concrete; DOPA-iron bonds in mussel threads that reform after breaking.
- **Use:** extends the life of every other material on this list.

#### 7.35 Underwater Adhesive and Patching Compound

- **Mix:** heavy stock with iron or calcium salts for wet setting.
- **Real precedent:** mussel byssal glue holds in surf.
- **Uses:** hull repairs, pier repairs, dam leaks, bridge piers.

### Group I: Silk Materials

#### 7.36 Suspension Cables and Bridge Lines

- **Silk:** dragline (MaSp), drawn from dope and twisted into strands.
- **Replaces:** steel wire rope, iron chain, hemp cable.
- **Properties:** about a quarter the weight of high-tensile steel for the same breaking load; absorbs shock loads that snap steel; does not rust.
- **Real precedent:** Darwin's bark spider bridges rivers with single lines.
- **Self-tensioning:** controlled supercontraction lets cables tighten themselves in rain or be tightened by soaking during installation.
- **Limits:** stretches far more than steel (about 25-35% before failure versus under 1%), so bridges flex and bounce. Designs need stiffening trusses or pre-stretched cable. UV sheathing required.

#### 7.37 Rope, Rigging and Bowstrings

- **Silk:** dragline core with a minor ampullate cover (humidity-stable outer layer).
- **Replaces:** hemp, flax and sinew.
- **Properties:** light, rot-resistant when treated, very high shock absorption for climbing, mooring and towing lines.
- **Limits:** high stretch is wrong for bowstrings and standing rigging unless pre-stretched; tubuliform-rich blends suit those.

#### 7.38 Silk Fiber Reinforcement

- **Use:** chopped silk fibers mixed into CR concrete, mortar, road surfaces and plaster.
- **Real precedent:** modern concrete uses steel, glass or polypropylene fibers at roughly 0.1-2% by volume to control cracking. Horsehair and straw did the same in historical plaster and adobe.
- **Mix (suggested canon):** 0.2-1% silk fiber by volume.
- **Effect:** turns sudden brittle failure into gradual failure; cracks stay hairline instead of opening.
- **Limits:** dragline fibers must be pre-shrunk or swapped for minor ampullate fibers to avoid supercontraction cracking.

#### 7.39 Geogrids and Geotextiles

- **Silk:** aciniform and tubuliform mesh.
- **Replaces:** modern plastic geogrids and filter fabrics.
- **Use:** laid between road layers, behind retaining walls and under embankments. Grids lock aggregate in place and spread loads. Fabrics separate soil from stone and filter drains.
- **Real precedent:** geosynthetics are standard in modern roads. Brushwood and reed mats served similar purposes in older road building.
- **Effect:** allows thinner road bases over soft soil.

#### 7.40 Nets: Rockfall, Safety, Cargo and Fishing

- **Silk:** orb-web architecture. Stiff dragline frame with stretchy flagelliform infill.
- **Real precedent:** orb webs stop fast-flying insects many times the spider's weight because the capture spiral stretches while the frame holds. Modern rockfall barriers use the same principle with steel rings and braking elements.
- **Uses:** rockfall barriers on mountain roads, scaffold safety nets, cargo nets, fishing nets, arrester nets for runaway wagons.

#### 7.41 Tensile Roofs, Sails and Tents

- **Silk:** aciniform fabric, tubuliform-coated, hydrophobin-skinned.
- **Replaces:** canvas, leather tents, linen sails.
- **Real precedent:** modern tensile architecture (cable-net and membrane roofs) spans large areas with very little material.
- **Uses:** market halls, arena roofs, sails, awnings, field shelters.
- **Limits:** UV degradation; needs pigment or coating.

#### 7.42 Soft Armor and Ballistic Fabric

- **Silk:** layered aciniform and dragline fabric.
- **Basis:** toughness stops blades, arrows and projectiles. Dragline absorbs roughly three times the energy of Kevlar per unit volume, though its lower stiffness means it deflects more on impact.
- **Real precedent:** padded silk garments were used against arrows historically. Silk vests were made and tested against handgun bullets in the 1890s.
- **Uses:** gambesons, vests, shield facings, blast curtains.

#### 7.43 Rigid Silk Composite Panels and Hulls

- **Silk:** tubuliform-rich fabric laminated with CR binder or shellac.
- **Replaces:** fiberglass, plywood, planking.
- **Uses:** boat hulls, doors, crates, carriage bodies, water tanks.
- **Real precedent:** silk fiber composites are studied as tougher alternatives to glass fiber composites.

#### 7.44 Anchor Cement

- **Source:** piriform cement.
- **Use:** fixes cables, bolts and fittings to stone, timber and metal; rock bolts in tunnels and cliff roads.
- **Real precedent:** piriform attachment discs routinely hold better than the dragline attached to them.
- **Design note:** branching disc patterns spread load the same way modern adhesive anchors do.

#### 7.45 Tunable Sticky Coatings

- **Source:** aggregate glue.
- **Uses:** vermin and insect traps around curing works (a direct counter to Infrastructure 9.1), temporary hold for masonry, labels, sealing tapes.
- **Tuning:** set for local humidity.

#### 7.46 Dry Grip Fabrics

- **Source:** cribellate nanofiber wool.
- **Uses:** climbing gloves, boot soles, hook-free fasteners, dust filters.
- **Limits:** clogs with dirt; works best dry.

#### 7.47 Underwater Air Bells and Caissons

- **Source:** diving bell silk.
- **Use:** silk domes anchored to riverbeds or harbor floors hold air for underwater workers building piers, bridge foundations and dams.
- **Real precedent:** the water spider lives in such a bell for long periods. Pressurized caissons built the foundations of the Brooklyn Bridge.
- **Magic:** gas exchange through the membrane keeps the air breathable, extending the real physical-gill effect.

#### 7.48 Silk Optics

- **Source:** silkworm-type fibroin.
- **Forms:** lenses, light-guiding fibers, transparent films, diffraction gratings.
- **Real precedent:** silk fibroin optics developed at Tufts (Omenetto and Kaplan) include lenses and light-guiding fibers made at room temperature from water solutions.
- **Uses:** lamps, light pipes carrying daylight into deep buildings and mines, spectacles.

#### 7.49 Silk Aerogel and Sponge Insulation

- **Process:** freeze dilute fibroin, then remove the ice; a porous sponge remains.
- **Real precedent:** silk fibroin sponges and aerogels are made this way in labs.
- **Uses:** wall and roof insulation, sound damping, packing.

#### 7.50 Silk Paper and Cloth

- **Source:** fibroin with sericin left on as natural sizing.
- **Real precedent:** sericin is the gum that binds raw silk cocoons together. Silk paper exists in traditional East Asian crafts.
- **Uses:** durable paper, maps, banners, lightweight wall coverings.

### Group J: Marine and Other Animal Biopolymer Materials

#### 7.51 Underwater Sand Masonry

- **Source:** sandcastle worm style cement.
- **Process:** cement released into wet sand or sprayed onto stones underwater condenses into dense droplets that do not wash out, then sets in under a minute.
- **Uses:** breakwaters, pier footings, harbor walls, river crossings, reef-building for coastal defense.
- **Advantage:** no cofferdam or dewatering needed.

#### 7.52 River Stone Bonding

- **Source:** caddisfly-style silk.
- **Uses:** binding loose riverbed stone into scour aprons, fords, weirs and fish ladders.
- **Real precedent:** caddisfly cases hold together in fast streams.

#### 7.53 Instant Gel Barriers

- **Source:** hagfish-style thread secretion.
- **Ratio:** 1 kg secretion to about 25,000 L water.
- **Uses:** sealing dam leaks and breached levees, plugging flooded tunnels, firefighting gel that clings to walls, temporary dust and erosion control, sealing hull breaches.
- **Limits:** soft unless stretched and drawn; temporary unless converted to Tier 3 hydrate stone.

#### 7.54 Recyclable Fiber

- **Source:** velvet worm style fiber.
- **Property:** dissolves back into dope with water treatment, then redraws.
- **Uses:** formwork ties, scaffolding lashings, rope stock that is re-spun when worn.

#### 7.55 Thermoplastic Protein Parts

- **Source:** suckerin-like secretion.
- **Process:** molded warm and damp; hardens on cooling and drying; can be remelted.
- **Uses:** pipe fittings, hinges, handles, gears, buttons, repairable hardware.

#### 7.56 Tunable Mirrors and Privacy Glass

- **Source:** reflectin-like protein layered into windows.
- **Function:** reflectivity shifts with a trigger (moisture, salt, mana).
- **Uses:** heat-rejecting windows in summer, privacy glass, signal mirrors, lighthouse reflectors.

#### 7.57 Gradient Joints

- **Source:** byssus and squid beak gradient structure.
- **Function:** smooth stiffness change from rigid stone or metal to flexible cable, fabric or rubber.
- **Uses:** cable terminations, tent anchors, tool handles, joints between rigid road slabs and flexible bridge decks.
- **Basis:** sudden stiffness changes concentrate stress and cause failure at joints. Gradients remove the weak point.

#### 7.58 Keratin Composites

- **Forms:** horn plates, hoof-wall impact panels, scale armor, wear tiles.
- **Real precedent:** horse hoof walls absorb repeated heavy impacts. Pangolin scales resist bites. Horn served for lantern panes and bow limbs.
- **Uses:** floor tiles in high-traffic halls, stair treads, door kickplates, armor scales.

#### 7.59 Long-Life Elastic Bands

- **Source:** elastin and abductin analogs.
- **Advantage:** elastin's long lifespan suits permanent elastic parts such as door springs, window counterweights and earthquake dampers.

#### 7.60 Cascade-Set Repair Kits

- **Source:** fibrin-style two-part secretion.
- **Use:** a small packet of trigger stirred into a pail of working stock sets the whole batch within minutes.
- **Uses:** pothole repair, emergency wall patching, battlefield fortification.

### Group K: Partner Materials

#### 7.61 Antimicrobial Coating

- **Source:** chitosan.
- **Use:** blended into or sprayed onto all protein materials.
- **Effect:** directly counters rot and microbial attack (Infrastructure 9.1).

#### 7.62 Shrilk Panels

- **Source:** chitosan laminated with silk fibroin.
- **Real precedent:** reported comparable to aluminum alloy at about half the weight.
- **Uses:** lightweight structural panels, carriage bodies, furniture, containers.

#### 7.63 Varnish, Sealing Wax and Insulation

- **Source:** shellac-like resin.
- **Uses:** wood finish, waterproof seal over protein surfaces, electrical insulation where the setting has electricity, small molded parts.

#### 7.64 Wax Waterproofing

- **Source:** beeswax-like secretion.
- **Uses:** rope and fabric waterproofing, mold release, surface skin for CR roofs.

#### 7.65 Water-Repellent Skin

- **Source:** hydrophobin or BslA analog.
- **Use:** added in trace amounts to any mix; it migrates to the surface and assembles into a water-repellent film.
- **Effect:** rain beads and rolls off; protects silk components from supercontraction and rot.

#### 7.66 Grown Blocks

- **Source:** fungal mycelium fed on straw or sawdust, stabilized with binder.
- **Real precedent:** Hy-Fi tower (New York, 2014); commercial mycelium packaging and insulation.
- **Uses:** insulation, interior walls, temporary structures.
- **Note:** heat or drying kills the fungus once the block is grown, stopping further growth.

#### 7.67 Cellulose Membranes and Filters

- **Source:** bacterial cellulose mats grown in vats.
- **Uses:** water filters, paper, drumskins, membranes.

#### 7.68 Wasp Paper Panels

- **Process:** wood fiber pulped with dilute binder, layered and dried.
- **Real precedent:** paper wasps make nest paper this way. René de Réaumur proposed wood-pulp papermaking in 1719 after studying wasp nests.
- **Uses:** roof underlayment, partition panels, lanterns.

#### 7.69 Termite-Style Ventilated Earth

- **Process:** earth walls built with internal channels and chimneys.
- **Real precedent:** termite mounds move air for cooling and gas exchange. The Eastgate Centre in Harare used a similar passive cooling design.
- **Uses:** granaries, cellars, homes in hot climates.

#### 7.70 Rapid-Freeze Winter Works

- **Source:** ice-nucleating protein analog.
- **Use:** sprayed water freezes quickly just below 0°C, building fibered ice roads, crossings and walls fast in winter.
- **Real precedent:** snowmaking with *Pseudomonas syringae* proteins.
