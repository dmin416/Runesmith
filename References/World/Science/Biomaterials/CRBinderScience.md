## 2. The Real Science of the Two Proteins

### 2.1 Collagen

- **Abundance.** Collagen makes up roughly a quarter to a third of all protein in mammals.
- **Structure.** Three protein chains twist into a triple helix. Each chain repeats the pattern Gly-X-Y. X is often proline. Y is often hydroxyproline. Glycine sits at every third position because it is the only amino acid small enough to fit in the crowded core of the helix.
- **Stability.** Hydroxyproline locks the helix into shape through stereoelectronic effects and bridging water molecules. Producing it requires vitamin C. Scurvy is collagen failure.
- **Hierarchy.** Single molecules (about 300 nm long, 1.5 nm wide) pack into staggered fibrils with a repeating 67 nm band pattern (the D-period). Fibrils bundle into fibers, then into tendon, skin and bone. The gaps in the staggered packing are where bone mineral first nucleates. Collagen is literally a mineral scaffold.
- **Crosslinking.** The enzyme lysyl oxidase converts lysine side chains into reactive aldehydes that bond neighboring molecules together. More crosslinks produce a stiffer and less soluble material. Sugar-driven crosslinks (glycation) accumulate with age, which is part of why old tissue stiffens.
- **Heat.** Loose mammalian collagen in solution unravels at roughly 37-41°C. Unraveled collagen is gelatin, which melts when warm and sets when cool. Dense fibrillar collagen survives higher heat. Tanned leather survives much higher still (chrome-tanned leather resists shrinking at around boiling temperature).
- **Mechanics.** Tendon reaches roughly 50-150 MPa tensile strength with a stiffness of about 1-2 GPa. Per unit weight, tendon rivals structural steel in tension.
- **Existing construction uses (real).** Hide glue, bone glue, gesso, paper sizing, parchment, leather, gelatin, sausage casings.

### 2.2 Resilin

- **Where it occurs.** Insect joints and energy-storing structures: locust and dragonfly wing hinges, flea jump pads, cicada sound organs.
- **Structure.** Flexible random-coil chains, heavily hydrated, tied together by dityrosine and trityrosine crosslinks (two or three tyrosine rings fused together). Peroxidase enzymes form these links.
- **Resilience.** Returns roughly 92-97% of the energy put into it. Most rubbers return noticeably less.
- **Extension.** Stretches to roughly three times its resting length before breaking.
- **Fatigue life.** Insect wing hinges flex hundreds of millions of times over a lifetime without fatigue failure.
- **Hydration switch.** Wet resilin is soft rubber. Dry resilin is glassy and hard. Water is the dial.
- **Fluorescence.** Dityrosine glows blue under ultraviolet light. Resilin-rich structures shine blue under a blacklight.
- **Lab precedent.** Recombinant resilin (Elvin et al., 2005) was produced in bacteria and set into a solid rubber in seconds using a ruthenium catalyst, persulfate and visible light.

### 2.3 Why the Blend Works

Every strong natural material is a composite of a stiff phase plus a tough phase.

- Bone: brittle mineral plus tough collagen.
- Insect cuticle: stiff chitin fibers plus proteins that include resilin.
- Reinforced concrete: brittle stone plus ductile steel.

In CR binder the collagen fraction forms the load-bearing scaffold and mineral template. The resilin fraction forms an elastic net that bridges cracks, absorbs impact and resists fatigue.

**Sacrificial bonds.** Bone gains much of its toughness from weak internal bonds that break under load, unfold hidden molecular length to absorb energy, then reform afterward (Fantner et al., 2005). CR materials inherit the same behavior. Small cracks dissipate energy instead of spreading, then partially close.

### 2.4 Tuning the Ratio (suggested canon)

| Collagen : Resilin | Behavior | Best uses |
|---|---|---|
| 90 : 10 | Stiff, ceramic-like once mineralized | Windows, tiles, structural blocks |
| 70 : 30 | Tough with slight flex | Road surfaces, pipes, foundations |
| 50 : 50 | Leathery and tough | Membranes, roofing, ditch liners |
| 20 : 80 | Rubbery | Seals, tires, mounts, expansion joints |

Ways the ratio can be controlled in-setting: feeding the bug different diets, different castes or life stages of bug producing different blends, or separating the secretion afterward (resilin and collagen differ in solubility and density so settling or salting-out could split them).

### 2.5 Expanding the Secretion Family (suggested canon options)

Real orb-weaving spiders carry up to seven different silk glands, each producing a different protein for a different job. A bug with the same arrangement is biologically consistent. Setting options:

1. **Multi-gland bug.** One bug with several spinnerets or glands, each yielding a different secretion. Output per gland is lower than a single-purpose gland.
2. **Castes or breeds.** Different bugs in a colony produce different secretions, like ant castes.
3. **Diet switching.** The same gland shifts output with feed: protein-rich feed yields silk, mineral-rich feed yields binder.
4. **Re-spinning.** CR binder itself can be drawn into fiber under shear (collagen fiber is extruded industrially), with silk-like behavior added by magic.
5. **Partner organisms.** Fungi, bacteria and plants supply non-protein partners such as chitosan, cellulose and hydrophobins.

### 2.6 Spider Silks

**Shared structure (real).** Spider silk proteins (spidroins) are huge, often 250-350 kDa, with long repeating cores flanked by terminal domains that control assembly. The repeats combine two kinds of segment:

- Poly-alanine and glycine-alanine runs that stack into tiny beta-sheet crystals. These act like rivets holding the fiber together. They give strength.
- Glycine-rich runs (GGX and GPGXX motifs) that stay loosely coiled. These give stretch.

The ratio of crystal to coil sets each silk's character. More crystal means stronger and stiffer. More coil means stretchier.

**Spinning from water (real).** Spiders store silk as a liquid crystalline "dope" at roughly 30-50% protein in water. As it flows down the spinning duct, sodium is swapped for potassium, the pH drops, water is pulled out and the flow is stretched. The proteins lock into solid fiber at room temperature with no toxic solvents and no heat. Industrial nylon and Kevlar need high temperatures or concentrated acid.

**The seven orb-weaver glands (real):**

| Gland | Silk | Job in the web | Tensile strength (approx.) | Stretch before break | Key trait |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Major ampullate (MaSp1, MaSp2) | Dragline | Frame, radii, safety line | About 1.1 GPa (up to about 1.6 GPa in Darwin's bark spider) | About 25-35% | Strong and tough; supercontracts when wet |
| Minor ampullate (MiSp) | Scaffold | Temporary spiral during construction | Somewhat below dragline | About 25-35% | Does not supercontract; stable in humidity |
| Flagelliform (Flag) | Capture spiral core | Catches flying prey | About 0.5 GPa | About 200-300% | Extreme stretch; elastin-like repeats |
| Aggregate | Glue droplets | Coats the capture spiral | Not a fiber | Viscous | Stickiness tuned to humidity |
| Aciniform (AcSp) | Wrapping silk | Wraps prey, lines egg cases | About 0.7 GPa | About 50-80% | Among the toughest silks known |
| Tubuliform (TuSp, also called cylindrical) | Egg case silk | Outer egg case | About 0.4-0.5 GPa | About 20-40% | Stiff; resists weather and decay |
| Piriform (PySp) | Attachment cement | Anchors lines to surfaces | Not a fiber | Sets as branching discs | Anchor often holds better than the line it anchors |

**Other spider silks (real):**

- **Cribellate silk.** Older spider lineages (such as the Uloboridae) comb out a wool of nanofibrils tens of nanometers thick. It catches prey with no glue at all, using van der Waals attraction and capillary forces.
- **Diving bell silk.** The water spider (*Argyroneta aquatica*) lives underwater in a silk dome filled with air. The bell works as a physical gill, drawing dissolved oxygen in from the surrounding water.
- **Darwin's bark spider bridge lines.** *Caerostris darwini* strings single lines across rivers, reportedly up to about 25 m. Its dragline is the toughest biological material measured, around 350 MJ/m³ on average.

**Comparison with other fibers (real, approx., after Gosline et al., 1999 and later work):**

| Material | Strength | Stretch | Toughness (energy to break) | Density (g/cm³) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dragline silk | 1.1 GPa | about 27% | about 160 MJ/m³ | 1.3 |
| Darwin's bark spider dragline | 1.6 GPa | about 35-50% | about 350 MJ/m³ | 1.3 |
| Silkworm silk | 0.6 GPa | about 18% | about 70 MJ/m³ | 1.3 |
| Nylon | 0.95 GPa | about 18% | about 80 MJ/m³ | 1.1 |
| Kevlar | 3.6 GPa | about 2.7% | about 50 MJ/m³ | 1.4 |
| High-tensile steel | 1.5 GPa | about 0.8% | about 6 MJ/m³ | 7.8 |
| Rubber | 0.001 GPa | about 600% | about 100 MJ/m³ | 1.0 |

Toughness is the headline. Dragline absorbs roughly three times the energy of Kevlar before breaking and roughly 25 times that of steel by volume. By weight dragline carries about four times the load of high-tensile steel.

**Breaking length (real calculation).** The length of hanging fiber that would snap under its own weight: dragline about 85 km; high-tensile steel about 20 km.

**Supercontraction (real).** Wet dragline shrinks up to about half its length if free to move. If held at fixed length it pulls hard instead. Spiders likely use this to re-tighten webs sagging under dew. Minor ampullate silk does not do this.

**Temperature (real).** Spider silk stays tough far below freezing and does not begin to decompose until roughly 230°C or more.

**Production barrier (real).** Spiders cannot be farmed like silkworms because they are territorial and cannibalistic. A golden orb-weaver cape displayed at the Victoria and Albert Museum in 2012 required silk from about 1.2 million wild-caught spiders. Recombinant silk is now made in bacteria, yeast, silkworms and goats by several companies. A magical bug that secretes spider silk in bulk solves the problem nature never did.

**Recycling (real).** Many orb weavers eat their old web every day and reuse most of the protein in the next one. Suggested canon: worn CR silk can be fed back to the bug as raw material.

| Silk | Producer | Notable trait | Construction use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fibroin plus sericin | Silkworm (*Bombyx mori*) | Fibroin core is strong and forms transparent films; sericin is a water-soluble gum coat | Textiles, films, optics, sizing |

| Larval sewing silk | Weaver ants | Workers hold larvae and use their silk to sew leaves into nests | Joining sheet materials |
| Coiled-coil silk | Honeybees, hornets | Different structure from spider silk; reinforces wax comb cells | Reinforcing soft fillers |

| Foot silk | Webspinners (Embioptera) | Spun from glands in the front feet into gallery tunnels | Tunnel linings |
| Fishing lines | New Zealand glowworms | Sticky beaded threads hanging from cave ceilings | Sticky curtains |

### 2.8 Marine and Other Animal Biopolymers (real)

- **Mussel byssus.** Collagen core with silk-like and elastin-like ends, coated in a DOPA-iron cuticle. Stiffness grades smoothly from the soft end to the stiff end, which keeps the thread from tearing at the joint.
- **Sandcastle worm cement.** *Phragmatopoma* worms glue sand grains into tube reefs underwater. The glue is a complex coacervate: oppositely charged proteins (phosphate-rich and amine-rich) condense into a dense liquid that does not mix with seawater, then set within roughly 30 seconds as pH shifts. Russell Stewart's lab has synthesized imitations.
- **Barnacle cement.** Amyloid-like protein fibers that cure underwater into one of the strongest natural adhesives.
- **Hagfish slime.** Thread cells and mucus cells release into seawater. Within a fraction of a second a few grams of exudate gels liters of water. The finished slime is roughly 99.996% water. The threads are intermediate filaments up to about 15 cm long. When stretched, their coiled (alpha-helix) structure converts to beta-sheet and they stiffen like silk. Draw-processed threads have reached strength comparable to nylon.
- **Velvet worm slime.** Protein droplets form fibers under shear. Those fibers can be dissolved in water and redrawn into new fibers (Baer et al., 2017). A fully recyclable fiber.
- **Squid sucker ring teeth (suckerin).** A protein that behaves like a thermoplastic. It softens with heat and water, can be molded and hardens again.
- **Reflectin.** Squid and cuttlefish proteins that change reflected color and brightness as the protein clusters or spreads. Tunable mirrors.
- **Squid beak.** Stiffness changes roughly a hundredfold from the soft base to the hard tip through gradients of chitin, protein, water and catechol crosslinks (Miserez et al., 2008).
- **Sea silk.** Byssus of the noble pen shell (*Pinna nobilis*), woven into golden cloth since antiquity.
- **Keratin.** Hair, horn, hoof, claw, feather, baleen and pangolin scale. Disulfide bonds between cysteines lock it together. Horse hoof wall is among the most impact-resistant biological materials. Horn was the original lantern pane.
- **Elastin.** Vertebrate elastic protein in arteries, lungs and skin. Elastin in the body can persist for decades.
- **Abductin.** Rubber-like protein in scallop hinge ligaments that springs the shell open.
- **Fibrin.** Blood clotting protein. Soluble fibrinogen polymerizes on demand when thrombin cuts it, then Factor XIII (a transglutaminase) crosslinks the clot.
- **Conchiolin and amelogenin.** Organic matrices that template nacre and tooth enamel.

### 2.9 Non-Protein Partners (real)

- **Chitin and chitosan.** Chitin is the second most abundant natural polymer on Earth after cellulose, found in insect cuticle, crab shells and fungal cell walls. Chitosan, its deacetylated form, is naturally antimicrobial.
- **Shrilk.** Chitosan laminated with silk fibroin (Fernandez and Ingber, 2012) was reported comparable in strength to aluminum alloy at about half the weight.
- **Shellac.** Resin secreted by the lac insect (*Kerria lacca*). Used for varnish, sealing wax, early gramophone records and electrical insulation. An insect secretion already used as a manufacturing material.
- **Beeswax.** Waterproofing, lost-wax casting, thread coating.
- **Cellulose and bacterial cellulose.** Plant cellulose is the most abundant polymer on Earth. Bacteria (the mat in kombucha) spin pure cellulose nanofibers into tough sheets.
- **Lignin and suberin.** Lignin stiffens wood. Suberin waterproofs cork and bark.
- **Mycelium composites.** Fungal threads grown through farm waste bind it into blocks. A tower of grown mycelium bricks (Hy-Fi, 2014) stood in New York. Commercial packaging and insulation exist.
- **Hydrophobins.** Fungal proteins that self-assemble into an extremely water-repellent film wherever water meets air. They coat fungal spores and aerial threads.
- **BslA.** A bacterial protein that gives *Bacillus subtilis* biofilms a water-repellent "raincoat."
- **Ice-nucleating proteins.** *Pseudomonas syringae* bacteria carry proteins that make water freeze at around -2°C instead of supercooling. A freeze-dried form is sold to ski resorts for snowmaking.
