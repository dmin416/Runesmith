# CR Binder: Weaknesses, Roads, Drainage and Scaling

Design loot. Pack hub: `CRBinder.md`. Materials: `CRBinderMaterials.md`. Science: `CRBinderScience.md`.

## 9. Weaknesses, Failure Modes and Counters

### 9.1 Biological Attack

Protein is food. Collagen-digesting bacteria, fungi, rats, dermestid beetles and dogs will all attack untreated material. Leather rots and gets chewed for the same reason.

**Counters (all real):** tannin treatment (vegetable tanning), mineral encapsulation, high crosslink density (digestive enzymes cannot cut heavily crosslinked collagen), copper salts and borax as preservatives.

**Story hooks:** fresh roads attract vermin while curing; poorly made binder works rot within a few years; a "rot blight" fungus that eats CR materials could become a regional disaster.

### 9.2 Sunlight

UV light oxidizes tyrosine and tryptophan, which yellows proteins and eventually breaks chains. Early UV exposure helps by adding dityrosine crosslinks. Excess exposure over years causes embrittlement.

**Counters:** iron oxide pigment, soot, mineral-rich surface layers, waxy skin. These are the same reasons real tires are black.

### 9.3 Hydrolysis

Water slowly breaks protein bonds. The reaction is very slow at neutral pH and cool temperatures. Bone collagen survives tens of thousands of years in cold dry conditions. Acid, strong alkali and heat speed it up.

**Counters:** mineralization, crosslinking, keeping pH near neutral.

### 9.4 Freezing

Water in a soaked material expands when it freezes and pries the structure apart. This is the main destroyer of real concrete in cold climates.

**Counters:** low free water in the design; antifreeze proteins (real: polar fish and insects produce proteins that bind ice crystals and stop them growing) added as a domain of the binder.

### 9.5 Fire

Proteins char rather than melt. Wool (also a protein) is naturally flame-resistant with an ignition temperature around 570-600°C and tends to self-extinguish. Burning CR material smells like burning hair.

- Mineral-dominant grades (concrete, brick, tile): essentially fireproof.
- Fiber beams: burn like timber.
- Seals, tires and membranes: burn.

### 9.6 Creep

Proteins are viscoelastic. Under constant heavy load they deform slowly over years. Long beams sag; tall walls of low-mineral grades bulge.

**Counter:** mineral-dominant grades for load-bearing work.

### 9.7 Odor and Pests During Cure

Hide glue pots smell strongly of animal. Large-scale curing will make worksites smell like a tannery for days and attract flies.

### 9.8 Magic Dependence

Tier 3 materials depend on sustained magic. Amplified binder carries contamination risk. Anti-magic effects, dispelling or mana-dead zones could interfere with uncured work in every tier.

### 9.9 Supply

A single bug is a single point of failure. Whoever controls the bug controls construction. Theft, sabotage, disease and overwork of the bug all matter.

### 9.10 Silk-Specific Weaknesses

- **UV:** silk loses strength in strong sunlight within months unless pigmented, coated or sheathed. Real spiders rebuild webs constantly partly for this reason.
- **Supercontraction:** uncontrolled dragline shrinkage can crack rigid hosts or over-tension cables in storms. Counter with minor ampullate silk, pre-shrinking or hydrophobin skins.
- **Stretch:** silk is far less stiff than steel, so silk-cabled structures deflect more. Counter with trusses, pre-tension or tubuliform-rich blends.
- **Abrasion:** fine fibers fray at contact points. Counter with keratin wear sleeves or thicker strands.
- **Pests:** silk is protein and attracts the same moths, beetles and microbes as wool. Counter with chitosan.
- **Output:** fiber goods need far more secretion per job than binder goods (Section 12.4). Silk stays a premium product unless amplification also applies to the silk glands.

## 10. Roads (Side Note)

### 10.1 Core Principle

Water is the enemy of roads. Most road failures trace back to water in the base layers. An unbound gravel base can lose a large fraction of its load-bearing strength when saturated. A road is a layered structure that spreads concentrated wheel loads over a wider area of the soil beneath. Each layer reduces the pressure on the layer below.

### 10.2 Historical Systems (real)

| System | Era | Construction |
|---|---|---|
| Roman via | Antiquity | Large foundation stones, then rubble with lime, then fine gravel with lime, then fitted paving stones. Major roads were often around 1 m deep or more, raised on an embankment (agger), crowned and flanked by ditches |
| Trésaguet | France, 1770s | Thinner (about 25 cm) with a shaped foundation and crowned surface |
| Telford | Britain, early 1800s | Hand-placed large foundation stones on edge, smaller stones above, crowned; strong but labor-intensive |
| McAdam | Britain, 1810s-1820s | Layers of small angular broken stone (no piece over about 6 ounces) totaling about 25 cm, compacted by traffic. Angular stones interlock. Raised above the water table. No binder needed if the subgrade stays dry |
| Tarmac | 1902 onward | Macadam bound with tar, later bitumen |
| Modern flexible pavement | 20th century | Compacted subgrade, subbase, base, binder course, surface course |
| Modern rigid pavement | 20th century | Concrete slab on a granular base with jointing |

### 10.3 Road Geometry

- **Cross slope (crown):** paved surfaces about 1.5-2.5%; gravel surfaces about 4-6% because rough surfaces shed water slower.
- **Shoulders:** slope more steeply than the travel lane.
- **Longitudinal grade:** keep at least about 0.5% on curbed roads so water flows along the gutter. Animal-drawn traffic works best under about 5%; short pitches to 8-10% are tolerable.
- **Superelevation:** tilt the road inward on curves.
- **Elevation:** raise the road above surrounding ground in wet terrain, as the Romans did. 0.5-1 m of fill keeps the base above standing water.

### 10.4 Construction Sequence

1. **Route.** Follow contours. Avoid wet lowland. Ridge routes drain naturally.
2. **Clear and strip.** Remove topsoil and organic material, which compresses and rots.
3. **Drain first.** Cut ditches before building the road so the site dries out during construction.
4. **Cut, fill and compact the subgrade.** Compact in thin layers (about 15-20 cm each) at the soil's optimum moisture. Every soil compacts densest at one specific water content (the Proctor test measures it).
5. **Subbase.** Coarse, free-draining stone.
6. **Base.** Well-graded crushed stone. The ideal particle distribution follows the Fuller curve: percent passing a sieve = (sieve size ÷ largest stone size)^0.5. This packs small stones into the gaps between large ones for maximum density.
7. **Surface.**
8. **Shoulders and erosion protection.**

### 10.5 CR Binder Road Specification (suggested canon)

| Layer | Thickness | Material | Binder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subgrade | Native | Compacted soil, top 15 cm sprayed | 0.2-0.5% spray stock |
| Subbase | 15-30 cm | Coarse gravel or rubble | **None.** Leave it unbound so water drains to the side ditches |
| Base | 15-20 cm | Crushed stone, Fuller-graded | 0.5-1% |
| Surface | 5-10 cm | Fine graded aggregate | 2-4% at 70:30, sun-cured |
| Seal | 2-5 mm | Spray coat, grit-dusted for traction | Heavy stock at 50:50 |
| Joints | Every 10-20 m on rigid grades only | 20:80 strips | Neat |

### 10.6 Variants

- **Cheap rural road:** compacted local soil with 1% binder, crowned at 4%, with ditches. Quick and nearly free.
- **Permeable town street:** open-graded aggregate with no fines, bound only at stone contacts. Rain passes straight through into a stone reservoir below. Reduces surface ditches and flooding.
- **Paver street:** cast binder blocks laid on a sand bed. Easy to lift and replace for repairs or buried pipes.
- **Bog crossing:** binder-sealed fiber mats over a brush layer replace plank and log (corduroy) roads.

### 10.7 Silk and Partner Materials in Roads

- **Geogrid in the base:** an aciniform grid on soft subgrade reduces required base thickness. Modern geogrids allow noticeably thinner aggregate layers over weak soils.
- **Separator fabric:** silk fabric between subgrade and subbase stops clay pumping up into the stone, the main reason gravel roads turn to mud.
- **Fiber-reinforced surface:** 0.2-0.5% minor ampullate fiber in the wearing course controls cracking.
- **Hydrophobin seal:** a trace in the seal coat makes the surface shed water.
- **Bridges:** silk suspension cables with piriform anchor cement allow light footbridges and pack bridges over gorges with minimal material hauled in.
- **Rockfall nets:** orb-web style nets above mountain roads.
- **Pothole kits:** cascade-set patch mix in buckets.
- **Pest control:** aggregate-glue strips along curing roads trap vermin.

## 11. Drainage (Side Note)

### 11.1 Principles

1. Get water off the surface fast.
2. Get water out of the base faster.
3. Carry water away from the road.
4. Release it without causing erosion.

### 11.2 Surface Drainage Elements

- **Crown and cross slope:** sheds rain to the edges.
- **Side ditches:** V-shaped or trapezoidal. The ditch bottom must sit below the bottom of the subbase so the base drains into it. Side slopes of about 2:1 to 4:1 (horizontal to vertical) stay stable and are safer for vehicles.
- **Ditch lining by water speed (real rules of thumb):** grass handles roughly 1-1.5 m/s. Faster flow needs stone lining or a hard liner. CR-lined ditches handle much higher speeds.
- **Check dams:** small barriers across steep ditches that slow water and trap sediment.
- **Turnouts (lead-off ditches):** divert ditch water away from the road every 50-150 m depending on slope so volume never builds up enough to erode.
- **Cross drains:** culverts under the road at low points and at intervals. Unpaved low-traffic roads use water bars (angled berms) and broad rolling dips instead.

### 11.3 Culverts

- **Minimum diameter:** about 45-60 cm so they can be cleaned.
- **Slope:** match the stream grade, ideally at least 1-2% so the pipe flushes itself.
- **Cover:** at least about 30 cm of fill, or half the pipe diameter, whichever is greater.
- **Inlet:** headwall and wingwalls to guide flow and prevent erosion around the pipe.
- **Outlet:** stone or cast apron to absorb the water's energy. Unprotected outlets scour holes that eventually undermine the culvert.
- **Alignment:** follow the natural stream line even if the culvert crosses the road at an angle.

### 11.4 Subsurface Drainage

- **French drain:** a gravel-filled trench, optionally with a perforated pipe, that collects groundwater and carries it away.
- **Edge drains:** run along the road edge beneath the shoulder to pull water out of the base.
- **Interceptor drains:** dug across a slope uphill of the road to catch groundwater before it reaches the roadbed.
- **Filter rule (Terzaghi, real):** the stone around a drain must be fine enough to keep soil from washing in yet coarse enough to let water through. The particle size at which 15% of the filter is finer should be under about 4-5 times the size at which 85% of the soil is finer. It should also be over about 4-5 times the size at which 15% of the soil is finer. Graded stone layers (fine next to soil, coarse next to the pipe) satisfy both rules.

### 11.5 Urban Drainage

- Gutters and curbs along streets.
- Catch basins (street inlets) with sediment sumps.
- Storm sewers sloped at least about 0.5%. Flowing water must reach roughly 0.6-0.9 m/s to keep pipes self-cleaning.
- Swales and infiltration basins that let water soak into the ground instead of flooding downstream.

### 11.6 Sizing Example (real method)

**Rational method:** Q = C × i × A ÷ 360

- Q = peak flow in m³/s
- C = runoff coefficient (pavement 0.7-0.95, gravel 0.4-0.6, grass 0.1-0.35, forest 0.05-0.25)
- i = rainfall intensity in mm/hour
- A = catchment area in hectares

Example: a 2 hectare mixed catchment (C = 0.5) in a 50 mm/hour storm:
Q = 0.5 × 50 × 2 ÷ 360 = **0.139 m³/s**

**Manning's equation for pipe capacity:** Q = (1 ÷ n) × A × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2)

- n = roughness (smooth cast pipe about 0.012-0.013; corrugated metal about 0.024)
- A = flow area; R = hydraulic radius (diameter ÷ 4 for a full round pipe); S = slope

| Pipe | Slope | Full-flow capacity (n = 0.013) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 300 mm | 2% | about 0.137 m³/s | Too small, no margin |
| 450 mm | 2% | about 0.40 m³/s | Correct; also meets the cleaning minimum |

### 11.7 CR Binder Drainage Builds

- **Monolithic ditch liners:** thin 3-5 cm shells at 50:50 that flex with frost heave and soil movement instead of cracking like concrete. They toughen in rain, which is exactly when they carry water.
- **Seamless culverts:** cast in place over an inflatable bladder or woven reed core. No joints to leak or separate.
- **Perforated drain pipe:** cast with pull-out pegs to form holes.
- **Filter fabric substitute:** binder-bonded plant fiber mats placed between soil and drain stone.
- **Bound permeable gravel:** stone bonded only at contact points for drains that never clog with shifting stone.
- **Outlet aprons:** cast in place or used to lock loose riprap together.
- **Check dams:** cast in minutes from local stone and binder.

### 11.8 Silk and Partner Materials in Drainage

- **Filter fabric:** aciniform silk fabric wrapped around drain stone replaces graded stone filters and keeps fines out.
- **Drain socks:** knitted silk tubes over perforated pipe.
- **Underwater culvert repair:** sandcastle-type cement patches flooded culverts without draining them.
- **Scour protection:** caddisfly-style binding locks outlet stone in place.
- **Emergency flood response:** thread gel plugs breaches and seals sandbag walls.
- **Chitosan-treated liners:** resist algae and root attack in ditches and canals.
- **Silk erosion mats:** open-weave silk mesh pinned over seeded slopes until grass roots.

## 12. Scaling Math

### 12.1 Binder Required per Kilometer of Road

Road 1 km long, 5 m wide.

| Element | Calculation | Mass of mix | Binder (Tier 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilized base | 1,000 m × 5 m × 0.2 m = 1,000 m³ at about 2.0 t/m³ | about 2,000 t | 1% = about 20 t |
| Surface course | 1,000 m × 5 m × 0.075 m = 375 m³ at about 2.3 t/m³ | about 860 t | 3% = about 26 t |
| Ditch liners (both sides) | 2 × 1,000 m × 1.5 m perimeter × 0.04 m = 120 m³ | about 250 t | 3% = about 7.5 t |
| Culverts (8 per km) | 8 culverts × 8 m × 450 mm pipe with 5 cm wall = about 5 m³ | about 11 t | 4% = about 0.45 t |
| **Total** | | | **about 54 t per km** |

For comparison, real cement stabilization of the same base at 5% would consume about 100 t of kiln-fired cement for the base alone.

### 12.2 Amplification Factor and Bug Output

Kilometers of road per year (suggested canon framework):

| Bug output | ×1 (no amplification) | ×10 | ×100 | ×1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kg/day | about 7 m | about 68 m | about 0.7 km | about 6.8 km |
| 10 kg/day | about 68 m | about 0.7 km | about 6.8 km | about 68 km |
| 100 kg/day | about 0.7 km | about 6.8 km | about 68 km | about 680 km |

**Implication:** without strong amplification, one bug cannot pave a kingdom. It can supply high-value goods (windows, seals, glue, armor, culverts). With ×100 or more, the bug reshapes national infrastructure. The amplification factor is the master balance dial for the setting.

### 12.3 Value per Gram

Highest leverage (small binder mass, high value replaced):

1. Adhesives, sealants, paint and anchor cement
2. Instant gel barriers (thread secretion)
3. Seals, gaskets, springs
4. Silk cables, rope and nets
5. Windows, protein glass and silk optics
6. Underwater cements
7. Culverts, pipes and liners
8. Mortar
9. Fiber reinforcement
10. Bricks and concrete
11. Road surfaces
12. Bulk soil stabilization

An economically rational owner of a low-output bug sells glue and windows. An owner of a high-output or high-amplification bug builds roads and harbors.

### 12.4 Silk Yield Math

1 kg of dragline silk at a density of 1.3 g/cm³ is about 770 cm³ of fiber.

| Cable cross-section | Breaking load at 1.1 GPa | Length from 1 kg | Working load (safety factor 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 cm² (10 mm²) | about 11 kN (about 1.1 t) | about 77 m | about 0.37 t |
| 1 cm² (100 mm²) | about 110 kN (about 11 t) | about 7.7 m | about 3.7 t |
| 5 cm² (500 mm²) | about 550 kN (about 56 t) | about 1.5 m | about 19 t |

Steel cable of the same breaking load weighs roughly four times as much.

**Footbridge example (suggested canon):** a 30 m pedestrian suspension bridge with two 2 cm² main cables (each about 35 m long including sag and anchorage) uses about 18 kg of dragline for the cables. Hangers and a silk-net deck bring the total to about 30 kg. Combined cable breaking load is about 44 t.

**Reinforcement:** fiber at 0.5% by volume in 1 m³ of concrete uses about 5 L of fiber, roughly 6.5 kg of silk. One kilometer of road surface (375 m³ from Section 12.1) at 0.3% needs about 1.1 m³ of fiber, roughly 1.5 t of silk.

**Gel barriers:** thread secretion at hagfish dilution gels about 25 m³ of water per kilogram, enough to plug a breach several meters wide.

## 13. Setting Consequences

- **Kiln industries collapse:** lime burners, cement works, brickyards and glassblowers lose their markets. Charcoal and firewood demand drops sharply, which saves forests.
- **Forgery and authentication:** CR materials glow blue under ultraviolet light from dityrosine. Inspectors with a UV source can identify genuine binder work.
- **Vermin economy:** cats, terriers and preservative salts become part of every construction contract.
- **Strategic asset:** the bug becomes worth more than a gold mine. Expect theft, breeding attempts, counterfeit "diluted" binder and guild monopolies.
- **Military engineering:** rapid fortifications, instant bridges, Tier 3 temporary walls that collapse when dispelled. Enemies will hunt for the dispel weakness.
- **Smell:** curing districts reek like tanneries.
- **Silk economy:** dragline cable makes long light bridges and rigging possible everywhere. Shipping, mountain travel and siege warfare change first.
- **Underwater frontier:** sandcastle-type cement and diving bells make harbor building and river engineering cheap.
- **Armor shift:** silk soft armor competes with mail and plate.
- **Recycling loop:** worn silk feeds back to the bug, so old ropes and nets become valuable scrap.
