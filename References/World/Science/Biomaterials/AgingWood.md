## 1. Wood Structure

Wood is a mass of hollow, elongated cells cemented together. Nearly every property that changes during seasoning and aging traces back to the chemistry of the cell wall.

### 1.1 Cell Wall Chemistry

| Component | Share of dry weight | Structure | Role | Aging behavior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cellulose | 40-50% | Long unbranched glucose chains bundled into crystalline microfibrils | Tensile strength along the grain | Crystallinity rises for centuries then chains slowly shorten |
| Hemicelluloses | 20-35% | Short branched sugar polymers (xylans, mannans) | Binds microfibrils to lignin; attracts water strongly | First component to degrade; loss lowers moisture uptake |
| Lignin | 18-35% | Three-dimensional aromatic polymer | Rigidity, compression strength, water resistance | Oxidizes slowly; source of color change and UV weathering |
| Extractives | 2-15% (higher in some tropical heartwoods) | Oils, resins, tannins, phenolics, waxes | Color, scent, natural decay resistance | Volatiles evaporate; tannins and resins oxidize and harden |
| Ash (minerals) | 0.2-1% | Calcium, potassium, magnesium, silica | Minor; silica dulls tools | Stable |

### 1.2 Cell Wall Layers

Each cell wall is built in layers:
- **Middle lamella:** lignin-rich glue between neighboring cells
- **Primary wall:** thin outer layer laid down during growth
- **S1 layer:** thin, microfibrils wound nearly crosswise
- **S2 layer:** the thickest layer, up to 80-90% of wall thickness. Its microfibril angle controls stiffness and longitudinal shrinkage. A low angle (near parallel to the cell axis) gives stiff wood with little lengthwise shrinkage. A high angle (juvenile wood, reaction wood) gives flexible wood that shrinks noticeably along its length.
- **S3 layer:** thin inner layer lining the cell cavity

### 1.3 Hardwood Anatomy

All native Hawaiian trees are flowering plants (hardwoods) or tree ferns. Hawaiʻi has no native conifers.

Hardwood tissue types:
- **Vessels (pores):** water-conducting tubes visible as holes on end grain. Large open vessels dry fast. Vessels plugged with tyloses (balloon-like ingrowths) resist liquid flow and dry slowly.
- **Fibers:** thick-walled support cells. Fiber wall thickness drives density.
- **Axial parenchyma:** storage cells running lengthwise
- **Rays:** ribbons of storage cells running from pith to bark. Rays restrain radial shrinkage, which is why radial shrinkage is smaller than tangential. Rays are also weak planes where checks open.

### 1.4 Growth Rings

In seasonal climates each year produces lighter earlywood and denser latewood. Hawaiʻi's weak seasonality means many native trees, including ʻōhiʻa and koa, form faint, irregular or missing rings. Ring counts in native wood are unreliable for aging a tree.

### 1.5 Sapwood and Heartwood

- **Sapwood:** outer living wood that conducts water. Usually pale. Contains stored sugars and starch that feed stain fungi and insects. Low natural durability in nearly every species.
- **Heartwood:** inner dead wood where the tree has deposited extractives. Usually darker, more aromatic and more decay resistant. Heartwood generally dries more slowly than sapwood because extractives and tyloses block flow.

### 1.6 Problem Wood Types

- **Pith:** the soft center of the stem. Pieces containing the pith (boxed heart) crack radially as they dry.
- **Juvenile wood:** the first 5-20 rings around the pith. Lower density, higher microfibril angle, higher lengthwise shrinkage, more warp.
- **Tension wood:** hardwoods form this on the upper side of leaning trunks and branches. It contains gelatinous fibers, saws with a fuzzy surface, shrinks abnormally along its length and causes severe bow and crook. Leaning koa often contains it.
- **Compression wood:** the conifer equivalent; not relevant to native Hawaiian trees.
- **Knots:** branch bases embedded in the trunk. Knots shrink differently from surrounding wood and loosen or crack during drying.

### 1.7 Grain Types

| Grain | Description | Drying behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Straight | Fibers parallel to the trunk axis | Dries flat; splits cleanly |
| Spiral | Fibers twist around the trunk | Boards twist as they dry |
| Interlocked | Spiral direction reverses in alternating bands (ʻōhiʻa) | Resists splitting; warps and twists; tears out when planed |
| Wavy, curly, fiddleback | Fibers ripple in waves (figured koa) | Prone to distortion; dries more slowly; produces shimmering light effects called chatoyance |

### 1.8 Density

Specific gravity (SG) is oven-dry mass divided by the mass of an equal volume of water. Density drives almost every mechanical property. Strength rises roughly in proportion to density, while hardness rises faster than density.

| Category | SG range | Native examples |
|---|---|---|
| Very light | under 0.30 | Wiliwili |
| Light | 0.30-0.45 | Pāpala, ʻohe makai, ʻaiea, ʻōlapa |
| Medium | 0.45-0.65 | Koa, kāwaʻu |
| Heavy | 0.65-0.85 | ʻŌhiʻa, māmane, naio, kōlea, ʻālaʻa |
| Very heavy | over 0.85 | Kauila, uhiuhi, lama, olopua, mēhamehame |

Values for native woods are approximate and vary with growth site.

## 2. Water in Wood

### 2.1 Moisture Content

**Dry basis (standard for lumber):**

MC (%) = (wet weight − oven-dry weight) ÷ oven-dry weight × 100

**Wet basis (common for firewood):**

w (%) = (wet weight − oven-dry weight) ÷ wet weight × 100

**Conversion:** wet basis = dry basis ÷ (1 + dry basis), using decimals. Wood at 100% MC dry basis is 50% water by weight.

### 2.2 The Three States of Water

1. **Free water:** liquid in the cell cavities. Moves by capillary flow. Its loss changes weight only.
2. **Bound water:** water molecules hydrogen-bonded to hydroxyl groups on hemicellulose and the disordered regions of cellulose. Its loss changes size and strength.
3. **Water vapor:** in the air spaces of the cell cavities

### 2.3 Fiber Saturation Point (FSP)

FSP is the moisture content where cell cavities are empty of liquid water while cell walls remain fully saturated. Typical value is 25-30% MC, with a range of about 20-40%. Woods rich in extractives tend to have lower FSP because extractives occupy space in the cell walls.

Above FSP: drying reduces weight only.
Below FSP: every percent of moisture lost changes size and strength.

### 2.4 Maximum Moisture Content

The densest wood can hold the least water because it has the least empty space:

MC max (%) = (1 ÷ SG − 1 ÷ 1.54) × 100

The number 1.54 is the density of solid cell wall material.

| SG | Maximum MC |
|---|---|
| 0.25 (wiliwili) | about 335% |
| 0.55 (koa) | about 117% |
| 0.75 (ʻōhiʻa) | about 68% |
| 1.00 (kauila) | about 35% |

### 2.5 Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC)

Wood exchanges moisture with the air until it reaches a balance point set by relative humidity (RH) and temperature. It never dries below that point without heat.

**EMC at about 21°C:**

| Relative humidity | EMC |
|---|---|
| 10% | 2.5% |
| 20% | 4.5% |
| 30% | 6.2% |
| 40% | 7.7% |
| 50% | 9.2% |
| 60% | 11.0% |
| 70% | 13.1% |
| 80% | 16.0% |
| 90% | 20.5% |
| 98% | 26.9% |

Higher temperature lowers EMC slightly, roughly one point for every 15-20°C rise at the same RH.

### 2.6 Hysteresis

Wood drying down from green settles 1-2 points higher than wood picking up moisture from a drier state at the same humidity. Wood that has been fully dried once and then exposed to humid air stays slightly drier than wood that never dried below that level.

### 2.7 Response Time

- Thin surfaces track daily humidity swings.
- A 25 mm board takes weeks to follow a seasonal shift.
- A 150 mm beam lags months to years.
- Finishes slow moisture exchange without stopping it.

## 3. Shrinkage and Movement

### 3.1 Total Shrinkage, Green to Oven-Dry

| Direction | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tangential (parallel to growth rings) | 4-12% | Largest |
| Radial (across rings, along rays) | 2-8% | Restrained by rays |
| Longitudinal (along grain) | 0.1-0.2% | Larger in juvenile and tension wood, up to 1-2% |
| Volumetric | 7-20% | Sum of all three |

The tangential to radial ratio (T/R) usually runs 1.5 to 2.5. A low T/R ratio means stable wood that distorts little. Koa has an unusually low T/R ratio, around 1.1, which is part of its reputation for stability.

### 3.2 Shrinkage Calculation

Shrinkage between FSP and any lower moisture content is close to linear:

Shrinkage (%) = Total shrinkage × (FSP − MC) ÷ FSP

**Example:** Total tangential shrinkage 8%. Drying from 30% to 12%:
8 × (30 − 12) ÷ 30 = 4.8%
A 200 mm wide flatsawn board loses about 9.6 mm of width.

### 3.3 In-Service Movement

Dimensional change per 1% change in moisture content (below FSP):
- Tangential: about 0.25-0.35%
- Radial: about 0.15-0.20%

**Example:** A 300 mm flatsawn panel moved between a leeward house (11% EMC) and a windward house (17% EMC) changes width by about 300 × 6 × 0.003 = 5.4 mm. Joinery must allow for this.

### 3.4 Sawing Patterns

| Cut | Face orientation | Width shrinks | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Flatsawn (plainsawn) | Tangential face | Tangentially (most) | Cups away from the heart side; widest boards; most figure on some species |
| Quartersawn | Radial face | Radially (least) | Stays flat; most stable; ray fleck visible |
| Riftsawn | Rings at 30-60° to face | In between | Straight grain look; stable |

### 3.5 Distortion Types

- **Cup:** curl across the width. Flatsawn boards cup away from the heart side.
- **Bow:** curve along the length on the face
- **Crook:** curve along the length on the edge
- **Twist:** corners rise out of plane. From spiral or interlocked grain.
- **Diamonding:** square stock with diagonal rings shrinks into a rhombus
- **Ovaling:** round turning blanks become oval

### 3.6 Round Logs and Poles

Tangential shrinkage exceeds radial shrinkage, so a whole log cannot shrink evenly. Its circumference wants to shrink more than its radius allows. The result is V-shaped radial cracks running in from the bark toward the pith.

Control methods:
- Split the log in half or quarters to remove the pith.
- Saw a kerf (relief slot) along one side to the pith so the crack opens there.
- Bore out the pith on turning stock.

## 4. Drying Physics

### 4.1 Two Drying Phases

**Constant-rate phase (above FSP):**
- Free water flows through cell cavities and pits by capillary action toward the surface.
- Drying speed is controlled by the air: temperature, humidity and airflow.
- Weight drops quickly with no shrinkage.

**Falling-rate phase (below FSP):**
- Bound water moves by diffusion through cell walls and as vapor through cavities.
- Drying speed is controlled by the wood: density, thickness and permeability.
- Shrinkage and strength gain begin.

### 4.2 The Surface Stress Problem

The surface drops below FSP while the core is still wet. The surface tries to shrink around a core that will not shrink.

Sequence:
1. Early drying: surface in tension, core in compression. Too-fast drying opens surface checks.
2. Surface sets in a stretched state.
3. Late drying: the core finally shrinks. Stress reverses. Surface goes into compression while the core goes into tension. Severe cases split the core internally (honeycombing).
4. The locked-in reversed stress is called case hardening. It causes boards to cup or pinch saws when resawn.

### 4.3 Thickness and Drying Time

Diffusion-controlled drying time scales roughly with the square of thickness. Doubling thickness can triple or quadruple drying time. The "one year per inch" rule works for 25-50 mm stock and underestimates thicker stock.

### 4.4 Drying Factors

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Each 10°C rise speeds diffusion substantially |
| Relative humidity | Lower RH lowers final EMC and speeds surface drying |
| Airflow | Faster airflow helps up to about 1-2 m/s across the stack, then gains taper off |
| Thickness | Drying time rises with roughly the square of thickness |
| Density | Dense woods dry slower and check more easily |
| Permeability | Sapwood and open-pored woods dry faster than heartwood and plugged woods |
| Grain exposure | End grain loses water 10-15 times faster than side grain |

## 5. Air Drying Procedure

### 5.1 Felling

- Seasonal sap variation is small in Hawaiʻi. Felling timing matters less than processing speed.
- Fell at the start of the drier months for the specific site so the first drying stage happens in favorable weather.
- Fell cleanly to avoid shake (ring separation) and crushing.

### 5.2 Log Handling

- In warm, humid lowlands, logs left lying stain within days and begin decaying within months.
- Process within days where possible.
- Keep logs off the ground on skids.
- Seal the ends immediately.
- Remove bark from logs that will sit. Bark slows drying and shelters beetles.

### 5.3 Breakdown

- Split or saw to final rough size plus 10-15% for shrinkage and planing.
- Remove the pith from boards and blocks.
- Quarter logs for small stock.
- Thin pieces dry fastest; cut no thicker than the end use needs.

### 5.4 End Sealing

End grain loses water 10-15 times faster than side grain. Unsealed ends dry ahead of the rest of the board and crack.

Sealing materials:
- Wax
- Plant resin or pitch
- Clay or mud mixed with fat or fiber
- Animal fat or oil
- Any thick paint or wax emulsion

Apply heavily within hours of cutting. Reapply if cracks appear.

### 5.5 Stack Construction

**Foundation:**
- Level base 300-450 mm off the ground on piers or logs
- Good drainage; no vegetation under the stack
- Foundation beams spaced to match the sticker spacing

**Stickers:**
- Thin dry strips 19-25 mm thick, all the same thickness
- Spaced 300-600 mm apart along the boards. Closer for thin stock or warp-prone species.
- Stacked vertically in line from bottom to top so weight passes straight down
- First and last stickers flush with board ends to protect the ends

**Layers:**
- Boards of equal thickness in each layer
- Small gaps between boards in wide stacks to create vertical airflow chimneys
- Stack width about 1.2-1.8 m

**Top:**
- Overhanging sloped roof to shed rain and block direct sun
- Heavy weight (stones, logs) spread across the top to restrain warp in the upper layers

**Orientation:**
- Stickers parallel to the prevailing wind so air flows between layers. On windward and northern Hawaiʻi sites that means stickers running roughly northeast to southwest.

**Shelter:**
- Dense species and figured koa need shade during the first weeks to prevent surface checks.
- In wet zones an open-sided shed is necessary.

### 5.6 Poles and Posts

- Peel bark completely.
- Leave whole for posts.
- Saw a kerf along one side to the pith to control where checks open.
- Stand vertically or rack off the ground.

### 5.7 Bowls and Turning Blanks

- Rough-turn green to a wall thickness about 10% of the diameter.
- Dry slowly inside a container of shavings or a breathable sack.
- Re-turn to final shape once dry and stable.

### 5.8 Final Conditioning

Bring wood into the space where it will be used weeks before working it so it reaches the local EMC.

## 6. Kilns and Accelerated Drying

### 6.1 Conventional Kilns

- Heated chamber with fans and humidity control
- Schedule starts mild (about 40-50°C, high humidity) and ends hot (70-80°C or higher, low humidity).
- Equalizing step brings all boards to the same MC.
- Conditioning step uses a short burst of high humidity to relieve case hardening.
- Hardwood 25 mm: days to weeks

### 6.2 Dehumidification Kilns

- Lower temperature (about 25-50°C)
- Moisture removed by a refrigeration coil
- Gentle; suited to check-prone dense woods

### 6.3 Solar Kilns

- Insulated box with a clear south-facing glazed roof or wall, a black heat absorber, fans and vents
- Reaches 10-25°C above outside air during the day
- Night cooling with higher humidity naturally conditions the wood and reduces case hardening.
- Dries 25 mm hardwood to 8-12% in weeks to a few months
- Highly effective on Hawaiʻi's leeward slopes

### 6.4 Fire and Smoke Drying

- Wood racked above a low smoky fire or in a smokehouse
- Speeds drying while smoke deposits phenolic compounds that slightly resist insects and fungi
- Risk of uneven drying and checking if heat is too strong

### 6.5 Water Seasoning

- Traditional practice: logs soaked in flowing water for weeks to months, then air dried
- Leaches sugars and starch that feed stain fungi and insects
- Reported to reduce checking in some species

### 6.6 Industrial Methods

Vacuum drying, radio-frequency drying and microwave drying use pressure or electromagnetic energy to drive moisture out quickly. These are mentioned for completeness. Magic vacuum path: `WoodProcessing.md`.

## 7. Drying Defects

| Defect | Appearance | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| End checks | Cracks at board ends | Fast end-grain drying | Seal ends; flush end stickers |
| Surface checks | Short cracks on faces, usually along rays | Surface drying too fast | Shade, slower airflow, higher humidity early |
| Honeycombing | Internal cracks hidden inside | Severe case hardening; core tension | Avoid harsh early drying |
| Case hardening | Locked-in stress; boards cup or pinch saws when resawn | Surface set in tension early | Conditioning; slower schedule |
| Collapse | Washboard surface, crushed cells | Very wet dense wood dried too hot | Air dry below FSP before heat |
| Cup | Curl across width | Tangential versus radial shrinkage | Quartersaw; weight stacks |
| Bow, crook | Lengthwise curve | Reaction or juvenile wood | Sort out reaction wood; close stickers |
| Twist | Corners out of plane | Spiral or interlocked grain | Heavy top weight; close stickers |
| Ring shake | Separation along growth rings | Felling stress; growth stresses | Careful felling |
| Blue stain | Gray-blue streaks in sapwood | Stain fungi in warm wet wood | Process quickly; keep airflow |
| Spalting | Black zone lines and bleaching | Early decay fungi | Dry quickly; or allow on purpose for decorative effect and stop before soft |
| Sticker stain | Dark or light bands where stickers sat | Slow drying under sticker contact | Dry stickers; narrow stickers |
| Mold | Fuzzy surface growth | Stagnant humid air | Airflow |

## 8. Measuring Moisture Without Instruments

### 8.1 Oven-Dry Method (the standard)

1. Cut a cross-section wafer about 25 mm thick at least 300 mm in from a board end.
2. Weigh it immediately.
3. Dry at 103°C (just above boiling) until weight stops changing, usually about 24 hours.
4. Weigh again.
5. MC = (wet − dry) ÷ dry × 100.

### 8.2 Sample Board Method (tracking a stack)

1. Cut a sample board 600 mm long from a representative board. Seal both ends.
2. Cut wafers from beside it and oven-dry them to find its starting MC.
3. Weigh the sample board. Calculate its oven-dry weight:
   Oven-dry weight = current weight ÷ (1 + MC as decimal)
4. Place the sample board in the stack where it can be pulled out.
5. Weigh it periodically. Current MC = (current weight ÷ oven-dry weight − 1) × 100.
6. When weight stops changing, the stack has reached EMC.

### 8.3 Without an Oven

- Dry thin shavings near a fire or in hot sun until they stop losing weight. Shavings dry far faster than solid wood and approximate oven-dry weight.
- A pan balance or steelyard made from a straight stick and a known weight works for repeated weighings.

### 8.4 Field Signs of Dry Wood

- Weight no longer drops between weighings
- Clear ringing sound when two pieces are knocked together. Wet wood thuds.
- Checks at the ends stop growing
- Shavings curl and break crisply instead of tearing
- Bark loosens and falls away

### 8.5 Meters

- Pin meters measure electrical resistance between two pins. Resistance rises sharply as wood dries.
- Pinless meters measure electrical properties through the surface.
- Both are reliable between about 7% and 25-30% MC.

## 9. Long-Term Aging Chemistry

Once wood reaches EMC it continues changing for centuries. These changes run faster in warm, humid conditions.

### 9.1 Hemicellulose Breakdown

- Acetyl groups split off hemicellulose, releasing acetic acid.
- The acid catalyzes further breakdown of hemicellulose, a slow self-feeding process.
- Hemicellulose holds most of the water-binding sites. Its loss lowers EMC by 1-2 percentage points over centuries.
- Lower EMC means smaller seasonal swelling and shrinking.
- The faint vinegar smell of old wooden chests comes from this acid.

### 9.2 Cellulose Changes

- The disordered (amorphous) regions of cellulose slowly reorganize into crystalline form.
- Crystallinity increases for the first several centuries, adding stiffness and strength.
- Chain length (degree of polymerization) slowly decreases through hydrolysis and oxidation.
- After a few centuries chain shortening dominates and strength declines.

### 9.3 Lignin Changes

- Lignin oxidizes to form quinones and other colored compounds.
- Light accelerates this, causing surface yellowing, browning or darkening.
- Some cross-linking increases rigidity.

### 9.4 Extractive Changes

- Volatile oils and terpenes evaporate. Scent fades in most species.
- Low-volatility oils (sandalwood santalols) persist for decades to centuries.
- Tannins polymerize and darken.
- Resins harden and stop bleeding.
- Some extractives that provide decay resistance slowly leach or break down, so very old heartwood can lose some natural durability.

### 9.5 Stress Relaxation

Growth stresses from the living tree and drying stresses slowly relax. Old wood resaws with less movement.

### 9.6 Mass and Density

Old wood loses a few percent of its mass from hemicellulose breakdown and extractive loss. Density drops slightly.

### 9.7 Rate and Temperature

Chemical aging roughly doubles in speed with every 10°C rise in temperature. Lowland Hawaiʻi averages around 24°C. Japanese temple timber studies represent sites averaging around 15°C. On that basis aging chemistry in lowland Hawaiʻi would run roughly 1.5 to 2 times faster. Cool upland sites above 1,500 m would age wood at a rate close to temperate temples.

## 10. Long-Term Mechanical Changes

### 10.1 Seasoning Gains (green to 12% MC)

For each 1% drop in MC below FSP, typical gains are:

| Property | Gain per 1% MC drop | Approximate total gain, green to 12% |
|---|---|---|
| Compression strength along grain | ~6% | Roughly doubles |
| Compression perpendicular to grain | ~5.5% | Roughly doubles |
| Bending strength (modulus of rupture) | ~4% | +50-70% |
| Shear strength | ~3% | +40-50% |
| Stiffness (modulus of elasticity) | ~2% | +20-30% |
| Tension perpendicular to grain | ~1.5% | +20-25% |
| Impact work | ~0.5% or less | Little change |

### 10.2 Centuries-Scale Curve

Studies of Japanese cypress (hinoki) from temples up to about 1,300 years old show the following trend:

| Age since felling | Bending and compression strength | Stiffness | Impact toughness | EMC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Freshly seasoned | Baseline | Baseline | Baseline | Baseline |
| 50-100 years | Rising | Rising | Beginning to fall | Down about half a point |
| 200-300 years | Peak, roughly 10-30% above baseline | Peak | Clearly lower | Down about 1 point |
| 500-700 years | Declining slowly | Declining | Lower | Down 1-2 points |
| 1,000-1,300 years | Back near baseline | Near baseline | Substantially lower | Down 1-2 points |

Denser hardwoods such as zelkova peaked earlier and declined faster than hinoki.

### 10.3 Brittleness

Impact toughness falls steadily over the entire aging period. Very old wood is strong under steady loads while breaking more suddenly under shock. Old tool handles and spear shafts are more likely to snap cleanly.

## 11. Acoustic Aging

### 11.1 Key Properties

- **Speed of sound:** square root of stiffness divided by density. Higher means a brighter response.
- **Sound radiation coefficient:** square root of (stiffness ÷ density³). Higher means a louder instrument for a given effort.
- **Damping (loss coefficient):** how quickly vibration dies out. Lower means longer sustain.

### 11.2 Changes With Age

- Damping decreases as hemicellulose degrades and extractives harden.
- Stiffness relative to weight increases during the first centuries.
- Lower EMC means less moisture to absorb vibration.
- The combined effect is a clearer, louder, longer-ringing tone. This is the basis for the value of aged tonewood.

### 11.3 Accelerated Imitation

Thermal modification (heating at 160-220°C without oxygen) degrades hemicellulose in hours. The result imitates decades of aging in damping and moisture behavior while reducing toughness.

### 11.4 Native Tonewood

Koa is a prized tonewood for stringed instruments, known for a bright, focused midrange that warms with age and playing.

## 12. Color and Appearance

### 12.1 Mechanisms

- **Photo-oxidation of lignin:** UV and visible light break down lignin at the surface into yellow and brown compounds.
- **Extractive photochemistry:** light darkens some extractives and bleaches others.
- **Dark oxidation:** slower color shift from oxygen even without light
- **Heat:** speeds all reactions

### 12.2 Timing

Most visible color change happens in the first months to few years of light exposure. A board partly covered by an object shows a sharp outline where light was blocked. Color continues to deepen slowly after that.

### 12.3 Native Species Color Behavior

| Species | Fresh color | Aged color |
|---|---|---|
| Koa | Golden to reddish brown | Deeper red-brown; figure becomes more pronounced under finish |
| ʻŌhiʻa | Reddish brown heartwood, paler sapwood | Darker brown |
| ʻIliahi | Yellow-brown oily heartwood | Darker gold-brown |
| Lama | Dark heartwood | Darker |
| Kōlea | Reddish brown | Deeper red-brown |
| ʻAhakea | Yellow | Golden to tan |
| Aʻe (Zanthoxylum) | Yellow | Golden to tan |
| Wiliwili | Pale cream | Slightly yellowed |

### 12.4 Surface Patina

Handling adds oils and polish. Repeated waxing and use build a deep surface sheen. Sharp edges round off and grain texture rises slightly as softer wood wears faster than dense zones.

## 13. Outdoor Weathering

### 13.1 Process

1. UV light breaks down lignin in the outer fraction of a millimeter.
2. Rain washes away the broken lignin, leaving loose cellulose fibers.
3. Surface fibers detach and erode.
4. Mold fungi colonize the surface, adding gray to black color.
5. Wet-dry cycling opens checks along the grain.

### 13.2 Rate

- Surface erosion: about 6 mm (1/4 in) per century for softwoods, less for dense hardwoods.
- Dense zones erode more slowly than light zones, leaving ridged texture.
- Wood beneath the weathered layer stays sound if it dries between wettings.

### 13.3 Hawaiʻi Conditions

- UV index regularly reaches 11 or higher at sea level and higher at elevation. Weathering runs faster than in temperate regions.
- Salt spray on coasts deposits salt crystals that push fibers apart, giving a fuzzy surface. Salt also inhibits decay fungi.
- Windward rain keeps surfaces wet long enough for decay. Leeward surfaces weather mostly from sun.

## 14. Biological Degradation

### 14.1 Conditions Fungi Need

- Moisture above about 20% MC (active decay usually above 25-30%)
- Oxygen
- Temperature between about 10 and 35°C, fastest at 25-30°C
- Food (wood itself)

Removing any one stops decay. Wood kept below 20% MC or fully submerged does not rot. Lowland Hawaiʻi sits near the ideal decay temperature year-round.

### 14.2 Fungal Decay Types

| Type | What it eats | Appearance | Strength loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brown rot | Cellulose and hemicellulose; leaves lignin | Brown, cracks into cubes, crumbles | Very rapid; large strength loss at small weight loss |
| White rot | All components including lignin | Bleached, stringy or spongy | Gradual |
| Soft rot | Cellulose within cell walls | Soft surface layer; fine cracks when dry | Slow; tolerates very wet or ground contact conditions |
| Stain fungi | Sugars and starch in sapwood | Blue-gray streaks | Minimal; increases permeability |
| Molds | Surface sugars | Fuzzy colored growth | None |

### 14.3 Insects and Marine Borers in a Human-Free Hawaiʻi

**Present:**
- Endemic longhorn beetles (Plagithmysus and relatives): many species, most specialized on particular native trees such as koa, māmane and ʻōhiʻa. Larvae bore into living, stressed, dying or freshly felled wood. Debarking and prompt drying deny them access.
- Other endemic wood-boring beetles: attack dead and decaying wood
- Shipworms and gribbles: marine borers that tunnel into any wood in seawater, including driftwood, pilings and hulls

**Absent:**
- The destructive termites now in Hawaiʻi (Formosan subterranean termite, West Indian drywood termite) are human introductions.
- Ants: Hawaiʻi has no native ant species.

## 15. Waterlogged and Buried Wood

### 15.1 Anaerobic Preservation

Wood buried in oxygen-starved mud, peat or still water escapes most fungi. Only slow bacterial erosion and soft rot act on it. Such wood can last thousands of years.

### 15.2 Condition

- Cellulose in the outer layers degrades while lignin remains.
- Wood holds its shape while waterlogged because water supports the weakened cell walls.
- Air drying collapses and cracks the weakened cells severely.

### 15.3 Mineral Staining

Iron in water reacts with tannins to blacken wood. This produces "bog oak" type dark wood.

### 15.4 Recovery

- Keep wet until ready to process.
- Dry extremely slowly, wrapped or buried in damp material, over months to years.
- Modern conservation replaces water with polyethylene glycol before drying.

### 15.5 Hawaiʻi Settings

- Montane bogs can preserve wood and pollen for thousands of years.
- Dry lava tubes hold stable, dry air that preserves wood, plant remains and bones for centuries to millennia.

## 16. Fire, Smoke and Heat Treatment

### 16.1 Fire Hardening

- Heat a spear point or digging stick tip slowly over coals until lightly browned.
- Drives out moisture and hardens the surface.
- Overheating makes the tip brittle.

### 16.2 Surface Charring

- Burn the surface to a char layer 2-5 mm deep, then brush off loose char.
- The carbon layer resists UV, insects and decay fungi.
- Reduces surface hardness and strength slightly.

### 16.3 Smoke Treatment

- Smoke deposits phenolic and tar compounds on and just under the surface.
- Provides mild resistance to insects and fungi.

### 16.4 Thermal Modification

- Heating at 160-230°C in steam, inert gas, hot oil or buried in hot sand without oxygen
- Reduces EMC by up to 40-50%
- Greatly increases dimensional stability and decay resistance
- Darkens wood throughout
- Reduces strength 10-30%, with the largest loss in toughness

### 16.5 Torrefaction

Heating to 200-300°C produces a brittle, water-resistant, energy-dense fuel.

### 16.6 Charcoal

- Pyrolysis above about 300°C in limited air (pit or earth-covered mound)
- Yield 25-35% of the dry wood weight
- Heat value 29-33 MJ/kg, roughly double that of dry wood
- Dense woods (ʻōhiʻa, lama) give dense, long-burning charcoal

## 17. Fossilization and Lava Tree Molds

### 17.1 Petrification

- Requires burial in sediment soaked with mineral-rich water
- Minerals (usually silica) fill cell cavities and replace cell walls molecule by molecule
- Takes thousands to millions of years
- Can preserve cell-level detail in stone

### 17.2 Lava Tree Molds

Unique to volcanic forests:
1. Fluid pāhoehoe lava flows through a wet forest.
2. Lava chills against the moisture-rich trunk and forms a solid shell.
3. The trunk burns away inside.
4. The flow level drops as lava drains downhill.
5. Hollow stone chimneys 1-4 m tall remain standing, often with bark texture imprinted inside.

Charcoal found beneath lava flows is used to radiocarbon-date the eruptions.

## 18. Heartwood Formation in the Living Tree

### 18.1 Process

- As a tree ages, inner sapwood cells stop conducting water.
- Living ray and parenchyma cells convert stored starch into extractives.
- Extractives are deposited in cell walls and cavities.
- Tyloses plug vessels in many species.
- The cells die and the zone becomes heartwood.

### 18.2 Effects of Tree Age

- Heartwood proportion increases with tree age.
- Outer heartwood of old trees usually has the highest extractive content and the best decay resistance.
- Inner heartwood near the pith of very old trees can lose some extractives over time.
- Slow-grown old trees from high or stressful sites usually give denser, darker, more figured wood.

### 18.3 Native Examples

- **ʻIliahi (sandalwood):** fragrant heartwood forms over decades. Oil concentration is highest in the roots and lower trunk of mature trees. Young trees are mostly scentless sapwood.
- **Koa:** old, slow-grown trees from higher elevations yield the darkest color and most figure. Young fast-grown koa is paler and plainer.
- **ʻŌhiʻa:** old trees carry a large, dark, dense heartwood core.

## 21. Firewood Seasoning

### 21.1 Heat Value

Dry wood of nearly any species yields about 19-20 MJ/kg at 0% moisture. Water reduces usable heat two ways: it adds dead weight and it absorbs heat to evaporate.

Net heat per kg of wet wood:

Net heat (MJ/kg) ≈ 19 × (1 − w) − 2.44 × w
where w is wet-basis moisture as a decimal.

| Condition | Wet-basis moisture | Net heat |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh cut | 50% | ~8.3 MJ/kg |
| Partially seasoned | 35% | ~11.5 MJ/kg |
| Seasoned | 20% | ~14.7 MJ/kg |
| Kiln dry | 10% | ~16.9 MJ/kg |

Seasoned wood gives nearly double the heat per kilogram compared with fresh wood.

### 21.2 Seasoning Method

- Cut to length and split. Splitting exposes inner wood and speeds drying many times over.
- Pieces 75-150 mm across dry fastest.
- Stack off the ground, bark side up, with a top cover and open sides.
- Most woods season in 6-12 months. Very dense woods need 12-24 months.

### 21.3 Signs of Seasoned Firewood

- Radial cracks across the ends
- Much lighter than fresh pieces
- Hollow ring when knocked together
- Bark loose or falling off
- Lights readily and hisses little

### 21.4 Native Firewood

| Species | Fuel quality |
|---|---|
| ʻŌhiʻa | Excellent; dense, hot, long-lasting coals |
| Māmane | Excellent; very hot |
| Naio | Excellent; fragrant smoke |

## 22. Hawaiʻi Drying and Aging Guide

### 22.1 Approximate EMC by Zone

| Zone | Typical air | Approximate EMC under cover |
|---|---|---|
| Leeward coast (Kawaihae, Waikoloa) | Warm, relative humidity 60-70% | 11-13% |
| Kona mid-slope (300-900 m) | Warm, frequent afternoon clouds | 13-15% |
| Hilo and windward coast | Warm, humid, frequent rain | 15-17% |
| Windward wet forest and cloud belt | Near-constant cloud | 19-22%; air drying stalls |
| Upper leeward slopes and the Saddle (1,800-2,500 m) | Cool, dry air above clouds much of the time | 9-12% |
| Subalpine and alpine (above the inversion) | Cold, very dry, intense sun | 5-8%; fast drying with severe checking risk |

### 22.2 Best Drying Sites

- Leeward lower and middle slopes between about 200 and 1,500 m: warm, breezy and dry enough to reach usable EMC
- The Saddle and upper leeward slopes: fast drying with frost and strong sun; shade required
- Windward lowlands: unsuitable for seasoning without heat; maximum decay hazard

### 22.3 Decay Hazard by Zone

| Zone | Decay hazard |
|---|---|
| Windward lowland wet forest | Extreme |
| Windward montane wet forest | High (cooler temperatures slow fungi somewhat) |
| Kona mesic belt | Moderate to high |
| Leeward lowland dry | Low above ground; moderate in ground contact |
| Subalpine | Low |

### 22.4 Approximate Air-Drying Times for 25 mm Native Boards (leeward conditions)

| Species | Approximate time to EMC | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wiliwili | 2-4 months | Protect from stain; keep dry |
| Pāpala, ʻohe makai | 2-4 months | Light woods dry fast |
| Koa | 6-12 months | Shade figured stock; weight the stack |
| Kōlea, ʻālaʻa | 9-15 months | Moderate checking risk |
| ʻŌhiʻa | 12-18 months or more | Seal ends; shade; heavy weighting; checks and twists |
| Māmane, naio | 12-18 months | Dense; slow |
| Kauila, uhiuhi, lama, olopua | 18-24 months or more | Keep dimensions small; dry very slowly |

These are field estimates. Thicker stock takes disproportionately longer.
