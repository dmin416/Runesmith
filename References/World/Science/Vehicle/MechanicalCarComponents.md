## Core Manufacturing Processes

Nearly every part below is made by some combination of these processes:

- **Sand casting:** A wooden pattern is pressed into packed sand to form a mold. Sand "cores" are set inside to create hollow passages. Molten iron, brass or aluminum is poured in, cooled and broken out.
- **Forging:** Heated steel is hammered or pressed between dies. This aligns the metal's grain and produces the strongest parts, such as crankshafts, connecting rods, axles and gears.
- **Machining:** Lathes cut round surfaces. Milling machines cut flat surfaces and slots. Drills and boring bars make holes. Grinders and hones bring surfaces to final precision.
- **Stamping:** Sheet steel is pressed between matched steel dies to form panels and brackets.
- **Heat treatment:** Steel is hardened by heating and quenching, then tempered to reduce brittleness. Case hardening (carburizing) gives a hard skin over a tough core. Induction hardening heats only the surface with electric current.
- **Joining:** Parts are joined by hot rivets, bolts, brazing, gas welding, arc welding or spot welding.

## 1. Frame / Chassis

The frame is the structural backbone that everything mounts to.

**Simplest:** A wooden ladder frame of ash or oak, taken from carriage building. It is joined with mortise and tenon joints, bolted and reinforced with iron straps forged by a blacksmith.

**Intermediate:** Two steel C-channel side rails, either rolled or pressed from steel plate, connected by crossmembers with hot rivets. Rivets are heated red, inserted and hammered over so they shrink tight as they cool.

**Most capable:**
- **Welded frames:** Perimeter and X-braced frames, arc welded in jigs.
- **Unibody (monocoque):** The body itself carries the loads. Dozens of stamped panels are spot welded into a single shell. The Lancia Lambda pioneered this in 1922, and it was standard by the 1960s.
- **Tubular spaceframe:** Used in racing. Chrome-moly steel tubes are cut, notched to fit and welded in a fixture into a triangulated cage that is very stiff for its weight.

## 2. Body

**Simplest:** An open buggy body built by a coachbuilder. A wooden framework is covered in wood panels, then given many coats of varnish, each hand-sanded.

**Intermediate:**
- **Coachbuilt metal skin:** A wood frame is covered with steel or aluminum sheet shaped by hand using hammers, shot bags and the English wheel (a rolling machine that stretches metal into compound curves).
- **All-steel stamped body:** Introduced by Budd for Dodge in 1914. Panels are pressed in large dies and welded together.

**Most capable:**
- **Large-scale stamping:** Deep-draw stampings come from hardened tool-steel dies, followed by robotic-free spot welding lines.
- **Corrosion protection:** Phosphate dip, then electrocoat primer, where the body is dipped in paint and electrically charged so paint coats every hidden cavity. Enamel or lacquer topcoats are oven-baked.
- **Fiberglass:** Glass fiber mat is laid up in molds with resin, as on the 1953 Corvette.

## 3. Engine (Overall)

The engine runs on the four-stroke cycle: intake, compression, power, exhaust.

**Simplest:** The 1885 Benz had a single cylinder, very low compression and under 1 horsepower. The intake valve was "automatic," sucked open by piston vacuum.

**Intermediate:** An inline four-cylinder side-valve ("flathead") cast-iron engine. The Ford Model T made about 20 horsepower from 2.9 liters.

**Most capable:** Overhead-valve V8s (the Chevrolet small-block, 1955), then overhead-cam and dual-overhead-cam designs with four valves per cylinder, high compression and mechanical fuel injection. Some added a supercharger or turbocharger. The Cosworth DFV racing engine (1967) made around 400 horsepower from 3 liters with no computer.

### 3a. Engine Block

**Simplest:** Cylinders are cast individually in iron and bolted to a separate crankcase. Bores are cut on a boring mill.

**Intermediate:** A monobloc: all cylinders and the crankcase in one iron casting, with water jackets formed by sand cores inside the mold. It is machined on dedicated multi-spindle machines that drill and bore many holes at once.

**Most capable:**
- **Thin-wall precision iron castings:** Accurately positioned cores make blocks lighter.
- **Aluminum blocks:** These use pressed-in iron cylinder liners or removable "wet" liners.
- **Precision finishing:** Bores are honed to a crosshatch pattern that holds oil. Main bearing bores are line-bored in one pass for perfect alignment.

### 3b. Cylinder Head

**Simplest:** No separate head. The cylinder top is cast closed.

**Intermediate:** A detachable cast-iron flathead containing the combustion chamber, with the valves in the block beside the cylinder.

**Most capable:**
- **Design:** Aluminum crossflow heads with hemispherical or pentroof combustion chambers.
- **Valve fittings:** Hardened steel valve seats are pressed in while the head is hot and shrink tight. Bronze valve guides are installed and reamed.
- **Camshaft mounting:** Overhead camshaft bearings are machined into the head.

### 3c. Pistons and Rings

**Simplest:** Heavy cast-iron pistons turned on a lathe. Rings are cast iron, cut from a turned sleeve and split.

**Intermediate:** Cast aluminum alloy pistons with split skirts to allow for expansion.

**Most capable:**
- **Pistons:** Forged aluminum, cam-ground slightly oval so they become round when hot, and tin plated.
- **Rings:** Chrome- or moly-faced compression rings, plus multi-piece oil control rings.

### 3d. Connecting Rods and Bearings

**Simplest:** Rods forged by a blacksmith. Babbitt (a soft tin-lead alloy) is melted and poured directly into the rod, then scraped by hand until it fits the crank.

**Intermediate:** Drop-forged steel rods with poured babbitt.

**Most capable:**
- **Rods:** Forged alloy steel (such as 4340), shot-peened to resist fatigue cracks.
- **Bearings:** Replaceable precision insert bearings with a steel backing and a copper-lead or tri-metal lining.

### 3e. Crankshaft

**Simplest:** Hand forged and machined, with two main bearings and no counterweights.

**Intermediate:** Drop-forged carbon steel with three main bearings, turned on lathes using offset fixtures for the crankpins.

**Most capable:**
- **Construction:** Forged alloy steel with integral counterweights and five main bearings on an inline four.
- **Finishing:** Journals are induction hardened or nitrided, then ground to within a few ten-thousandths of an inch.
- **Balancing:** Dynamically balanced on a spinning machine, with a rubber-bonded harmonic damper on the nose.

### 3f. Valvetrain and Camshaft

**Simplest:** An automatic intake valve held shut by a light spring and opened by suction. The exhaust valve is pushed open by a cam. Valves are forged steel and lapped into their seats by hand with grinding paste.

**Intermediate:** Side valves, both cam-operated, driven by gears from a camshaft in the block.

**Most capable:**
- **Overhead valve (pushrods):** Uses hydraulic lifters that self-adjust using oil pressure.
- **Overhead cam:** Chain-driven or gear-driven, up to dual overhead cams. Sodium-filled exhaust valves carry heat away from the valve head. Double valve springs and roller followers handle high rpm.
- **Desmodromic valves:** Closed by a second cam instead of a spring (Mercedes W196 racer).
- **Camshaft manufacture:** Forged steel or chilled cast iron. Lobes are ground on a cam grinder that copies the shape of a master cam.

## 4. Fuel System

### Tank and Pump

**Simplest:** A soldered tin-plate tank mounted above the engine, feeding by gravity. Model T owners sometimes backed up steep hills to keep fuel flowing.

**Intermediate:** A rear-mounted tank of stamped terne-coated steel halves, seam welded together. A mechanical diaphragm pump is driven by a lobe on the camshaft.

**Most capable:** High-pressure mechanical pumps, or electric pumps with no electronics, plus baffled racing fuel cells.

### Carburetor

**Simplest:** A surface carburetor, where air passes over a pool of gasoline and picks up vapor. Later came the float-feed spray carburetor (Maybach, 1893): a float valve keeps fuel at a set level, and airflow through a venturi draws fuel out of a jet.

**Intermediate:** Updraft, then downdraft carburetors with added circuits:
- **Idle circuit:** Supplies fuel at idle.
- **Accelerator pump:** Squirts extra fuel when the throttle opens.
- **Choke:** Enriches the mixture for cold starts.
- **Power valve:** Enriches the mixture at full throttle.

**Most capable:** Progressive four-barrel carburetors, constant-velocity SU carburetors with a tapered metering needle, and multiple twin-choke Webers (one barrel per cylinder).
- **Bodies:** Die-cast zinc or aluminum.
- **Jets:** Brass, drilled to calibrated sizes.
- **Floats:** Soldered brass or molded material.

### Mechanical Fuel Injection (Peak Non-Computer Fuel Delivery)

- **Bosch (Mercedes 300SL, 1954):** A cam-driven pump has one plunger per cylinder, injecting directly into the cylinder.
- **Kugelfischer:** A three-dimensional "space cam" reads both throttle position and rpm to set fuel quantity mechanically.
- **Lucas:** Used a shuttle-metering system.
- **Hilborn:** Constant-flow injection for racing.
- **Manufacture:** Plungers and barrels are lapped together to clearances of a few microns.

### Forced Induction

- **Roots supercharger:** Two lobed rotors, gear-driven from the crank (1920s Mercedes, Blower Bentley).
- **Centrifugal supercharger:** A belt-driven impeller.
- **Turbocharger:** An exhaust-driven turbine spins a compressor (Oldsmobile Jetfire and Corvair Spyder, 1962). Turbine wheels are investment-cast in nickel superalloy. A spring-loaded wastegate limits boost with no electronics.

### Air Filter

Progression: wire screen, then oil-bath air cleaner, then pleated paper element.

## 5. Ignition System

**Simplest:**
- **Hot tube:** A platinum or nickel tube screwed into the cylinder is heated red by an external burner. Compression pushes the mixture into the tube, where it ignites.
- **Trembler coil (Model T):** A buzzing vibrator contact makes a stream of sparks.

**Intermediate:**
- **High-tension magneto (Bosch, 1902):** A spinning magnet and windings generate their own high voltage. No battery is needed. Still used in aircraft.
- **Kettering battery-coil system (about 1910):**
  - The battery feeds the coil's primary winding, made of a few hundred turns of heavy wire on a laminated iron core.
  - Breaker points, opened by a cam in the distributor, interrupt the current. The collapsing magnetic field induces about 20,000 volts in the secondary winding, which has thousands of turns of fine wire.
  - A condenser prevents the points from arcing.
  - A rotor directs the spark to each plug wire in turn.
  - Spinning flyweights advance the timing with rpm. A vacuum diaphragm advances it under light load.

**Most capable (no processor):** Dual-point distributors for higher rpm, then transistorized and capacitive-discharge ignition triggered by a magnetic pickup. These replace the points with discrete electronic switching but involve no computing.

**Spark plugs:** A steel shell with a mica insulator early on. Later insulators are fired ceramic (sillimanite, then high-alumina). Electrodes are nickel alloy, with platinum-tipped plugs at the top end.

## 6. Lubrication

**Simplest:** Total-loss drip oilers (glass cups dripping oil onto bearings at a set rate) and screw-down grease cups. The oil is burned or dripped away.

**Intermediate:** Splash lubrication. Scoops on the connecting rods dip into oil troughs and fling oil around the crankcase (Model T).

**Most capable:**
- **Full-pressure systems:** A gear pump with cut gears in a cast housing forces oil through drilled galleries and through passages in the crankshaft to every bearing. A full-flow filter and an oil cooler complete the circuit.
- **Dry sump (racing):** Scavenge pumps empty the crankcase into a separate tank, so oil never starves in hard cornering.

---

## 7. Cooling

**Simplest:**
- **Air cooling:** Cast fins on the cylinders.
- **Hopper cooling:** A water tank on the cylinder boils off and gets refilled.

**Intermediate:**
- **Thermosiphon (Model T):** Hot water rises naturally to the radiator and cool water sinks back, with no pump.
- **Pumped systems:** A belt-driven centrifugal water pump and a fan move coolant through a soldered brass and copper radiator, first honeycomb, later tube-and-fin.

**Most capable:**
- **Pressurization:** A pressure cap raises the boiling point.
- **Thermostat:** A wax-pellet thermostat opens as the wax melts and expands.
- **Coolant:** Ethylene glycol antifreeze.
- **Fan control:** A viscous fan clutch uses silicone fluid and a bimetal coil to engage the fan only when hot.
- **Radiator:** Crossflow radiators.
- **Advanced air cooling:** Ducted shrouds and a powerful fan blow over finned aluminum cylinders (Porsche).

---

## 8. Exhaust

**Simplest:** An open pipe, or a basic can muffler.

**Intermediate:** A cast-iron exhaust manifold, steel pipe and a baffled muffler that reflects sound waves to cancel them.

**Most capable:**
- **Tuned headers:** Equal-length tubes are bent and welded so exhaust pulses help pull the next charge out of the cylinder (scavenging).
- **Other refinements:** Dual exhaust systems, chambered or glass-packed mufflers and aluminized steel for corrosion life.
- **Early catalytic converters (1975):** Oxidation-only units with no electronic control.

---

## 9. Starting System

**Simplest:** A hand crank at the front of the engine. Backfire kickback broke many wrists and arms.

**Intermediate:** The electric starter (Cadillac, 1912). A DC motor with a Bendix drive throws a small pinion gear into the flywheel ring gear by inertia, then retracts it when the engine fires.

**Most capable:** Solenoid-actuated pre-engaged starters, and gear-reduction starters that are smaller with more torque.

---

## 10. Charging and Electrical

**Simplest:** No electrical system. A magneto handles ignition, and lights burn kerosene.

**Intermediate:**
- **Generator:** A DC generator (dynamo) uses brushes riding on a copper commutator.
- **Regulation:** A vibrating-contact voltage regulator and cutout relay manage it mechanically, using electromagnets that pull contacts open and closed.
- **Battery:** A 6-volt lead-acid battery in a hard-rubber case, with lead grid plates pasted with lead oxide.

**Most capable:**
- **12-volt systems:** Standard from the mid-1950s.
- **Alternator (Chrysler, 1960):** A spinning electromagnet inside fixed windings, with silicon diodes converting AC to DC. It charges even at idle.
- **Wiring:** Color-coded harnesses with PVC insulation, protected by fuses.

**Lighting progression:**
1. Oil and kerosene lamps.
2. Acetylene: water drips onto calcium carbide to make gas.
3. Incandescent bulbs.
4. Sealed-beam headlamps (1940).
5. Halogen lamps (1960s).

---

## 11. Clutch

**Simplest:** No true clutch. A drive belt is loosened to slip, or shifted from a fixed pulley to a free-spinning one.

**Intermediate:**
- **Cone clutch:** A leather-faced cone.
- **Wet multi-plate clutch:** Runs in oil (Model T).
- **Single dry plate:** A steel disc with riveted asbestos-based friction facings, clamped by coil springs.

**Most capable:**
- **Diaphragm spring:** A pressure plate using a stamped spring-steel cone, with lighter pedal effort and even clamping.
- **Racing clutches:** Twin-plate designs with sintered metallic or ceramic facings.
- **Actuation:** Hydraulic operation.

---

## 12. Transmission

**Simplest:** Belts and pulleys (Benz 1885), with chain drive to the rear wheels.

**Intermediate:**
- **Planetary (Model T):** Two forward speeds and reverse, selected by foot pedals tightening bands around drums.
- **Sliding-gear "crash box" (Panhard, 1890s):** Gears slide into mesh, and drivers must double-clutch to match speeds.
- **Constant mesh:** Gears stay meshed and are locked to the shaft by dog clutches.
- **Synchromesh (Cadillac, 1928):** Bronze cone rings use friction to match gear speeds before the dogs engage.

**Most capable, manual:** Fully synchronized four- and five-speed boxes with quiet helical gears, plus close-ratio racing dog boxes.

**Most capable, automatic (fully hydraulic, no computer):**
- **Hydra-Matic (1940):** A fluid coupling with planetary gearsets shifted by oil pressure.
- **Torque-converter automatics:**
  - **Torque converter:** An impeller, turbine and stator multiply torque through fluid.
  - **Valve body:** A maze of precision spool valves in machined aluminum acts as a hydraulic logic system.
  - **Shift inputs:** A centrifugal governor reports road speed, and a vacuum modulator or throttle cable reports engine load. The valve body uses both to decide shift points.

**Manufacture:**
- **Gears:** Cut on hobbing and gear-shaping machines, carburized, then shaved or ground.
- **Cases:** Cast iron, later die-cast aluminum.

---

## 13. Driveline

**Simplest:** Roller chain and sprockets from a cross-shaft to the rear wheels.

**Intermediate:**
- **Torque tube:** A tube encloses the driveshaft and locates the axle.
- **Hotchkiss open driveshaft:** Two Cardan universal joints running on needle bearings.

**Most capable:** Constant-velocity joints (the Rzeppa ball-type joint, 1920s) make front-wheel drive practical. Transaxles combine the gearbox and differential in one unit.

**Driveshaft manufacture:** Seamless steel tube, with welded yokes, dynamically balanced.

---

## 14. Differential and Axles

### Differential

The differential lets the outer wheel turn faster than the inner wheel in a turn.

**Simplest:** One driven wheel, or a solid axle where the tires scrub in turns.

**Intermediate:** An open bevel-gear differential with straight-cut ring and pinion gears, later spiral bevel.

**Most capable:**
- **Hypoid gears:** Offset gears lower the driveshaft and are very strong. They need extreme-pressure gear oil. Cut on Gleason gear generators and lapped together as matched pairs.
- **Limited-slip:** Clutch-pack units such as Positraction (1957).
- **Lockers:** Fully locking differentials.

### Axles

**Simplest and intermediate:** A solid beam axle in a "banjo" housing of pressed steel halves welded together. Axles are semi-floating or full-floating (heavy duty).

**Most capable:** Independent rear suspension (swing axle, double wishbone) and the De Dion tube.

---

## 15. Suspension

**Simplest:** Full-elliptic leaf springs from carriage practice. Leaves are strips of spring steel in graduated lengths, curved, heat-treated (quenched and tempered) and clamped together.

**Intermediate:**
- **Springs and axles:** Semi-elliptic leaf springs front and rear, with a forged I-beam front axle.
- **Friction dampers:** Scissor-type shock absorbers that rub discs together.

**Most capable:**
- **Independent front suspension (GM, 1934):** Double wishbones with coil springs.
- **MacPherson strut:** Introduced in the early 1950s.
- **Other springing and control:** Torsion bars and anti-roll bars.
- **Hydraulic dampers:** Telescopic units force oil through valved orifices.
- **Citroën hydropneumatic (DS, 1955):** Nitrogen-filled spheres act as springs. Mechanical height-corrector valves level the car automatically with no electronics. Deep dive: `AirHydraulicSuspension.md`.
- **Coil spring manufacture:** Wound hot or cold on a mandrel from spring-steel wire, heat-treated and shot-peened.

## 16. Steering

**Simplest:** A tiller (early Benz, Oldsmobile Curved Dash), or a center-pivot axle like a wagon.

**Intermediate:**
- **Steering box:** A steering wheel drives a worm-and-sector gearbox.
- **Ackermann geometry:** Angled steering arms make the inner wheel turn sharper than the outer. Each wheel pivots on a kingpin and the two are linked by a tie rod.

**Most capable:**
- **Recirculating ball:** Steel balls roll between the worm and nut to cut friction.
- **Rack and pinion:** Precise and light.
- **Hydraulic power steering (Chrysler, 1951):** An engine-driven vane pump supplies pressure. A rotary valve senses the twist of a small torsion bar to apply assist.
- **Collapsible column:** Introduced in 1967.

---

## 17. Brakes

**Simplest:** A wooden or leather-faced block levered against the tire (spoon brake), or a band tightened around a drum.

**Intermediate:** Internal-expanding drum brakes, first on the rear only, then on all four wheels. They are operated by rods and cables, with shoes lined with woven asbestos.

**Most capable:**
- **Hydraulic brakes (Duesenberg, 1921):** A master cylinder pushes fluid to wheel cylinders, equalizing force automatically.
- **Dual-circuit master cylinders:** Mandatory in the US from 1967.
- **Vacuum booster:** Uses intake manifold vacuum to multiply pedal force.
- **Disc brakes (Jaguar at Le Mans, 1953):** Calipers squeeze pads against cast-iron rotors. Vented rotors followed.
- **Proportioning valve:** Balances front and rear braking.
- **Mechanical anti-lock (Dunlop Maxaret, Jensen FF, 1966):** A flywheel senses wheel deceleration and releases pressure.

**Manufacture:**
- **Drums and rotors:** Gray iron, cast and machined.
- **Shoes:** Stamped steel with bonded or riveted linings.
- **Cylinders:** Cast iron, bored and honed, with molded rubber cups.

Caldris brake jobs: `Brakes.md`.

## 18. Wheels

**Simplest:** Wooden artillery wheels made by a wheelwright: hub, spokes and felloe (rim segments). An iron tire is heated to expand, fitted, then quenched so it shrinks tight.

**Intermediate:**
- **Wire-spoke wheels:** Tensioned steel spokes laced to a hub and trued by adjusting spoke tension.
- **Disc wheels:** Pressed steel, stamped and welded.

**Most capable:** Cast and forged magnesium or aluminum alloy wheels, with center-lock knock-off hubs for racing.

Caldris wheel ladder: `Wheels.md`.

---

## 19. Tires

**Simplest:** Solid rubber.

**Intermediate:**
- **Pneumatic tires (Michelin, 1895):** Clincher-bead tires with an inner tube.
- **Casing and rubber:** A bias-ply casing of cotton fabric layers. Carbon black is added to the rubber for far better wear.
- **Vulcanizing:** Tires are cured with sulfur in heated steel molds.

**Most capable:**
- **Stronger cords:** Rayon and nylon.
- **Tubeless tires:** Standard by the mid-1950s.
- **Steel-belted radials (Michelin X, late 1940s):** Better grip, wear and fuel economy.
- **Racing slicks:** Soft-compound, low-profile tires.

---

## 20. Instruments

**Simplest:** None.

**Intermediate:**
- **Speedometer:** A flexible cable from the transmission spins a magnet inside an aluminum cup. Eddy currents drag the cup against a hairspring to move the needle.
- **Odometer:** Numbered drums turned by gears.
- **Oil pressure gauge:** A Bourdon tube, a curled tube that straightens under pressure.
- **Temperature gauge:** A sealed capillary tube filled with vapor.
- **Fuel gauge:** A float moves a rheostat, read by a bimetal gauge.
- **Ammeter:** Shows charging current.

**Most capable:** Mechanical tachometers, warning lights and full gauge clusters.

---

## 21. Glass

Progression:
1. No windshield.
2. Plate glass, which shatters into knives.
3. Laminated safety glass: a plastic layer (later PVB) bonded between two glass sheets.
4. Tempered side glass, heated and air-quenched so it breaks into small cubes.
5. Float glass (Pilkington, 1959): molten glass floated on molten tin for perfectly flat sheets, then sag-bent over molds into curves.

---

## 22. Interior and Safety

**Seats:** Horsehair padding over coil springs, then foam rubber.

**Safety equipment:** Lap belts in the 1950s, the three-point belt (Volvo, 1959), padded dashboards and energy-absorbing steering columns.
