## Ship types by era

| Era | Type | Size | Crew | Rig | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 800 to 1100 | Longship | 20 to 30 m | 30 to 70 | One square sail plus oars | Fast, shallow, open deck. Raids and coastal runs |
| 1200 to 1400 | **Cog** | 20 to 30 m, up to about 200 t | 10 to 20 | One square sail, stern rudder | Hanseatic cargo carrier. Raised castles fore and aft |
| 1200 to 1600 | Galley | 40 m and up | Hundreds of rowers | Oars plus lateen | Mediterranean war and fast cargo. Moves without wind |
| 1400 to 1600 | **Caravel** | 15 to 25 m, 50 to 150 t | about 20 | Lateen, later mixed | Points high into the wind. Exploration |
| 1400 to 1600 | **Carrack** | 30 m and up, 200 to 1,000+ t | 50 to 100+ | Three to four masts, mixed | First true ocean cargo ship. Columbus's flagship |
| 1550 to 1700 | **Galleon** | 300 to 1,000+ t | 100 to 300+ | Square and lateen | Armed cargo and war. Treasure fleets |
| 1590s to 1700s | **Fluyt** | 200 to 500 t | About a third of rival crews | Square | Dutch bulk carrier. Cheaper to build. Big hold. Ran Europe's trade |
| 1600s to 1800s | East Indiaman | 500 to 1,400 t | 100 to 150 | Full rig | Armed merchant for long hauls |
| 1650 to 1850 | Ship of the line | 1,000 to 3,500 t | 500 to 850 | Full rig | 60 to 120 guns |
| 1700s to 1800s | Brig and schooner | 50 to 300 t | 5 to 15 | Two masts. Schooner fore-and-aft | Coastal and island trade. Small crews |
| 1818 on | Packet ship | 400 to 1,000 t | 30 to 40 | Full rig | Scheduled Atlantic service |
| 1840s to 1860s | **Clipper** | 1,000 to 2,000 t | 40 to 60 | Huge sail area, narrow hull | Fastest sail. Tea and gold rush runs |
| Ancient to now | **Polynesian double hull** (waʻa kaulua) | 15 to 20 m | 10 to 20 | Two hulls, crab-claw or sprit sails | Settled the Pacific without instruments |

## Speed

```
Hull speed (knots) ≈ 1.34 × √(waterline length in feet)
1 knot = 1.852 km/h
```

| Waterline | Hull speed |
|---|---|
| 10 m | about 7.7 kn |
| 20 m | about 10.9 kn |
| 30 m | about 13.3 kn |
| 40 m | about 15.4 kn |

**What ships really averaged:**

- Ship logs from 1750 to 1830 give about **4.5 to 6 knots** in a moderate breeze. Copper sheathing in the 1780s gave a jump (cleaner hulls).
- Packet ships New York to Liverpool: **21 to 29 days**.
- Clippers: **14 to 17 knots** at their best. The fastest logged about 22 knots.
- Medieval cogs and carracks: assume **3 to 5 knots** average. Records are thin.
- Hōkūleʻa in 1976 sailed Maui to Tahiti, about 2,600 miles, in about 31 to 34 days with no instruments.

**Passage time:**

| Average speed | 500 nautical miles | 2,600 nautical miles |
|---|---|---|
| 3 kn | about 7 days | about 36 days |
| 4 kn | about 5 days | about 27 days |
| 5 kn | about 4 days | about 22 days |
| 6 kn | about 3.5 days | about 18 days |
| 8 kn | about 2.6 days | about 13.5 days |

Add days for storms, calms, beating upwind and port stops.

## Sailing against the wind

- **Square rig** points about 60 to 70° off the wind at best. Fast downwind. Poor upwind.
- **Fore-and-aft rig** (lateen, schooner) points about 40 to 45°. Better upwind. Needs fewer hands.
- **Tacking.** Zigzag upwind. A ship heading straight into the wind covers maybe a third to half the distance it sails.
- **Trade winds** blow steady all year in the tropics. Routes follow them. Island east sides are wet, west sides dry (`../../Geography/VolcanicIsland/Weather.md`).
- **Doldrums.** Calm belts where ships sit for days.

## Navigation

| Era | Tool | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| 1100s | Magnetic compass | Heading in any weather |
| 1270s on | Portolan charts | Coast lines and bearings between ports |
| 1400s | Mariner's astrolabe, quadrant | Latitude from sun or Pole Star |
| 1500s | Log line with knots, half-minute glass | Speed through the water |
| 1569 | Mercator projection | Straight compass lines on a chart |
| 1594 | Backstaff | Sun height without staring at it |
| 1731 | Octant | Accurate latitude at sea |
| 1757 | Sextant | Better latitude and lunar distances |
| 1761 | Harrison's H4 chronometer | Longitude from time |
| Always | Lead line | Depth and seabed sample |
| Polynesian | Star compass | 32 star houses, 11.25° each. Swells, clouds, birds and currents |

**Dead reckoning.** Heading plus speed plus time. Error grows each day. Latitude was easy by the 1400s. Longitude stayed guesswork until the chronometer.

## Life aboard

### Rations (Royal Navy, 1700s)

| Daily | Weekly |
|---|---|
| About 1 lb (450 g) biscuit | 4 lb beef, 2 lb pork |
| About 1 gallon of weak beer | 2 pints peas, 2 pints oatmeal |
| | 6 oz butter, 12 oz cheese |

- Salt meat kept for years in casks. Some came out so hard it could be carved like wood.
- **Scurvy** killed more sailors than battle on long voyages. In 1795 the Admiralty ordered ¾ ounce of lemon juice a day. Scurvy still lingered for decades.
- **Water** went foul in casks. Beer and wine kept better. Design figure: about 4 L per person per day. Sixty crew for thirty days need about 7,200 L.
- A sailor fed hot food daily with meat four days a week ate better than many people ashore.

### Hazards

| Hazard | Note | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Storm | Waves and wind break masts and open seams | Heave to, run before it under bare poles, or ride to a sea anchor |
| Lee shore | Wind blowing the ship onto rocks | The killer. Stay far off |
| Reefs and shoals | Worst near islands | Lead line, lookouts, daylight entry |
| Fire | Wood, tar, rope and powder | Strict rules for flame and lamps |
| Calm | Days without wind | Rowing boats tow. Rations stretch |
| Disease | Scurvy, typhus, dysentery | Fresh food, citrus, clean water, ventilation |
| Piracy | Near trade routes and straits | Convoy, guns, speed |
| Seasickness | Most adapt in two to three days | Look at the horizon, stay on deck, eat lightly |

## What magic changes

### Mana as an engine

Rough power to push a hull (Admiralty estimate, average hull):

| Ship | Speed | Power | Raw MP/s | Raw MP per 10 h |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 t fishing boat | 5 kn | about 3 kW | about 280 | about 10 million |
| 100 t coaster | 6 kn | about 14 kW | about 1,400 | about 50 million |
| 300 t brig | 7 kn | about 46 kW | about 4,600 | about 165 million |
| 1,200 t Indiaman | 7 kn | about 116 kW | about 11,600 | about 420 million |
| 2,000 t clipper | 14 kn | about 1.3 MW | about 130,000 | about 4.7 billion |

- **No caster pushes a ship for a voyage.** Even at about 720 J per paid mana (`../Energy/ManaHandPhysics.md` §2) the coaster costs about 19 MP/s. That buys minutes in a calm or a push off a lee shore, not days.
- **Stones and runes** are the engine for mana ships. A heat rune and a boiler gives a rune steamer (`../Energy/SteamEngines.md`).
- **Wind is free.** A 300 m² sail in an 8 m/s wind pulls on the order of 10 kN. Mana that shapes or steadies the wind on the sails beats mana that pushes the hull.
- **Cutting drag** is cheaper than adding thrust. A thin film along the hull lowers skin friction (`../Energy/Shear.md`).

### Other magic uses at sea

| Use | Note |
|---|---|
| Fresh water from sea | About 380 MP per liter raw at the physical minimum (`../Energy/EnergyPumping.md`). Expensive for a whole crew. Fine for a few people |
| Cold storage | Heat pumping keeps meat and fruit fresh and beats scurvy (`../Energy/EnergyPumping.md`) |
| Calm water around the hull | Damping costs the energy removed. Small patches only |
| Bilge pumping | Lift costs are small next to propulsion (`../Energy/EnergyPumping.md`) |
| Echolocation and depth | Sound in water reaches far (`../Energy/Sound.md`). Reefs and monsters below |
| Storm warning | Infrasound from distant storms (`../Energy/Infrasound.md`) |
| Gyro steadiness | A spinning disc steadies a bunk or a workbench (`../Energy/Spin.md`) |
| Ship cannons | Magic cannons already exist on Caldris ships |
