# Vehicle Wheels

Earth tech ladder for vehicle wheels. Each entry lists the era and the tools, materials and processes that must exist first. Use for invent beats and for reading Caldris ceiling. Do not paste Earth brand names into prose unless the story invents them.

**Accuracy:** solid popular engineering-history summary. Dates and “firsts” are order-of-magnitude, good enough for prerequisite checks.

**Caldris readout** (`../../Tech/Technology.md`): craftsman economy with early-industrial magitech (mana-stone trains, smith guilds, no mass manufacturing). Typical carts and coaches: **iron-tired wooden wheels**, wheelwright craft, hub liners if the shop is good. Rail: flanged metal wheels within early industrial reach (cast / wrought / early steel via forge + magic heat). **Not** baseline: vulcanized pneumatic tires, pressed mass-production discs, carbon fiber, electronics, mecanum / omni / spherical drives. Those wait on Roland invent arcs or rare prestige builds.

Companion: `Suspension.md`.

## Precursors and Antiquity

**1. Log rollers**
- Pros: no manufacturing beyond cutting logs; move heavy loads with no axle.
- Cons: logs must be moved from rear to front continuously; no steering.
- Tech level: Stone Age (before 5000 BC). Stone axes and human or animal muscle.

**2. Solid single-piece disc** (cross-section of a trunk)
- Pros: simplest true wheel; carries heavy loads.
- Cons: needs a very large trunk; splits along the grain; heavy.
- Tech level: Late Neolithic (c. 4000 BC). Polished stone or copper axes and adzes; the axle-and-hole fit needs a working concept of rotating joints.

**3. Tripartite plank wheel** (Sumerian and Mesopotamian carts; Ljubljana Marshes wheel)
- Pros: built from planks with no giant trunk; strong when cross-braced with cleats or battens.
- Cons: very heavy; poor traction on soft ground; wears unevenly.
- Tech level: Copper Age (c. 3500 BC). Copper saws chisels and drills for plank dressing and dowel joinery.

**4. Cutout and lunate plank wheel**
- Pros: lighter than the solid version.
- Cons: weaker; cutouts concentrate stress.
- Tech level: Copper to Early Bronze Age (c. 3000 BC). Same as plank wheels plus bronze tools for clean interior cuts.

**5. Spoked wooden wheel** (Sintashta chariots; Egyptian and Hittite chariots)
- Pros: far lighter; allows high speed; strong when built well.
- Cons: requires precise joinery; spokes loosen with dry-out; hard to repair without a wheelwright.
- Tech level: Middle Bronze Age (c. 2000 BC). Bronze chisels drills and drawknives; a turned hub; seasoned hardwoods; specialist wheelwright guild knowledge.

**6. Steam-bent felloe wheel**
- Pros: continuous strong rim; better fatigue life.
- Cons: bending needs skill and correct timber species; joints can open.
- Tech level: Late Bronze Age (c. 1500 BC). Steam or fire bending of green wood plus bronze woodworking tools.

**7. Iron-tired wooden wheel** (Celtic and Roman wagons)
- Pros: protects the rim; holds spokes in compression; extends wheel life.
- Cons: heavy; hoop must be sized exactly; hoops loosen or shrink with weather.
- Tech level: Iron Age (c. 500 BC). Bloomery iron smelting; forge welding; heat-shrink fitting of hoops.

**8. Dished wheel** (spokes angled outward from the hub)
- Pros: resists lateral loads on rough roads; sheds mud.
- Cons: complex hub and spoke geometry; needs matching axle taper.
- Tech level: Iron Age to Classical (c. 500 BC to 100 AD). Iron tools for precise mortise angles; established wheelwright craft tradition.

**9. Bronze and iron hub liners and nave hoops** (Roman)
- Pros: reduces hub wear; allows heavy loads.
- Cons: needs lubrication with tallow or oil.
- Tech level: Classical Era (c. 100 BC to 300 AD). Bronze casting; iron smithing; animal-fat lubricants.

**10. Wheelbarrow wheel** (Chinese)
- Pros: one large wheel gives leverage and carrying capacity; simple.
- Cons: tips; balance depends on the operator.
- Tech level: Classical Era (c. 100 AD). Iron tools and basic carpentry.

## Pre-Industrial to Early Industrial

**11. Wooden railway wagonway wheel**
- Pros: cheap; suits wooden rails.
- Cons: wears rapidly; low load limit.
- Tech level: Early Modern (c. 1550 to 1700). Carpentry and simple ironwork.

**12. Cast iron flanged wheel**
- Pros: durable; resists rail wear; flange keeps the vehicle on track.
- Cons: brittle; cracks under shock; heavy.
- Tech level: Early Industrial (c. 1760 to 1800). Coke-fired blast furnaces; sand-mold foundry casting.

**13. Wrought iron spoked wheel** (locomotive and carriage)
- Pros: tougher than cast iron; repairable by forging.
- Cons: forge welds can fail; labor intensive.
- Tech level: Early Industrial (c. 1800 to 1850). Puddling process for wrought iron; large steam hammers; skilled forge welding.

**14. Tension-spoked wheel** (Cayley's 1808 design)
- Pros: extremely light for its strength; hub hangs from the upper spokes.
- Cons: needs high-tensile wire or rod; each spoke must be tuned.
- Tech level: Early Industrial (c. 1808 concept; practical c. 1870). Wire drawing of consistent iron or steel; threaded nipples.

## Late 19th Century

**15. Wire-spoked bicycle wheel** (Starley, 1870)
- Pros: light; strong; simple repairs; tunable by tightening spokes.
- Cons: spokes loosen; building requires skill; rims dent.
- Tech level: Second Industrial Revolution (c. 1870). Drawn steel wire; precision-threaded nipples; rolled rims.

**16. Solid rubber tire wheel**
- Pros: no puncture risk; low maintenance.
- Cons: harsh ride; heavy; poor traction.
- Tech level: Mid 19th Century (c. 1845 to 1870). Vulcanized rubber (Goodyear 1839); rubber-to-steel band bonding.

**17. Pneumatic tire wheel** (Dunlop, 1888)
- Pros: comfortable; grips well; light unsprung mass.
- Cons: punctures; needs pressure maintenance.
- Tech level: Late 19th Century (c. 1888). Vulcanization; rubberized fabric; air-tight valves; later cord-ply tire construction.

**18. Artillery wheel** (heavy wooden car and gun-carriage wheel)
- Pros: strong; economical at early car scale.
- Cons: heavy; rots; weak in wet conditions.
- Tech level: Late 19th Century (c. 1880 to 1920). Industrial wheelwrighting; steel tire bands; machine-cut spokes.

**19. Steel railway wheel** (rolled tire on a center)
- Pros: standard for heavy rail; wear-resistant; can be re-profiled.
- Cons: tire loosening; cracking; heavy.
- Tech level: Mid 19th Century (c. 1852). Crucible then Bessemer steel; large forging and rolling mills (Krupp seamless tire).

**20. Monobloc rolled steel railway wheel** (modern standard)
- Pros: no loose tire; consistent quality; robust.
- Cons: entire wheel replaced when worn out; residual stress issues.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1900 to 1930). Open-hearth steelmaking; heavy hydraulic presses and wheel mills; controlled heat treatment.

**21. Resilient railway wheel** (rubber between hub and tire; used on trams)
- Pros: quieter; damped; reduces track damage.
- Cons: rubber ages; more parts.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1920 to 1940). Rubber-metal bonding; precision-machined steel components.

## Early 20th Century Automotive

**22. Wire wheel with knock-off hub** (Rudge-Whitworth)
- Pros: light; fast tire changes; strong for the era; classic look.
- Cons: spokes need cleaning and truing; not suited to tubeless tires.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1908). Precision splined hubs; drawn steel wire; skilled hand or jig assembly.

**23. Pressed steel disc wheel**
- Pros: cheap; strong; mass produced; simple to service.
- Cons: heavy; rusts; poor cooling for brakes.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1920s). Sheet steel rolling; large stamping presses and dies; spot and arc welding.

**24. Split-rim and multi-piece truck wheel** (lock ring type)
- Pros: allows changing large truck tires without a specialized machine.
- Cons: dangerous during inflation if assembled incorrectly; lock rings can fail.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1910s to 1920s). Roll-formed steel sections; machined lock rings.

**25. Drop-center rim**
- Pros: allows a one-piece rim with easy tire mounting; became universal.
- Cons: bead can unseat under sideloads.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1920s). Roll forming and flash-butt welding of steel strip.

**26. Demountable rim**
- Pros: quick tire swap.
- Cons: extra weight and complexity.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1910s). Stamped steel and clamp hardware.

## Modern Materials

**27. Multi-piece wheel** (bolted or welded barrels and center)
- Pros: custom widths and offsets; repairable.
- Cons: seal leaks; assembly torque critical; heavy.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1960s to 1970s). Precision machining; sealants; torque-controlled fastening.

**28. Carbon fiber wheel**
- Pros: lowest rotating mass; stiff; aerodynamic shapes.
- Cons: very expensive; brittle in impact; heat sensitive.
- Tech level: Late 20th to Early 21st Century (c. 1990s to 2010s). Carbon fiber production; resin systems; autoclave or molded curing; nondestructive testing.

**29. Composite polymer wheel** (small vehicles and casters)
- Pros: light; corrosion free; cheap when injection molded.
- Cons: limited load and heat; creep.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1950s to 1970s). Petrochemical plastics; injection molding machinery.

**30. Aerodynamic disc wheel** (cycling)
- Pros: lowest drag; simple shape.
- Cons: crosswind handling; heavy in some builds.
- Tech level: Late 20th Century (c. 1980s). Composite layup or bonded shells; wind tunnel testing.

## Specialized and Unconventional

**31. Track roadwheel and sprocket system** (tanks and tractors)
- Pros: spreads load; excellent off-road traction.
- Cons: heavy; noisy; wear intensive.
- Tech level: Early 20th Century (c. 1900 to 1918). Alloy steel casting and forging; manganese steel track links; heavy machining.

**32. Cage or lugged steel wheel** (early tractors)
- Pros: strong traction in mud; simple to make.
- Cons: destroys paved roads; harsh ride.
- Tech level: Late 19th Century (c. 1890s to 1920s). Steel plate rolling and riveting or welding.

**33. Run-flat and solid non-pneumatic wheel**
- Pros: no flats; suits military and industrial use.
- Cons: harsh; heavy; heat buildup.
- Tech level: Mid to Late 20th Century (c. 1940s to 1990s). Synthetic rubber compounding; molded polyurethane or reinforced sidewalls.

**34. Airless spoke wheel** (Michelin Tweel and similar)
- Pros: no punctures; tunable stiffness.
- Cons: noise; heat; limited speed.
- Tech level: Early 21st Century (c. 2005 onward). Advanced polymer engineering; finite element modeling; precision multi-material molding.

**35. Mecanum wheel**
- Pros: full omnidirectional motion with a fixed axle.
- Cons: low load capacity; rollers wear; poor on rough ground.
- Tech level: Late 20th Century (c. 1970s). Precision machined angled rollers; independent motor drive per wheel; electronic motion control.

**36. Omni wheel**
- Pros: sideways sliding plus forward drive; simpler than mecanum.
- Cons: vibration; small contact patches.
- Tech level: Mid 20th Century (c. 1950s to 1970s). Precision machined rollers; small motors and basic control.

**37. Spherical or ball wheel**
- Pros: any direction of motion; compact for balancing robots.
- Cons: complex drive; slip; limited load.
- Tech level: Early 21st Century (c. 2000s). Real-time sensor feedback; high-speed motor control; inertial sensing.

**38. Caster wheel**
- Pros: swivels freely; cheap.
- Cons: shimmy; limited speed.
- Tech level: Early Modern to Industrial (c. 1700s to 1800s). Metalworking with bearings or bushings; cheap mass production by 1900.

**39. Transformable wheel-leg**
- Pros: stair and obstacle climbing.
- Cons: mechanical complexity; low speed.
- Tech level: Early 21st Century (c. 2000s to 2020s). Servo actuators; embedded controllers; lightweight structural materials; sensor-driven control.

## Summary by Technological Level

- **Stone Age to Neolithic (before 3500 BC):** log rollers; solid disc.
- **Copper and Bronze Age (3500 to 1200 BC):** plank wheel; cutout wheel; spoked wooden wheel; steam-bent felloe wheel.
- **Iron Age and Classical (1200 BC to 500 AD):** iron-tired wheel; dished wheel; hub liners; wheelbarrow wheel.
- **Early Modern (1500 to 1750):** wooden wagonway wheel; caster wheel (early forms).
- **Early Industrial (1750 to 1850):** cast iron flanged wheel; wrought iron wheel; tension-spoked wheel (concept).
- **Second Industrial Revolution (1850 to 1914):** steel railway wheel; wire-spoked bicycle wheel; solid rubber tire wheel; pneumatic tire wheel; artillery wheel; cage wheel; early track systems.
- **Early 20th Century (1914 to 1945):** monobloc rail wheel; resilient rail wheel; wire wheel with knock-off hub; pressed steel disc; split rim; drop-center rim; demountable rim.
- **Mid 20th Century (1945 to 1990):** multi-piece wheel; composite polymer wheel; omni wheel; mecanum wheel; run-flat wheel; disc wheel.
- **Late 20th to 21st Century (1990 onward):** carbon fiber wheel; airless spoke wheel; spherical wheel; transformable wheel-leg.

Wheel history follows the same pattern as suspension: each major leap came from a new material or process, from joinery and hoops to wrought iron and steel and finally to composites.
