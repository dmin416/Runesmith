# Roads

How roads are built and what kills them. Suspension loads: `Suspension.md`. Era: `../../Tech/Technology.md`.

## Narrative

Every good road is a drainage project with a wearing surface on top. Water ruins the subgrade. State power and trade volume set how deep the stack gets.

## Detail

### Three layers

1. **Subgrade** – native ground  
2. **Base** – spreads wheel load  
3. **Surface** – takes wear and sheds water  

Saturated subgrade ruts and frost-heaves. Solve drainage before arguing pavement.

### Build ladder

| Type | Build | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Dirt track | Clear + traffic compact | Dust / mud |
| Corduroy / fascine | Logs or brush over bog | Rough; rots ~10–20 years (logs) |
| Plank road | Sawn plank on stringers | Smooth then rot ~5–8 years |
| Stone causeway | Raised rubble | Labor heavy, durable |
| Rammed earth | Compacted fill | Qin/Han style |
| Roman stack | Raised *agger*; large stone → rubble/lime → fine → optional slabs | Often ≥1 m deep; 4–6 m wide; crown + ditches |
| Inca-style | Drystone, steps, rope bridges | Foot / pack beast, not wheels |
| Macadam | Thin layers of small **angular** stone, cambered | Cheap; dust under fast rubber → needs binder |
| Tarmac / asphalt | Tar or bitumen + stone | Flexible |
| Concrete slab | Rigid; needs joints | Long life, costly |
| Chip seal | Spray binder + chips | Cheap rural |

**McAdam rule:** angular stones lock. Round stones roll apart. Keep earth and rounded gravel out of the structural layers.

City surfaces: cobbles (cheap, rough), setts (grip, noisy under iron tires), wood block (quiet, slippery/manure), brick, natural asphalt rock.

### Supporting works

Culverts, side ditches, fords, retaining walls, switchbacks. Draft-animal grades under about **5–8%**. Bridges: log → stone arch → timber truss → iron. Tolls fund builds.

### Caldris filter

Iron-tired wheels destroy soft tops. Wide tires or rubber (when invented) change what surfaces survive. Local stone, lime, wood and natural bitumen decide what a kingdom can afford. Army roads and coach/mail roads are different budgets. Magitech rollers and binders are invent, not baseline.

Street baseline is medieval social structure with magitech long-haul (rail / airships). Most overland roads stay dirt or stone-patched; engineered macadam / county-tax roads are invent or prestige. Monster density makes solo travel dangerous; caravans are normal (`../../Tech/Technology.md`).

## Earth era ladder (institutions and speeds)

Anchor for invent bands. Named Earth laws and routes are examples only.

### Medieval (c. 500 to 1500)

**Legal concept over physical road.** A "highway" was mostly a right of passage, not a built structure. If the track became impassable, travelers had the legal right to detour across neighboring fields.

**Surfaces**

- Mostly packed earth with no foundation or drainage. Ruts, potholes and standing water were constant.
- Winter and spring turned roads into deep mud that could stop carts entirely. Summer brought dust and hardened ruts that broke wheels and axles.
- Surviving Roman roads were still used. They deteriorated over centuries because locals took the stone for building.

**Maintenance**

- No central authority handled it. Manors, monasteries and town councils did patchwork repairs when they chose to.
- Bridges were often built by the Church or guilds and funded through charitable bequests or toll grants.
- Some kingdoms required brush cleared far back from market-town roads to deny cover to robbers (England's Statute of Winchester, 1285: 200 feet each side).

**Towns:** Major cities began paving key streets with stone (Paris under Philip Augustus around 1185). Most streets were dirt with a central gutter carrying sewage and refuse.

**Travel speeds**

- On foot: 15 to 20 miles per day
- Horseback: 20 to 30 miles per day; royal messengers with fresh horses could reach 40 to 50
- Ox or horse carts: 8 to 15 miles per day
- Rivers and coastal shipping were preferred for heavy goods. Packhorses often beat wagons overland.

**Hazards:** Bandits, flooded fords, no signposts, frequent tolls and a lack of inns in remote areas.

### Renaissance (c. 1400 to 1650)

**Little physical improvement.** Trade grew faster than roads did. More wheeled traffic wore roads down worse than in medieval times.

**Surfaces:** Largely the same dirt tracks. Heavier four-wheeled wagons and the new coach cut deeper ruts. Some main routes got gravel or stone patches near towns.

**Administration begins**

- Local forced labor under unpaid surveyors (England Highways Act 1555: each parish; householders owed days of unpaid work). Readiness was poor because nobody wanted the job.
- First national road officials appear (France: Sully as *grand voyer*, 1599). Trees planted along royal roads for shade and route marks.
- Cities led in urban paving and street straightening.

**Vehicles:** The coach (from Hungarian Kocs) spread in the 1500s. Without spring suspension it was punishing; early models hung the body on leather straps (`Suspension.md`).

**Postal relays:** Fresh horses every 15 to 25 miles let couriers cover 100+ miles per day (exceptional). Earth example: Taxis network from 1490 across the Holy Roman Empire.

**Travel speeds:** Coaches made roughly 20 to 30 miles per day on decent routes, often far less in wet seasons.

### Victorian (1837 to 1901)

**Inherited a road revolution.** Engineering breakthroughs came just before Victoria. Then railways gutted long-distance road traffic.

**Engineered surfaces**

- **Macadam (John McAdam, 1810s to 1820s):** Layers of small, angular broken stone about 10 inches deep, compacted by traffic and cambered so water drained off. Cheap and effective. Standard for main roads.
- **Telford:** Heavy hand-laid stone foundation under a gravel surface. Stronger but more expensive.
- Macadam was dusty dry and muddy wet. Tar was sometimes added late in the century; true tarmac patented 1902.

**Turnpikes and collapse**

- Toll trusts funded maintenance; England peaked around 1830 with over 20,000 miles of turnpike.
- Railways after 1840 killed stagecoach traffic and toll revenue. Trusts wound down (last English trust 1895).
- Forced parish labor gave way to local highway tax, then main roads to county councils.

**Rural roads:** Away from main routes, country lanes stayed narrow dirt with deep winter mud.

**Urban streets**

- Granite setts (squared stones, often mistaken for cobbles) common in cities. Wood block paving cut noise in some districts.
- Compressed rock asphalt appeared in London around 1869.
- Horse manure was a massive urban problem. Crossing sweepers cleared paths for tips.

**Other:** Plank roads had a brief mid-1800s boom in America. Corduroy (logs crosswise) covered swamp. 1890s bicycle craze pushed Good Roads movements.

**Travel speeds**

- Mail coaches in the 1830s: about 10 mph including stops
- Private carriages on good roads: 8 to 12 mph
- Railways by mid-century: 30 to 50 mph, which made road travel a local affair again

### Quick comparison

| | Medieval | Renaissance | Victorian |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical surface | Dirt, ruts, mud | Dirt, deeper ruts | Macadam on main roads; dirt elsewhere |
| Who maintained | Landowners, Church, ad hoc | Parishes (forced labor) | Turnpike trusts, then county councils |
| Funding | Charity, tolls | Unpaid labor | Tolls, then local taxes |
| Long-distance option | Rivers, sea | Rivers, courier relays | Railways |
| Wagon / coach pace | 8 to 15 mi/day | 15 to 30 mi/day | 8 to 12 mph on good roads |
