## 1. The Core Principle: Water Is the Enemy

Nearly every trail failure traces back to water. A trail is a long, narrow strip of compacted, unvegetated ground cut across a landscape. Compacted soil absorbs very little water, so the tread collects rain and runoff. Collected water concentrates into channels. Concentrated water accelerates, gains energy, detaches soil particles and carries them away. The trail then deepens into a trench, which collects even more water.

Sustainable trail design has three goals:

1. **Get water off the tread frequently** before it gains volume and velocity.
2. **Keep the tread on stable mineral soil or rock** that resists detachment and holds its shape.
3. **Keep users on the tread** so the trail does not widen, braid or spawn shortcuts.

Everything else in this document (grades, outslope, gravel selection, rock armoring, planting) serves those three goals.

## 2. Soil Science Fundamentals

### 2.1 Soil Texture

Soil is classified by the size of its mineral particles.

| Particle | Size range | Trail behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Boulder / cobble | Over 75 mm (3 in) | Stable armor if embedded; rolls if loose |
| Gravel | 2 to 75 mm | Drains well; stability depends on shape and fines |
| Sand | 0.05 to 2 mm | Drains fast; no cohesion; loose and soft when dry; washboards and ravels |
| Silt | 0.002 to 0.05 mm | Highly erodible; muddy when wet, dusty when dry; very frost-susceptible |
| Clay | Under 0.002 mm | Cohesive and hard when dry; slick, plastic and rut-prone when wet; low permeability |

Most natural soils are mixtures. A **loam** contains a balance of sand, silt and clay. A **gravelly loam** with some rock content often makes the most durable native tread because the clay binds, the sand and gravel provide structure and the mix compacts tightly.

### 2.2 Why Silt Is the Worst Trail Soil

Erodibility is not simply "small particles erode more." The relationship is U-shaped:

- **Clay** particles are tiny but bond to each other through electrochemical attraction (cohesion). They resist detachment. Once dislodged, they stay suspended for a long time, which is why clay soils produce muddy brown runoff.
- **Coarse sand and gravel** are heavy enough that water needs high velocity to move them.
- **Silt and very fine sand** sit in the worst zone. They are light enough to move easily and too coarse to bond strongly. They detach at the lowest water velocities of any particle size.

The RUSLE soil erodibility factor (K) reflects this. In US customary units, clean sands and well-aggregated clays often fall around 0.05 to 0.25 while silt loams commonly run 0.40 to 0.60 or higher. A trail on silt loam can lose soil several times faster than the same trail on gravelly loam.

### 2.3 Soil Horizons and Why Organic Soil Fails

A typical forest soil profile:

| Horizon | Name | Content | Trail suitability |
|---|---|---|---|
| O | Litter and duff | Leaves, needles, decomposing organic matter | Unsuitable |
| A | Topsoil | Mineral soil mixed with organic matter | Poor |
| B | Subsoil | Mineral soil with accumulated clay and iron | Good to excellent |
| C | Parent material | Weathered rock | Excellent if not loose |
| R | Bedrock | Solid rock | Excellent |

Organic material fails as tread because it:

- Has very low bearing strength and compresses under load.
- Holds several times its own weight in water and stays wet.
- Decomposes over time, leaving voids that collapse.
- Turns into mud that churns and displaces under traffic.

Proper construction removes the O and usually the A horizon from the tread footprint, building the tread on B horizon mineral soil. The removed duff is cast well downslope (scattered, not piled) or saved for revegetating disturbed areas.

### 2.4 Shear Strength (Mohr-Coulomb)

Soil resists failure through shear strength, described by the Mohr-Coulomb equation:

```
τf = c' + σ' tan φ'
```

- **τf** = shear strength at failure
- **c'** = effective cohesion (bonding between particles, mostly from clay and roots)
- **σ'** = effective normal stress = total stress (σ) minus pore water pressure (u)
- **φ'** = effective internal friction angle (interlocking and friction between grains)

The key insight is the **pore water pressure term**. When soil saturates, water pressure between grains pushes them apart and reduces effective stress. Friction drops. This is why:

- Wet trails rut and deform while dry trails stay firm.
- Slopes fail during or just after heavy rain.
- Saturated fill slopes slump.

Typical friction angles:

| Material | Approximate φ' |
|---|---|
| Loose rounded sand | 28° to 32° |
| Dense angular sand | 35° to 45° |
| Rounded gravel | 32° to 36° |
| Crushed angular gravel | 40° to 48° |
| Silt | 26° to 32° |
| Clay (drained) | 18° to 28° |

Angular particles interlock. Rounded particles roll past each other. This single property explains most of the difference between good and bad trail gravel.

### 2.5 Compaction

Compaction squeezes air out of soil pores, increasing **bulk density**, strength and resistance to erosion while decreasing permeability.

**Optimum moisture content (Proctor principle):** Every soil has a moisture level at which it compacts to maximum density.

- Too dry: friction between particles prevents them from sliding into tight arrangement.
- Too wet: water fills the pores and is incompressible, so particles cannot pack together.
- Optimum: water lubricates particles enough to slide into place without filling the voids.

**Field test:** Squeeze a handful of soil. At optimum moisture it forms a ball that holds its shape, leaves no free water on the hand and crumbles when poked. If it oozes or smears, it is too wet. If it will not form a ball, it is too dry.

**Lift thickness:** Compact in layers (lifts) of 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm). Thicker lifts leave a loose zone at the bottom that later settles.

**The compaction trade-off:** A compacted tread is strong but nearly impermeable. Undisturbed forest soil can infiltrate 50 to 250+ mm of water per hour. Compacted trail tread may infiltrate 0 to 10 mm per hour. The tread becomes a runoff generator, which is why drainage features are mandatory rather than optional.

**Compaction off the tread is harmful.** Root growth is severely restricted when bulk density exceeds roughly 1.6 g/cm³ in clay soils or 1.8 g/cm³ in sandy soils. Trampling beside the trail kills vegetation, which then exposes soil to erosion.

### 2.6 Plasticity and Expansive Clays

**Atterberg limits** describe how fine-grained soil behaves with changing moisture:

- **Plastic limit:** moisture content where soil changes from crumbly to moldable.
- **Liquid limit:** moisture content where soil starts to flow.
- **Plasticity index (PI)** = liquid limit minus plastic limit.

High-PI clays turn greasy and deform under traffic when wet, then shrink and crack when dry. **Expansive clays** (smectite and montmorillonite minerals) swell dramatically when wet, heaving and cracking structures. Trails through these soils need extra drainage, armoring or aggregate surfacing.

## 3. Hydrology: How Water Moves on and Around Trails

### 3.1 Raindrop Impact (Splash Erosion)

Large raindrops reach terminal velocities around 9 m/s (20 mph). Each impact detaches soil particles and throws them up to 1 to 1.5 m. On bare soil, splash also:

- Breaks soil aggregates into fine particles.
- Clogs surface pores with those fines, forming a **surface crust** that sharply reduces infiltration.
- Supplies loose sediment for runoff to carry away.

Vegetation, leaf litter and duff absorb impact energy. Bare tread receives the full force.

### 3.2 Infiltration and Runoff

Runoff forms in two ways:

- **Infiltration-excess (Hortonian) flow:** rain falls faster than the surface can absorb it. Dominant on compacted trail tread.
- **Saturation-excess flow:** soil is already full of water and cannot absorb more. Dominant in valley bottoms, seeps and during long storms.

Once runoff starts, the tread behaves like a small paved channel.

### 3.3 Sheet Flow Versus Concentrated Flow

- **Sheet flow** is a thin, broad film of water. Depth is shallow, velocity is low and erosive force is small.
- **Concentrated flow** forms when water collects into a groove: a footpath rut, tire track, hoof print line or entrenched tread. Depth increases, velocity increases and erosive power rises sharply.

A well-designed trail keeps water in sheet flow and moves it off the tread sideways before it concentrates.

### 3.4 Velocity and Erosive Power

**Manning's equation** (SI units) gives the velocity of flowing water:

```
V = (1/n) × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2)
```

- **V** = velocity (m/s)
- **n** = Manning roughness coefficient (about 0.02 to 0.03 for smooth bare soil; 0.04 to 0.07 for rough rock or vegetation)
- **R** = hydraulic radius (cross-sectional flow area divided by wetted perimeter; roughly equal to depth in wide shallow flow)
- **S** = slope (as a decimal)

Velocity rises with both slope and depth. Doubling the depth of flow raises velocity by about 59 percent. Quadrupling the slope doubles the velocity.

**Bed shear stress** (the force water exerts on the soil surface):

```
τ = γ × R × S
```

- **γ** = unit weight of water (9,810 N/m³)

Soil erodes when τ exceeds the soil's critical shear stress.

**Why small changes in velocity matter so much:**

- Kinetic energy of water scales with **velocity squared**.
- Sediment transport capacity scales roughly with **velocity cubed to the fifth power**.
- **Airy's sixth-power law:** the maximum mass of a particle that flowing water can move scales with **velocity to the sixth power**. Doubling the velocity lets water move a stone 64 times heavier.

This is the physical basis for frequent drainage. Each drainage feature resets velocity to near zero before it can climb into the destructive range.

**The Hjulström curve** relates particle size to the velocity needed to erode, transport or deposit it:

- Fine sand and coarse silt (about 0.1 to 0.5 mm) erode at the lowest velocities, roughly 0.2 to 0.3 m/s.
- Clay needs higher velocity to erode because of cohesion but stays suspended at very low velocity.
- Gravel and cobbles need progressively higher velocity to move.
- Deposition happens where velocity drops: at the bottom of a grade, behind a water bar or in a flat spot. This is why drains fill with sediment.

### 3.5 Slope Length

The **Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE):**

```
A = R × K × LS × C × P
```

| Factor | Meaning | Trail relevance |
|---|---|---|
| A | Average annual soil loss | The outcome |
| R | Rainfall erosivity | Climate; fixed |
| K | Soil erodibility | Chosen by routing and surfacing |
| LS | Slope length and steepness | Controlled by grade and drainage spacing |
| C | Cover management | Bare tread is the worst case (C near 1.0) |
| P | Support practices | Drainage structures, armoring |

**LS** rises with both steepness and length. On a trail, the effective slope length is the distance water travels along the tread before leaving it. A drain every 30 feet produces a short effective slope length. No drains on a 500-foot descent produces a very long one. Every grade reversal, dip or water bar resets slope length to zero.

### 3.6 The Tread Watershed

Each trail segment between drainage points collects water from three sources:

1. Rain falling directly on the tread.
2. Runoff from the hillside above that crosses the trail.
3. Water channeled from upslope trail segments that failed to drain.

The smaller each tread watershed, the less water any single point must handle. Many small drains outperform a few large ones.

## 4. Erosion Processes on Trails

| Process | Mechanism | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Splash erosion | Raindrop impact detaches particles | Pedestals of soil under pebbles, crusted surface |
| Sheet erosion | Thin overland flow removes uniform layer | Exposed roots and rocks, gradual lowering of tread |
| Rill erosion | Flow concentrates into small channels | Small grooves (under 30 cm deep) down the tread |
| Gully erosion | Rills merge and deepen | Deep channels that cannot be crossed by normal maintenance |
| Entrenchment | Tread lowers below surrounding ground | Trail becomes a trench; water cannot exit |
| Mechanical displacement | Feet, hooves and tires push, shear and loosen soil | Loose material on steep pitches, braking ruts, berms on outside edge |
| Freeze-thaw | Ice lenses and needle ice lift and loosen soil | Fluffy, puffy surface in spring; deep mud on thaw |
| Wind deflation | Wind removes fine particles from dry, loose tread | Coarse lag surface, dust |
| Mass wasting | Gravity moves soil downslope | Slumps, slides, tension cracks, buried tread |

### 4.1 The Entrenchment Feedback Loop

1. Water runs down the tread and removes soil.
2. The tread lowers below the surrounding ground surface.
3. The raised edges block water from leaving sideways.
4. More water stays in the trench, so erosion accelerates.
5. Users avoid the rocky, rutted trench and walk beside it.
6. The trail widens, vegetation dies and a parallel trench starts.

Once a trail is entrenched, outsloping becomes impossible without rebuilding or rerouting. Prevention through early drainage is far cheaper than repair.

### 4.2 User Traffic

Different users apply different forces:

- **Hikers:** moderate pressure (a standing adult exerts roughly 3 to 9 psi); downhill steps with heel strikes push soil downslope.
- **Horses:** a standing horse exerts roughly 12 to 15 psi per hoof with much higher peaks when moving. Shod hooves cut and punch into wet soil.
- **Bicycles:** concentrated linear contact; braking and skidding on steep descents shear and displace soil into ruts.
- **Motorized:** wheel spin and torque displace large volumes of soil, especially on climbs.

All user types cause far more damage on wet soil because saturated soil has lost much of its shear strength.

## 5. Trail Layout Science

Most erosion problems are layout problems. Good alignment prevents more damage than any structure can fix.

### 5.1 Fall Line Versus Contour

- **Fall line trails** run straight up and down the slope (the path a ball would roll). Water flows directly down the tread with nowhere to go. These almost always fail.
- **Contour (sidehill) trails** cross the slope at an angle. Water flowing downhill crosses the trail and continues on, rather than running along it.

### 5.2 The Half Rule

**A trail's grade should not exceed half the grade of the sideslope it crosses.**

| Sideslope grade | Maximum trail grade |
|---|---|
| 10% | 5% |
| 20% | 10% |
| 30% | 15% |
| 40% | 20% |

If the trail grade approaches the sideslope grade, the trail is effectively on the fall line. Water that lands on the tread will run along it instead of draining across it.

### 5.3 The Ten Percent Average Guideline

On most soils, an average trail grade of 10 percent or less is sustainable over long distances. Short pitches can be steeper if they are armored or on rock.

### 5.4 Maximum Sustainable Grade

Maximum grade depends on soil, rock content, rainfall, user type and drainage frequency.

| Tread material | Typical maximum sustained grade |
|---|---|
| Loose sand or silt | 5% to 8% |
| Loam | 10% to 15% |
| Well-graded gravelly soil | 15% to 20% |
| Armored tread or solid bedrock | 20% or more |

High rainfall, horse traffic or motorized use push these numbers lower.

### 5.5 Grade Reversals

A grade reversal is a short section where the trail stops climbing (or descending), dips slightly and then resumes its grade. Water reaching the low point has to leave the trail.

- Built into the alignment during layout, not added later.
- Spacing of about 20 to 50 feet on steeper trails; up to 100 feet or more on gentle grades in stable soils.
- Invisible to most users, so they are not bypassed.
- Self-maintaining when well placed.

### 5.6 Outslope

The tread surface tilts toward the downhill side, typically **3 to 5 percent** (roughly 3/8 to 5/8 inch per foot of tread width).

- Lets water sheet-flow across the tread and off the edge.
- Too little: water stays on the tread.
- Too much: users slide toward the edge and walk on the uphill side, which flattens and eventually reverses the slope.

**Inslope** (tread tilted toward the hillside) is used only with a constructed ditch and drain outlet, mainly on switchback approaches, sharp turns or where an outside drop-off makes outslope unsafe.

### 5.7 Full Bench Versus Partial Bench

- **Full bench:** the entire tread is cut into undisturbed mineral soil. Strongest and most durable construction.
- **Partial (half) bench:** part of the tread sits on cut soil and part on fill placed downhill. The fill portion settles, erodes and sloughs off, narrowing the trail over time.

Full bench is the standard for sustainable sidehill trail. Fill should only be used where it can be fully compacted, keyed in or supported by retaining structures.

### 5.8 Backslope

The **backslope** is the cut bank on the uphill side of a bench. Its stable angle depends on material:

| Material | Typical stable backslope (horizontal : vertical) |
|---|---|
| Solid rock | 0.25:1 to near vertical |
| Cohesive soil, firm clay | 0.75:1 to 1:1 |
| Gravelly soil | 1:1 to 1.5:1 |
| Sand or loose soil | 1.5:1 to 2:1 |

Round off the top edge of the backslope (the "hinge") so it does not overhang and collapse. Slough from an over-steep backslope falls onto the tread and creates an inslope that traps water.

### 5.9 Turns

- **Climbing turns** follow the slope around a turn without a constructed landing. Work on slopes under roughly 15 percent.
- **Switchbacks** use a flattened landing to reverse direction on steeper slopes. They require more excavation, retaining work and drainage at both the upper and lower approaches.
- **Shortcutting** is the main switchback failure. Users cut straight down between legs, creating a fall line gully. Prevention includes placing turns where natural obstacles (rock outcrops, large trees, dense brush) block the shortcut, armoring the corner and making the legs long enough that cutting saves little.

### 5.10 Places to Avoid

- **Flat ground:** water has nowhere to go and pools.
- **Toe slopes and valley bottoms:** groundwater emerges here as seeps and springs.
- **Long uniform grades:** no natural points for water to exit.
- **Unstable slopes:** active slides, steep fill, slopes with tension cracks.
- **Fine silty soils** wherever a rockier or better-drained route is available.

## 6. Drainage Structures

Drainage follows a hierarchy. Each step down costs more and requires more maintenance.

1. **Designed-in drainage:** contour alignment, grade reversals and outslope.
2. **Shaped drainage:** knicks and rolling grade dips cut into existing tread.
3. **Built drainage:** water bars, check dams and ditches.
4. **Crossing structures:** culverts, fords and bridges.
5. **Wet area structures:** turnpikes, causeways and boardwalks.

### 6.1 Knicks

A **knick** is a shallow, semicircular lowering of the tread, about 10 feet long and 2 to 4 inches deep at its center, sloped toward the outside edge. It removes the outer berm and drains a puddle or low spot on an otherwise outsloped trail. Fast to build and easy for users to pass.

### 6.2 Rolling Grade Dips

A **rolling grade dip** is an elongated version of a grade reversal retrofitted into an existing trail. The trail descends into a gentle trough and climbs out a short reverse grade, with the trough draining off the outside edge.

- Typically 15 to 30 feet long overall.
- Works best on trails under about 15 percent grade.
- Rideable and walkable, so users do not bypass it.
- The outlet must be armored if it discharges onto erodible soil.

### 6.3 Water Bars

A **water bar** is a barrier of rock, log or timber set across the tread at an angle to intercept water and turn it off the trail.

**Angle:** set at roughly 30 to 45 degrees off a line perpendicular to the trail (that is, angled downhill). Too shallow and water stalls, dropping sediment and filling the bar. Too steep and water runs fast along the bar and erodes the outlet.

**Construction:**
- Dig a trench across the tread and set the rock or log so at least half to two thirds of its depth is buried.
- Extend the bar into the backslope so water cannot sneak around the uphill end.
- Extend it past the outside edge so water discharges onto stable ground.
- Armor the outlet with rock to dissipate energy.
- Pack and compact soil on the uphill side to form a smooth approach.

**Drawbacks:** they trip hikers, bounce bikes, get walked around at the ends and fill with sediment. Grade dips are preferred where possible.

**Approximate spacing (rule-of-thumb starting points; erodible silty or sandy soils need the tighter end):**

| Trail grade | Spacing |
|---|---|
| 3% | 150 to 250 ft |
| 5% | 100 to 150 ft |
| 8% | 75 to 100 ft |
| 10% | 50 to 80 ft |
| 15% | 40 to 60 ft |
| 20% | 30 to 40 ft |
| 25% or more | 20 to 30 ft (or armor the tread) |

Better spacing method: watch the trail in heavy rain. Place a drain wherever water starts to visibly concentrate and carry sediment.

### 6.4 Check Dams and Steps in Entrenched Trails

In a trail already trenched too deep to outslope, **check dams** (rocks or logs set across the trench) slow water and trap sediment behind them. Over time the trench fills in step by step. Combined with backfill of compacted mineral soil or crushed rock, check dams convert a gully into a stepped trail.

### 6.5 Ditches

Inside ditches carry water along insloped sections to a drain outlet or culvert. Ditches must have enough grade to keep water moving (roughly 2 percent minimum) and must be armored if grade or flow is high.

### 6.6 Culverts

Culverts carry permanent or seasonal flow under the trail.

- Size for peak storm flow plus debris. Trail culverts are often 12 to 24 inches in diameter; smaller pipes plug.
- Set at a grade of about 2 percent or steeper so they self-clean.
- Align with the natural channel to avoid redirecting flow.
- Protect the inlet with a rock headwall so water cannot erode around the pipe.
- Extend the outlet beyond the fill slope and place a **riprap apron** (rock energy dissipator) so the discharge does not cut a gully.
- **Rock box culverts** (stone-lined channels capped with flat stone) are common in backcountry settings.

### 6.7 Drain Outlets

Every drain is only as good as its outlet.

- **Daylight** the outlet: water must leave the tread onto ground lower than the trail.
- Discharge onto stable, vegetated or rocky ground.
- Spread water rather than concentrating it.
- Never discharge onto an unstable fill slope or directly above a retaining wall.
- Armor outlets with rock where flow is strong.

### 6.8 Subsurface Drainage

Where groundwater seeps under the trail:

- **French drains:** trenches filled with open-graded rock, sometimes with perforated pipe, wrapped in geotextile to keep fines out.
- **Underdrains** beneath turnpikes or aggregate surfacing to intercept seepage.

## 7. Wet Areas

### 7.1 Why Wet Areas Fail

Saturated soil has high pore water pressure, so it has little shear strength. Under traffic it deforms, ruts and turns to mud. Users walk around the mud, widening the trail and spreading damage into the surrounding wetland.

### 7.2 Capillary Rise

Water climbs upward through fine soil by capillary action. Approximate maximum capillary rise:

| Soil | Capillary rise |
|---|---|
| Clay | Several meters (slowly) |
| Silt | About 1 m or more |
| Fine sand | 0.3 to 1 m |
| Coarse sand | 0.1 to 0.3 m |
| Gravel | A few centimeters |

A layer of open-graded coarse rock acts as a **capillary break** that keeps the tread surface drier than the saturated ground beneath it.

### 7.3 Geotextiles

Geotextile fabric between soft native soil and aggregate:

- **Separation:** prevents gravel from punching down into mud and prevents mud from pumping up into gravel under repeated loads.
- **Filtration:** lets water pass while holding soil in place.
- **Reinforcement:** spreads load over a wider area.

Without separation, aggregate placed directly on wet clay or silt can disappear into the subgrade within a few seasons.

**Geocells** (honeycomb plastic grids filled with aggregate) confine particles laterally and dramatically increase load-bearing capacity on soft ground.

### 7.4 Wet Area Structures

| Structure | Description | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Turnpike | Tread raised between rock or log retainers with ditches on both sides; geotextile and coarse fill under a compacted cap | Seasonally wet ground where ditches can drain to an outlet |
| Causeway | Same raised tread without ditches | Flat saturated ground where ditches have nowhere to drain |
| Puncheon | Wooden walkway on sills laid on the ground | Persistently wet soil, short distances |
| Boardwalk | Elevated walkway on posts or piers | Standing water, sensitive wetlands |
| Stepping stones | Large flat stones set into wet tread | Short wet spots, low traffic |
| Stone pitching / armoring | Tightly fitted rocks set on edge into the tread | Wet or steep tread with heavy use |

**Reroute first.** Moving the trail to higher, drier ground almost always costs less over the life of the trail than building and maintaining wet area structures.

## 8. Surfacing: Native Soil Versus Gravel Versus Rock

### 8.1 Native Soil

**Advantages:** no material hauling, natural appearance, low cost, easy to reshape during maintenance.

**Disadvantages:** performance depends entirely on soil type. Silt and fine sand erode readily. Clay ruts when wet. Organic soils fail outright.

**Best case:** well-graded gravelly loam with 10 to 25 percent clay content, built on a sustainable alignment with good drainage. This can outperform imported gravel.

### 8.2 Gravel Types

**Rounded gravel (pea gravel, river rock, bank-run rounded gravel)**
- Particles roll over each other like ball bearings.
- Low friction angle and no interlock.
- Migrates downhill, displaces under feet and tires, collects at trail edges.
- Unsuitable for slopes and trail surfaces generally. Usable as drainage fill.

**Open-graded crushed rock (for example 3/4 inch clean, no fines)**
- Angular particles of a narrow size range with empty voids between them.
- Excellent drainage and capillary break.
- Loose and unstable as a walking surface because nothing fills the voids.
- Best as a base layer under a dense-graded cap or in French drains.

**Dense-graded (well-graded) crushed rock (3/4 inch minus, crusher run, road base)**
- A full range of sizes from the maximum size down to dust.
- Smaller particles fill voids between larger particles, then fines fill the remaining voids.
- Compacts into a tight, almost pavement-like surface.
- The standard for durable aggregate trail tread and base.

**Crusher fines (3/8 inch minus or 1/4 inch minus)**
- Fine dense-graded material.
- Compacts to a smooth, firm surface suitable for accessible trails.
- Needs a sound base and good drainage; erodes on steep grades or under concentrated flow.
- **Fines content** (material passing the No. 200 sieve, 0.075 mm) of roughly 10 to 15 percent binds the surface. Too few fines and the surface ravels. Too many and it turns to mud when wet and dust when dry.

**Stabilized aggregate**
- Crusher fines mixed with a binder (plant-based stabilizers such as psyllium, polymer emulsions, lignin sulfonate or small amounts of cement).
- Resists erosion and raveling far better than plain crusher fines.

### 8.3 Gradation Science

A **well-graded** aggregate follows roughly a Fuller curve, where the percentage passing each sieve size is:

```
P = 100 × (d / D)^n
```

- **P** = percent passing sieve size d
- **D** = maximum particle size
- **n** = about 0.45 to 0.5 for maximum density

This distribution leaves the fewest voids and produces the highest compacted density and strength.

### 8.4 Particle Shape and Durability

- **Angular crushed rock** interlocks; friction angles of 40 to 48 degrees.
- **Rounded rock** rolls; friction angles of 32 to 36 degrees or lower when loose.
- **Hardness matters:** soft limestone, sandstone and weathered rock break down under traffic into fine dust that turns to mud. Harder rocks such as basalt, granite and quartzite last longer. The Los Angeles abrasion test measures resistance to breakdown; lower loss values are better.

### 8.5 Gravel Failure Modes

| Failure | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Washout on steep grades | Concentrated flow exceeds the velocity needed to move the particle size | Frequent drainage, larger aggregate or armoring, stabilizers |
| Raveling (loose surface) | Too few fines; insufficient compaction | Dense-graded mix, compaction at optimum moisture |
| Edge migration | Rounded or loose material pushed sideways | Angular aggregate, edging, compaction |
| Sinking into subgrade | Soft wet soil below; no separation | Geotextile, thicker base, drainage |
| Mud and dust cycles | Excess fines or soft rock | Correct gradation, durable rock |

**Thickness guidance:**
- Foot traffic on firm subgrade: about 3 to 4 inches compacted.
- Soft or wet subgrade: 6 inches or more over geotextile.
- Equestrian or heavy use: 6 to 8 inches or more with a dense-graded base.

### 8.6 Comparison Table

| Property | Native soil (good loam) | Native soil (silt or sand) | Rounded gravel | Dense-graded crushed | Crusher fines | Rock armoring |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drainage | Moderate | Silt poor, sand high | High | Moderate | Low to moderate | High (through joints) |
| Stability | Good | Poor | Poor | Excellent | Good on gentle grades | Excellent |
| Erosion resistance | Moderate to good | Poor | Poor on slopes | Good | Fair | Excellent |
| Cost | Low | Low | Moderate | Moderate to high | Moderate to high | High (labor) |
| Maintenance | Moderate | High | High | Low to moderate | Moderate | Low |
| Best use | Most backcountry trails | Gentle grades only | Drainage fill only | Heavily used and wet areas | Accessible and urban paths | Steep pitches, wet spots, drain outlets |

### 8.7 Sizing Rock to Resist Flowing Water

The **Isbash equation** estimates the velocity a rock of given size can withstand:

```
V = C × √[2g × (ρs − ρw) / ρw] × √D
```

- **V** = water velocity that will just move the rock
- **C** = coefficient (about 0.86 for loose rock; about 1.2 for embedded, interlocked rock)
- **g** = 9.81 m/s²
- **ρs** = rock density (about 2,650 kg/m³)
- **ρw** = water density (1,000 kg/m³)
- **D** = rock diameter

Rearranged, required rock diameter scales with **velocity squared**. If drainage outlet velocity doubles, the armoring rock needs to be four times larger in diameter. Embedding rock raises its resistance by about 40 percent compared to loose placement.

## 9. Rock Work

### 9.1 Rock Selection

- **Angular, flat-faced, durable rock** is preferred.
- Avoid rounded cobbles (they roll and pop out), shale and heavily weathered rock (they split and crumble).
- Rock from local sources blends visually and avoids importing weed seeds.

### 9.2 Burial Depth

Rock stability comes from confinement by surrounding soil, not from the rock's own weight. Bury **at least half and preferably two thirds** of each rock's depth. Set the largest dimension into the ground. A rock sitting on the surface will be kicked loose, rolled by water or levered out by frost.

### 9.3 Rock Steps

- Used on grades too steep to drain with dips alone (roughly over 15 to 20 percent).
- Typical rise of 6 to 8 inches; consistent rise improves safety and keeps users on the steps.
- Each step needs a stable foundation, with the downhill face supported by buried footer rocks.
- Compacted fill behind each step forms the tread.
- Extend steps across the full trail width and anchor the ends with large rocks so users do not walk around them.

### 9.4 Stone Pitching and Armoring

Rocks set on edge, tightly fitted like cobblestones, with joints packed with crushed rock and soil. The interlocking pattern spreads load and resists both traffic and water. Used for steep pitches, wet crossings and heavily used sections.

### 9.5 Retaining Walls

Retaining walls hold fill where the trail crosses steep ground.

**Dry stone walls:**
- Set a **footer course** of the largest rocks in a trench below grade.
- **Batter** the wall (lean it into the hillside) at roughly 1:4 to 1:6 (1 unit horizontal for every 4 to 6 units of rise).
- Follow **one-over-two, two-over-one** joint placement so no vertical seams run through multiple courses.
- Place **tie stones (headers)** that extend deep into the slope every few feet along each course.
- Backfill with crushed rock behind the wall for drainage so water pressure does not build up.
- Tilt each stone slightly inward so it rests against the slope.

**Crib walls:** interlocking logs or timbers forming boxes filled with rock and soil. Cheaper and faster where large rock is unavailable; limited lifespan from rot.

**Why drainage matters for walls:** a saturated backfill exerts much higher lateral pressure than a drained one. Most wall failures involve water.

### 9.6 Rock as User Control

Large rocks placed along trail edges, at corners and at shortcut points ("anchors" or "guide rocks") keep users on the tread, which prevents widening and braiding.

## 10. Vegetation and Trees

### 10.1 How Plants Stabilize Soil

**Canopy interception:** foliage catches rain before it reaches the ground. Conifer forests can intercept roughly 20 to 50 percent of annual rainfall in some climates. Some intercepted water evaporates and never reaches the soil.

Note: drops falling from tall canopies coalesce into large drops that can approach terminal velocity after falling about 8 meters or more. Tall canopy alone does not fully protect soil. The **understory and litter layer** provide most of the impact protection.

**Litter and duff:** absorb raindrop energy, keep infiltration high and slow surface flow.

**Root reinforcement:** roots act like steel rebar in concrete. They are strong in tension while soil is weak in tension. The **Wu-Waldron model** estimates the added cohesion from roots crossing a potential failure plane:

```
Δc ≈ 1.2 × Tr × (Ar / A)
```

- **Δc** = additional cohesion from roots
- **Tr** = average tensile strength of roots
- **Ar / A** = root area ratio (cross-sectional area of roots divided by area of soil)

Typical added cohesion ranges from about 1 to 20 kPa, which can be the difference between a stable and failed slope. Root tensile strength typically runs about 5 to 60 MPa. Smaller roots are often stronger per unit area than larger ones.

Roots work in different zones:
- **Fine fibrous roots** (grasses, forbs) bind the top 15 to 30 cm against surface erosion.
- **Lateral roots** (shrubs, trees) form a mat that holds shallow soil together.
- **Deep sinker and tap roots** cross potential failure planes and anchor soil to stable layers below (the "anchoring" or "buttressing" effect).

**Evapotranspiration:** plants pull water out of the soil and release it through leaves. This lowers soil moisture and pore water pressure, which raises effective stress and shear strength. A large mature tree can move hundreds of liters of water per day in summer. Deciduous trees provide little of this effect in winter, which is often the wettest season.

**Root decay after cutting:** when trees are cut, their roots slowly decay. Root reinforcement typically declines over 3 to 10 years after cutting, before new vegetation re-establishes. Landslide frequency often peaks in this window. Clearing large numbers of trees for a trail corridor on steep ground can reduce slope stability years later.

### 10.2 Problems With Trees Next to Trails

| Problem | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Windthrow | Wind levers a tree over; the root plate tears up a large mass of soil and leaves a pit. Trees on cut edges, with severed roots or newly exposed by clearing are most vulnerable. |
| Surcharge loading | Large trees add weight to slopes; usually outweighed by root reinforcement though significant on marginal slopes. |
| Exposed roots on tread | Tread erosion uncovers roots, which become trip hazards and slick surfaces when wet. Roots act as small dams, creating steps that concentrate water and accelerate erosion below them. |
| Root damage and decay | Wounded roots admit decay fungi, weakening the tree and eventually creating hazard trees. |
| Root heave | Growing roots lift and crack hardened surfaces, boardwalk footings and wall foundations. |
| Litter in drains | Leaves and needles clog water bars, culverts and ditches, especially after autumn leaf drop. |
| Shade | Keeps tread wet longer in wet climates (more mud and rutting). In hot dry climates, shade helps by reducing drying, dust and raveling. |
| Compaction of root zones | Most fine feeder roots live in the top 30 cm and spread well beyond the dripline. Trail traffic and construction compact this zone and starve the tree. |

### 10.3 Routing Trails Around Trees

- Route around large trees where possible, staying outside the **critical root zone**. A common rule of thumb is a radius of 1 foot per inch of trunk diameter at breast height.
- Use large trees as natural **anchors** at turns to discourage shortcutting.
- Where a trail must cross roots, **bridge them with fill** (mineral soil or aggregate) rather than cutting through them.
- Avoid severing structural roots (larger than about 2 inches in diameter) close to the trunk.
- Watch for **pistol-butt trees** (trunks curved at the base then straight above), which indicate slow soil creep.

### 10.4 Planting for Stabilization

| Plant type | Root depth | Strengths | Placement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasses and forbs | 15 to 60 cm | Fast cover, dense surface binding, protects from splash and sheet erosion | Disturbed areas, cut and fill slopes, closed shortcuts |
| Shrubs | 1 to 3 m | Deeper reinforcement, flexible in wind, low windthrow risk, block shortcuts | Fill slopes below trail, cut banks, switchback corners |
| Trees | 1 to 5 m or more | Deep anchoring, strong evapotranspiration, long-term stability | Set back from tread edge, below trail on stable slopes |
| Willow, dogwood, cottonwood cuttings | Variable, root readily from cuttings | Fast establishment in wet ground | Streambanks, seeps, wet fill slopes |

**Placement principles:**
- Keep trees back from the tread edge so roots do not grow under the tread and future windthrow does not take the trail with it.
- Plant shrubs and grasses close to the tread edge and on fill slopes.
- Avoid planting trees on top of or immediately behind retaining walls, where root growth and windthrow can displace the wall.
- Use native species from local seed sources; they are adapted to local soils and climate and do not spread invasively.

### 10.5 Soil Bioengineering Techniques

| Technique | Description |
|---|---|
| Live staking | Dormant cuttings of willow, dogwood or similar species driven into the soil; they root and sprout |
| Brush layering | Live branches laid in trenches across a slope, buried with tips protruding; reinforce like layers of rebar |
| Wattles (fascines) | Bundles of live branches laid in shallow trenches along the contour; slow runoff and trap sediment |
| Live crib walls | Log crib walls with live cuttings placed between the timbers; the plants take over as the wood rots |
| Erosion control blankets | Jute, coir or straw mats that hold seed and soil in place until vegetation establishes |
| Straw wattles and mulch | Temporary sediment control and surface protection |

### 10.6 Closing Shortcuts and Rehabilitating Old Trail

1. Break up compacted soil (scarify) so roots and water can penetrate.
2. Fill ruts and gullies with mineral soil; add check dams in deep channels.
3. Restore natural contour and drainage.
4. Add organic matter (saved duff) to the surface.
5. Transplant or seed native plants.
6. Place rocks, logs and brush across the old route ("vertical mulch") to block use and hide the line from view.
7. Make the official trail more appealing than the shortcut.

## 11. Slope Stability and Earth Movement

### 11.1 Types of Mass Movement

| Type | Speed | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Soil creep | Millimeters per year | Slow downslope movement of the upper soil layer; causes tilted fences and curved tree trunks |
| Slump (rotational slide) | Slow to moderate | Block of soil rotates along a curved failure surface; leaves a scarp at the top and a bulge at the toe |
| Translational slide | Moderate to fast | Soil slides along a planar surface, often where soil meets bedrock |
| Debris flow | Fast | Saturated soil, rock and organic debris flowing like wet concrete down channels |
| Rockfall | Very fast | Rocks detach from cliffs or steep cuts |

### 11.2 Infinite Slope Stability Model

For long, uniform slopes, the **factor of safety** against sliding is:

```
FS = [c' + (γ × z × cos²β − u) × tan φ'] / (γ × z × sin β × cos β)
```

- **FS** = factor of safety (above 1.0 is stable; below 1.0 fails; designers often target 1.3 to 1.5)
- **c'** = effective cohesion (including root cohesion)
- **γ** = unit weight of soil
- **z** = depth to failure plane
- **β** = slope angle
- **u** = pore water pressure
- **φ'** = effective friction angle

The equation shows exactly how slopes fail:
- **Rising pore pressure (u)** from rain or concentrated drainage reduces the resisting force.
- **Steeper slope (β)** increases the driving force.
- **Loss of root cohesion** reduces c'.

### 11.3 How Trails Destabilize Slopes

- **Undercutting the toe:** cutting a bench removes support from the soil above.
- **Loading the head:** placing fill on the upper part of a slope adds driving weight.
- **Concentrating water:** discharging drains onto unstable slopes raises pore pressure in a small area.
- **Removing vegetation:** clearing reduces root reinforcement and evapotranspiration.
- **Over-steepening cuts and fills:** exceeding the stable angle of the material.

### 11.4 Angle of Repose

The steepest angle at which loose material stays piled without sliding:

| Material | Approximate angle of repose |
|---|---|
| Dry fine sand | 30° to 34° |
| Rounded gravel | 32° to 36° |
| Angular gravel | 36° to 40° |
| Angular broken rock | 40° to 45° |
| Wet silt or clay | Highly variable; can drop below 20° when saturated |

Unretained fill slopes should not exceed angle of repose. A slope of **1.5:1 (horizontal:vertical)**, about 34 degrees, is a common maximum for compacted soil fill.

### 11.5 Building Fill Correctly

- Remove organic material and loose soil from the base.
- **Key in** the fill by cutting small steps (benches) into the native slope so the fill cannot slide on a smooth surface.
- Place and compact in 4 to 6 inch lifts at optimum moisture.
- **Never bury logs, stumps or brush in fill.** They rot and leave voids that cause the fill to collapse years later.
- Keep drain outlets off fill slopes.

### 11.6 Warning Signs of Unstable Ground

- Tension cracks parallel to the slope
- Scarps or steps in the ground surface
- Hummocky, lumpy terrain
- Leaning or curved trees
- Seeps, springs and wetland plants (sedges, rushes, horsetail, skunk cabbage) on a hillside
- Bulging ground at the base of slopes
- Tilted rocks, fences or old stumps

The best solution for unstable ground is usually **avoiding it** with a reroute.

## 12. Freeze-Thaw and Climate Effects

### 12.1 Frost Heave

When soil freezes, water migrates toward the freezing front by capillary action and forms **ice lenses**. The lenses grow and lift the soil surface. On thaw, the melted ice leaves the soil supersaturated with almost no strength.

**Frost susceptibility** is highest in **silts**, which combine strong capillary action (to pull water up) with enough permeability (to supply it quickly). Clean gravel and coarse sand are largely non-frost-susceptible. Clay pulls water strongly but moves it too slowly to form large lenses.

### 12.2 Needle Ice

On cold clear nights, thin ice needles grow out of moist bare soil, lifting soil particles a few centimeters. On thaw, the particles drop as a loose, fluffy layer that erodes easily. A major source of erosion on bare tread in climates with frequent freeze-thaw cycles.

### 12.3 Thaw Damage

Trails are most vulnerable during spring thaw, when meltwater saturates the upper soil and the frozen layer below prevents drainage. Traffic during this period causes deep rutting. Seasonal closures during thaw prevent damage that takes years to repair.

### 12.4 Climate Considerations

| Climate | Main problems | Responses |
|---|---|---|
| High rainfall | Saturated soil, mud, high runoff | Tight drainage spacing, aggregate surfacing, armoring, routing on well-drained soil |
| Arid | Loose dry soil, raveling, intense flash storms | Compaction at optimum moisture, stabilizers, rock armoring, larger drainage features for rare high flows |
| Cold with freeze-thaw | Frost heave, needle ice, thaw mud | Non-frost-susceptible surfacing, drainage, thaw season closures |
| Snowmelt dominated | Prolonged saturation in spring | Drainage sized for long-duration flow, wet area structures |
| Windy and exposed | Deflation of fines | Coarser surfacing, vegetation on edges |

## 13. Maintenance Science and Practice

### 13.1 Priorities

1. **Clean and repair drainage first.** A clogged drain concentrates water at a single point and can fail worse than no drain at all.
2. **Restore outslope** and remove berms.
3. **Remove slough** from the inside of the tread.
4. **Brush the corridor** so users stay centered on the tread.
5. **Clear downed trees** so users do not create bypass routes.
6. **Repair tread** damage.

### 13.2 Berms

A **berm** is a ridge of soil, organic debris and displaced material that builds up along the outside edge of the tread. It forms from slough, traffic pushing material outward and vegetation growing along the edge. A berm turns an outsloped trail into a channel. Removing berms is one of the most important routine maintenance tasks.

### 13.3 Slough

**Slough** is soil and rock that falls from the backslope onto the inside edge of the tread. It narrows the trail and creates an inslope that sends water along the inner edge. Remove it, cast it downhill (away from drains) and reshape the backslope if it keeps failing.

### 13.4 Brushing

Overgrown vegetation pushes users toward the outer edge or into the middle, which breaks down tread edges and widens the trail. Typical corridor dimensions:

| Use | Clearing width | Clearing height |
|---|---|---|
| Hiking | About 4 to 6 ft | About 8 ft |
| Mountain biking | About 4 to 6 ft | About 8 ft |
| Equestrian | About 8 to 10 ft | About 10 to 12 ft |

Cut branches flush at the branch collar so the tree can seal the wound properly.

### 13.5 Tread Repair

- Fill ruts and holes with **compacted mineral soil or dense-graded crushed rock**, not loose dirt or organic material.
- Compact at optimum moisture (after rain has soaked in but before the soil dries out or while it is still saturated).
- Fix the drainage problem that caused the damage, or the repair will fail.

### 13.6 Timing

- **Best:** a day or two after rain, when soil is moist and compacts well.
- **Worst:** during saturation (soil smears and will not compact) or in drought (soil will not bind).
- **Inspect during heavy rain** whenever possible. Water shows exactly where drainage is failing.

### 13.7 Maintenance Schedule

| Timing | Tasks |
|---|---|
| After major storms | Inspect and clear drains and culverts; check for washouts, slides and fallen trees |
| Spring (after thaw) | Full drainage inspection, berm removal, slough removal, log-out, tread repair |
| Summer | Brushing, construction projects, tread reshaping (when soil moisture allows) |
| Autumn (after leaf drop) | Clear leaves and needles from drains, culverts and ditches before winter rain |
| Annually | Walk the full trail, log problem spots, photo point monitoring |
| Every 3 to 5 years | Evaluate problem sections for reroute or reconstruction |

### 13.8 Monitoring

- **Photo points:** fixed locations photographed from the same spot and angle each year.
- **Cross-section measurements:** stretch a string level across the trail between fixed stakes and measure down to the tread every few inches. Changes in depth show entrenchment rate and soil loss.
- **Tread width measurements:** track widening.
- **Problem logs:** record location, problem type, cause and fix.

### 13.9 Diagnostic Table

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Water running down tread | Fall line alignment, no drainage, berm on edge | Grade dips, knicks, berm removal, reroute if fall line |
| Entrenched trench | Long-term concentrated flow | Check dams, backfill, reroute |
| Mud holes | Flat ground, poor drainage, fine soil, seeps | Drain outlets, aggregate over geotextile, turnpike, reroute |
| Trail widening | Mud, obstacles, overgrown vegetation | Fix mud, brushing, edge anchors |
| Braided trail | Users avoiding a problem in the main tread | Fix the problem, close and rehab side paths |
| Shortcuts at switchbacks | Turn too tight or legs too short; no barriers | Armor corners, natural barriers, rehab cut |
| Loose rocks and ravel on steep sections | Grade too steep for soil, mechanical displacement | Armor, rock steps, reroute to lower grade |
| Exposed roots | Tread erosion | Drainage, fill over roots, reroute around trees |
| Drains filled with sediment | Angle too shallow, erosion upslope | Adjust angle, add upslope drainage, clean regularly |
| Outlet gullies | Unarmored outlet, concentrated flow | Rock apron, spread discharge |
| Slumping outer edge | Fill on outer edge, saturation | Full bench, retaining wall, drainage |
| Cracks and bulges on slope | Slope instability | Stop discharging water above it, reroute |

## 14. Quick Reference

### 14.1 Formulas

| Formula | Use |
|---|---|
| τf = c' + σ' tan φ' | Soil shear strength |
| σ' = σ − u | Effective stress (water pressure reduces strength) |
| V = (1/n) R^(2/3) S^(1/2) | Water velocity (Manning) |
| τ = γ R S | Shear stress of flowing water on soil |
| Movable particle mass ∝ V⁶ | Airy's sixth-power law |
| A = R × K × LS × C × P | Soil loss (RUSLE) |
| Δc ≈ 1.2 Tr (Ar / A) | Root reinforcement (Wu-Waldron) |
| FS = [c' + (γz cos²β − u) tan φ'] / (γz sin β cos β) | Infinite slope stability |
| V = C √[2g(ρs − ρw)/ρw] √D | Rock size to resist flow (Isbash) |
| P = 100 (d / D)^n | Well-graded aggregate gradation (Fuller) |

### 14.2 Rules of Thumb

- Trail grade at or below half the sideslope grade.
- Average grade of 10 percent or less on most soils.
- Outslope of 3 to 5 percent.
- Grade reversals every 20 to 50 feet on steeper sections.
- Water bars at 30 to 45 degrees, buried at least half to two thirds.
- Compact in 4 to 6 inch lifts at optimum moisture.
- Bury rocks at least half to two thirds.
- Angular crushed rock, never rounded gravel, for tread.
- Roughly 10 to 15 percent fines in crusher fines surfacing.
- Geotextile between aggregate and soft wet soil.
- Unretained fill no steeper than 1.5:1.
- Dry stone walls battered 1:4 to 1:6 into the slope.
- Keep large trees back from the tread; shrubs and grasses near it.
- Clear drains after every major storm and after autumn leaf drop.
- Reroute before building wet area structures.
