# Piloting and Driving Positions

Hub: `Index.md`. Ride under the seat: `Suspension.md`. What the hands and feet operate: `Brakes.md`, `Wheels.md`. Exposed vs shelled pilot drag: `PerfectGlider/DesignB.md`. Weight-shift flying: `PerfectGlider/Joints.md`.

The best position is the one that lets the eyes see the path, the hands and feet stay on the controls, and the spine and the harness take the loads. No single seat wins for a wagon, a fighter, and a glider.

## Shared rules

**See the ground.** Eye height `h` and the downward angle `α` over the nose, the dashboard, or the horse's back set the first patch of ground that is visible:

`d = h / tan(α)`

| Eye height | Look-down angle | First ground seen |
|---|---|---|
| 1.6 m (high box) | 15° | about 6 m |
| 1.2 m | 10° | about 7 m |
| 1.1 m | 5° | about 13 m |
| Any | 0° (nose at the eye line) | never |

A driver of animals needs the near ruts and the horses' feet. A pilot in the flare needs the landing point. If the nose covers it, raise the eyes, droop the nose, or sit less reclined. A taildragger sits nose-up on the ground, so the forward view is blocked on purpose by the attitude. The cure is to S-turn while rolling, or to use a tricycle gear so the nose is down.

**Support the body.** An upright plank seat tires the back. A recline around **100 to 110°** between cushion and back (a little past a right angle) holds a driver for hours. Past roughly **120°** the head nods, the near ground disappears, and climbing out gets slow. Thighs supported, with a small gap behind the knee so the cushion does not cut circulation.

**Legs as springs.** Standing lets the knees absorb an unsprung cart, which is why chariots and some early boxes were stood in. It works for a short run. It fails on a long road, and a stop throws a standing person off the front. Once the vehicle has real brakes or real speed, sit them down.

**Harness.** At a walk, no belt and a clear way to jump is reasonable. The moment a stop can do what the arms cannot, a belt wins. A **lap belt alone** hinges the body forward into the wheel or the stick. Lap plus two shoulders loads the pelvis and the shoulders. A crotch strap stops the body sliding under the lap belt. Five-point is the best seat for anything that can crash or pull real g. A head tether to the shoulders (a HANS-type link) is the best neck protection in a forward hit, and it needs a helmet and shoulder belts or it does nothing.

**Hands stay on the controls.** Reins want both hands, so the brake must be a foot pedal or a lever that ratchets and stays (`Brakes.md`). A tiller is one hand and a long lever: fine on a boat or a walking-pace steamer, bad once the vehicle yaws faster than the driver can unwind it. A wheel is the best ground control above a walk: both hands, small angles, and it can be geared. A stick is the best aircraft pitch-and-roll control: one hand, two axes, forearms braced.

**Put the driver where the road is.** If traffic keeps to a side, seat them on the side that sees past the vehicle ahead when they pull out to pass. If there is no rule, the center of the vehicle is the fairest view left and right, and the best weight balance on a one-seat machine.

## Driving

| Position | Best for | Gives up |
|---|---|---|
| High upright box, able to stand | Animal power. See the hooves and the ruts. Climb down fast. | No lateral support. A real stop launches the driver. |
| Sprung bench on a rigid axle | The cheapest comfort on a cart with no vehicle springs | The body still rolls with the axle |
| Upright bucket, lumbar, slight recline | Hours on a road. A belt can be worn | Slow to leave. Needs a brake worth belting for |
| Low sport seat, more recline | Low center of gravity, some lateral g | Blind close to the nose unless the nose is low |
| Central single seat | View, balance, pedals in line with the body | No room for a second person beside |
| Cab over the front axle | Short vehicle, view straight down in front | The worst ride in the vehicle. Every bump is under the seat |
| Recumbent or prone | Lowest drag in a streamliner or a cycle car | Traffic view, neck, escape, and any crash |

**Best general road position, once there is a wheel and pedals:**

- Sit just **behind** the front wheel line, not over it, so the seat is not the first thing to hit a rut. High enough that the formula above shows ground within a few lengths.
- Heels on the floor. The ankle works the pedal. Knee still bent at about **120°** with the pedal pressed, not straight-legged. A locked knee drives crash load into the thigh and cannot modulate a brake.
- Pedals straight in front of that hip. An offset pedal twists the driver and loads one leg in a crash.
- Wheel large and fairly upright if there is no assist: more leverage, and the eyes look over the rim. Smaller and lower only when the steering is light and the nose is low.
- Hands at the sides of the wheel, not wrapped over the top, so a spinning rim does not break a thumb and the view of the road stays open.
- Lateral bolsters if cornering can slide the driver off the cushion. On a wagon they are wasted.

**Best animal-power position:** front, high, upright, feet braced on a board, reins in hand, brake lever with a ratchet on the side. The driver should be able to see the near ground and stand up to use their weight on the reins or to jump. Do not recline a coachman.

**Best unsprung cart:** spring the **seat** even when the axle is rigid. A leaf or a strap under the bench isolates the spine and is a smaller job than springing the whole body (`Suspension.md`).

**Standing** remains right for a fighting cart at a trot where the driver must jump and the trip is short. It is a bad driving position the moment the brake can skid a wheel.

## Piloting

Aircraft loads have names by where the blood goes.

| Load | What the pilot feels | Tolerance | What helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| +Gz | Pressed down into the seat in a pull-up. Blood leaves the head | A few seconds around 4 to 5 g seated upright, then greyout and blackout. A suit and a strain take a trained pilot toward 9 g | Shorter heart-to-brain column: recline, or prone/supine so the column is chest-deep |
| −Gz | Hung in the straps, or a pushover. Blood into the head | About 2 to 3 g. Redout. The worst common aerobatic load | Do not design a maneuver that needs more |
| +Gx | Pushed back into the seat. Launch, or a hard acceleration | Much higher for a short time, on the order of 10 g and beyond with a supported body | A seat and a headrest that take the whole back |
| −Gx | Thrown forward. A crash or a sudden stop | Harness and neck, not blood pressure | Shoulder belts, crotch strap, head tether |

A recline of about **30°** from upright is the fighter compromise: better g than a kitchen chair, and the pilot can still look out and lift their head. More recline keeps raising g tolerance and keeps costing the forward view and the ability to turn the head under load.

| Position | Best for | Gives up |
|---|---|---|
| Upright seat, center stick, rudder pedals | Seeing out, landing picture, maps, two-hand jobs | G tolerance. Drag if the body is the fuselage |
| Reclined ~30°, side stick | Combat g, hands still on throttle and stick | Look-back. A center stick hits the thighs in this seat |
| Semi-reclined, feet forward | Sailplanes, long soaring. View up and ahead. Rudder pedals | Not a g seat. Crash structure is often the shell |
| Recumbent, legs forward | Human-powered aircraft. The legs are the engine | Same view and escape problems as any deep recline |
| Prone, harness or chest pad | Lowest drag. Pull-up load goes chest-to-back instead of head-to-feet | Neck, looking up, hours of discomfort, parachute exit. Weight shift only works on small wings (`Joints.md`) |
| Supine | Launch and rockets, eyeballs-in | Landing view unless the seat tilts up or the window is placed for it |
| Seated harness, weight on the hips | Paraglider. Brake toggles at the hands | Not a rigid-wing cockpit |
| Tandem | Less frontal area, fighter and many trainers | The rear seat's view |
| Side by side | Teaching, talking, shared instruments | Wider fuselage, more drag |

**Best control split for an airplane:** pitch and roll on one hand (stick), yaw on the feet (pedals), power on the other hand. Roll on the feet was tried early and it is worse. A wheel (yoke) is the better pitch control on a large, stable craft with two crew and small corrections. It is the worse control for aerobatics.

**Best stick:** a center stick between the knees when the seat is upright and the work is precise. A side stick when the seat is reclined. Either way the stick should spring back to a known neutral, and the forearm should have a rest so the shoulder is not the pivot for a long flight.

**Best rudder pedals:** adjustable until the knee is slightly bent at full throw. Toe brakes hinged on the pedal are the compact way to steer on the ground without a third control. Heel brakes are an older layout and they make the ankle do two jobs badly.

**Best view for fighting or for traffic:** a canopy with the rail at the shoulder, eyes near the top of the fuselage, nothing structural in the forward hemisphere. Instruments low, not in the sightline.

**Best view for landing:** sitting high enough, in the landing attitude, that the aim point clears the nose by the formula above. Check it in the flare attitude, not in cruise. A faired pod that was perfect for drag can hide the field the moment the nose comes up.

**Best low-drag pod** (`DesignB.md` compares exposed and shelled): prone wins frontal area. A deeply reclined seat wins endurance and lookout. Use prone when the mission is straight glide inside a tight shell. Use the reclined seat when the pilot must search the sky or fly for hours. Human power forces the recumbent pedaling position regardless.

**Best escape:** the harness releases with one hand, the canopy or the harness is clear of the limbs, and a prone pilot is not asked to roll upright before they can leave. Prone lost real aircraft programs on this as much as on comfort.

## Pair them with the machine

| Machine | Position |
|---|---|
| Cart, no springs | High bench on its own straps or leaf. Standing only for a short fight |
| Coach with a team | High front box, upright, ratchet brake, reins |
| Fast road invent | Low seat, eyes still clear of a low nose, 100 to 110° back, heels down, harness |
| Hang glider / small wing | Prone, weight shift |
| Large rigid wing | Seat or recline. Aerodynamic controls. Weight shift will not roll it |
| Soaring | Semi-reclined, canopy, feet on rudder |
| Combat aircraft | About 30° recline, side stick, rudder, five-point harness |
| Rocket or catapult launch | Supine, head supported, eyeballs-in |
