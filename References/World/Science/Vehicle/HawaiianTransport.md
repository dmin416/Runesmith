Swinging from balloon tethers is the weakest option on your list. The best approach combines several systems: gravity for going downhill, trade winds for travel along ridges and coasts, and a cable or balloon lift to get height back.

**Why balloon-tether swinging fails**

- **It loses height.** A pendulum only returns the height the rider started from. Air drag means every swing ends lower than it began, so each station needs a winch or a climb to keep the rider moving.
- **The forces are brutal.** On a 200 m line released from horizontal, the rider hits about 140 mph at the bottom and the line pulls 3 times body weight. A gentler 30 degree swing keeps the bottom speed near 50 mph. Each swing then covers only about 200 m.
- **The anchor moves.** In 15 to 25 mph trade winds a 15 m helium sphere already leans 8 to 20 degrees on its own tether. A swinging rider shoves it around further.
- **The scale is huge.** Coverage takes five or more 15 m balloons per kilometer, each holding about 1,800 m3 of helium. An island needs hundreds of them, plus constant helium top-ups, lightning risk and a catch-or-die transfer at every swing.

It makes a great action scene but doesn't work as transport.

**Comparison for a 100 kg rider in Hawaiian trade winds**

| Method | Energy source | Reach | Speed | Direction | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balloon-tether swinging | Gravity on buoyant anchors | 200 to 400 m per swing | 50 to 140 mph at the bottom | Loses height every swing | Action-scene stunt only |
| Zip line | Gravity | Single spans up to about 2.8 km; about 12 km of travel per 1,000 m of drop | 30 to 60 mph | Downhill only | Excellent one-way |
| Cable car or gondola | Electric motor | Unsupported spans up to 3 km exist | 13 to 27 mph | Up, down, both ways | Best way uphill and over mountains |
| Tethered helium elevator | Buoyancy (the winch only pulls it back down) | 150 to 1,000 m vertical | 2 to 4 mph vertical | Vertical only | Cheap height for gliders |
| Paraglider | Ridge lift from the trades | Unlimited along a windward ridge; 8 to 12 km of glide per 1,000 m | 22 to 32 mph | Both ways along a ridge, easy downwind, weak upwind | Best free-flight transport |
| Hang glider | Ridge lift | Unlimited along a ridge; 10 to 15 km of glide per 1,000 m | 25 to 60 mph | Handles 20 mph headwinds | Best for flying into the wind |
| Wingsuit | Gravity | 2.5 to 3 km per 1,000 m, minus parachute deployment height | 90 to 110 mph | Downhill only, lands under a parachute | Thrill ride, not transport |
| Sailing outrigger canoe | Trade winds | Unlimited along the coast | 5 to 12 knots | Beam reach both ways along most coasts | Best coastal transport |
| E-bike on trails | Battery | 50 to 100 km per charge | 15 to 20 mph | Any direction | Best everyday baseline |

**The best network for a Hawaiian island**

1. **Coast ring:** Sailing canoes plus an e-bike trail around the island. The trades blow from the east-northeast most days, so north and south coasts get a sailable beam reach in both directions.
2. **Uphill:** Gondolas or tethered helium elevators carry riders from coastal towns up to ridge stations at 500 to 1,000 m. Buoyancy does the lifting for free.
3. **Along the ridges:** Paragliders and hang gliders ridge-soar the windward cliffs. On a trade wind day the Koolau Range on Oahu, or Molokai's north cliffs above Kalaupapa, give lift a pilot can fly in both directions for tens of kilometers.
4. **Downhill:** Zip lines run from the ridge stations down to leeward towns. One 1,000 m station feeds about 12 km of zip line travel with no energy input.
5. **Between islands:** Sail. The Kaiwi Channel is 42 km wide and the Alenuihaha about 48 km. A paraglider launched from 1,000 m reaches only about 10 km.

The trades blow free balloons and gliders downwind to the west-southwest and out to sea. Every return trip has to come back by ridge lift, gondola or the coast. The winning combination is gondolas or helium lifts for going up, gliders along the ridges, zip lines for coming down and sailing canoes along the coast.
