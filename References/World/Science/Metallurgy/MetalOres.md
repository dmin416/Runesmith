# Where to Find Extractable Metals

> **Earth / invent geography.** Gate with `../../Materials/Materials.md`.
> COMMON extract focus: Cu, Fe, Ag, Au, Sn, calamine (for brass). Chromite→stainless, metallic Zn retort, Kroll Ti, Al = **SPECIALTY** / invent unless a beat locks them. Public Ti stock = **adamantium** (converted Ti, `Metals.md`); mundane sponge Ti not street.

Earth ore geography and processing for a setting with alchemy and magical high heat. Alloy properties and industrial vacuum routes: `EarthAlloys.md`. Forge / barrier craft: `CraftMetal.md`. Hub: `../Science.md`.

General terms:

- **Roast:** heat the crushed ore in open air to convert sulfides to oxides.
- **Calcine:** heat without melting to drive off water or carbon dioxide.
- **Reduce:** strip oxygen from an oxide. Carbon does this for easy metals. Alchemy does it for the stubborn ones.
- **Flux:** limestone or silica added to a melt to pull waste rock into slag.
- **Retort:** sealed clay or iron vessel used when a metal boils off as vapor and must be caught.

## Ultramafic rock and its weathered soils

Dark, heavy, olive-green rock like peridotite and serpentine.

### Chromium

- **Found in:** chromite, dense black seams in layered intrusions and lumps in mountain belts where old ocean floor was pushed onto land.
- **Processing:**
  1. Crush the chromite and pick out the heavy black grains by washing.
  2. For alloying into steel: smelt the chromite with charcoal or coke in a very hot furnace. The result is ferrochrome (iron-chromium). Adding it to make stainless = **SPECIALTY** / invent (`Materials.md`); not Caldris baseline cookware or plate.
  3. For pure chromium: roast the ore with soda ash and lime in open air. This makes soluble sodium chromate.
  4. Leach with water and acidify to drop out chromium oxide.
  5. Reduce the oxide with powdered aluminum (thermite reaction) or with alchemy. Carbon alone leaves a brittle carbide.
- **Notes:** Melts near **1907 °C**. The chromate stage produces toxic yellow-orange solutions.

### Nickel

- **Found in:** pentlandite in the rock itself plus tropical red laterite soils and green garnierite ore where the rock has weathered.
- **Processing (sulfide ore):**
  1. Crush and wash to concentrate the sulfide grains.
  2. Roast to burn off sulfur as fumes and leave nickel and iron oxides.
  3. Smelt with silica flux. Iron goes into slag and a nickel-copper matte settles out.
  4. Roast the matte again to nickel oxide.
  5. Reduce with charcoal at high heat to metallic nickel.
- **Processing (laterite or garnierite):** Dry the ore. Smelt it with charcoal and limestone. The product is ferronickel, which works directly as an alloy ingredient.
- **Notes:** Melts at **1455 °C**. Meteoric iron is the only nickel source available without any of this.

### Platinum group metals (platinum, palladium, iridium, osmium, rhodium)

- **Found in:** tiny grains alongside chromite and nickel sulfides. They concentrate as placer nuggets in rivers draining these rocks.
- **Processing:**
  1. Pan the river sand. The grains are the densest thing in the pan and look like dull silver.
  2. Pick out any gold by amalgamating with mercury.
  3. Dissolve the remainder in aqua regia (nitric plus hydrochloric acid). Platinum and palladium go into solution.
  4. Add sal ammoniac (ammonium chloride) to the solution. Platinum drops out as yellow crystals.
  5. Heat the crystals hard. They decompose to gray platinum sponge.
  6. Press the sponge and hammer it hot. Platinum melts at **1768 °C** so magical heat allows true casting.
  7. Osmium and iridium do not dissolve and remain as hard black-gray flakes in the residue. They are the hardest metals in the group and need extreme heat to melt (iridium **2446 °C** and osmium **3033 °C**).
- **Notes:** Osmium powder gives off toxic fumes when heated in air.

### Magnesium

- **Found in:** magnesite and dolomite with seawater brine as a second source.
- **Processing:**
  1. Calcine magnesite to magnesia (magnesium oxide).
  2. Mix with powdered silicon or carbon in a sealed retort and heat hard.
  3. Magnesium boils off as vapor and condenses on the cool end of the retort as crystals.
  4. Remelt under a cover of salt flux to stop it burning.
- **Notes:** Burns with a blinding white flame. Water on a magnesium fire makes it worse.

## Granite country and the veins around it

### Tungsten

- **Found in:** wolframite and scheelite in quartz veins almost always beside tin (cassiterite).
- **Processing:**
  1. Crush and wash. The ore is very heavy and separates easily.
  2. Roast with soda ash to make soluble sodium tungstate.
  3. Leach with water and acidify to precipitate yellow tungstic acid.
  4. Calcine to tungsten oxide.
  5. Reduce with hot hydrogen or with alchemy to gray tungsten powder.
  6. Press the powder into a bar and sinter it just below melting. Then forge it while white hot.
- **Notes:** Melts at **3422 °C** so casting is impractical even with magical heat. Powder pressing is the standard route. Alloyed into steel it makes tool steel that holds an edge at red heat. Detail: `EarthAlloys.md` (Pure Tungsten / WC).

### Molybdenum

- **Found in:** molybdenite, a soft gray flaky mineral that looks like graphite or lead.
- **Processing:**
  1. Crush and float off the flakes in water with oil (they repel water).
  2. Roast in air to molybdenum oxide. Sulfur dioxide fumes come off.
  3. For alloying: add the oxide directly to molten steel.
  4. For pure metal: reduce the oxide with hydrogen or alchemy to powder then press and sinter.
- **Notes:** Melts at **2623 °C**. Works as a cheaper partial substitute for tungsten in steel.

### Bismuth

- **Found in:** native bismuth and bismuthinite in tin and silver veins.
- **Processing:**
  1. Native bismuth: heat the crushed ore just above **271 °C**. The bismuth melts and runs out of the rock (liquation).
  2. Bismuthinite: roast to oxide then reduce with charcoal.
- **Notes:** Very low melting point. Brittle with a pink tint and rainbow oxide skin. Combines with lead and tin to make alloys that melt in hot water.

### Lithium

- **Found in:** spodumene in coarse crystal-filled pegmatite veins.
- **Processing:**
  1. Heat spodumene to about **1100 °C**. The crystal structure changes and becomes soluble.
  2. Roast with sulfuric acid and leach with water.
  3. Precipitate lithium carbonate with soda ash.
  4. Convert to lithium chloride and melt it.
  5. Strip the chlorine with alchemy. Real-world practice uses electrolysis of the molten salt.
- **Notes:** Lightest metal. Floats on oil and reacts with water.

### Beryllium

- **Found in:** beryl (emerald and aquamarine) in pegmatite veins.
- **Processing:**
  1. Melt beryl with soda ash or limestone to break the crystal.
  2. Leach with acid and precipitate beryllium hydroxide.
  3. Convert to beryllium fluoride or chloride.
  4. Reduce with molten magnesium or by alchemy.
- **Notes:** Dust and fumes are severely toxic. Beryllium copper is a hard springy alloy.

## Silver-cobalt-nickel veins (old mining districts)

### Cobalt

- **Found in:** cobaltite, smaltite and skutterudite. Pink-crimson erythrite crust (cobalt bloom) marks the veins at the surface. Also carrollite in copper belts and asbolane in tropical laterites.
- **Processing:**
  1. Crush and sort by hand. The ores are silvery-gray and heavy.
  2. Roast in a long flue with condensing chambers. Arsenic leaves as white fumes and collects on the chamber walls.
  3. Smelt the roasted ore with flux. A cobalt-nickel-iron speiss or matte forms.
  4. Dissolve in acid and separate cobalt from nickel by precipitating cobalt oxide.
  5. Reduce the oxide with charcoal or aluminum to metal.
- **Notes:** Melts at **1495 °C**. Unroasted ore ground into glass makes blue smalt pigment.

### Nickel (vein ore)

- **Found in:** nickeline, the red-copper-looking ore.
- **Processing:** Roast to drive off arsenic then follow the sulfide route above (matte then oxide then charcoal reduction).

### Arsenic

- **Found in:** arsenopyrite.
- **Processing:**
  1. Heat the ore in a closed vessel with little air.
  2. Arsenic trioxide (white arsenic) sublimes and condenses as white powder.
  3. For metal: heat the powder with charcoal in a retort. Gray metallic arsenic condenses as a crust.
- **Notes:** All forms are lethal poison. Arsenic added to copper makes arsenical bronze.

### Antimony

- **Found in:** stibnite, gray blade-like crystals.
- **Processing:**
  1. Heat the crushed ore just above **550 °C**. The sulfide melts and runs away from the rock.
  2. Roast the melted sulfide to antimony oxide.
  3. Reduce with charcoal or by melting with iron scrap (iron takes the sulfur).
- **Notes:** Hardens lead and tin. Used in type metal and pewter.

## Lead and zinc deposits

### Zinc

- **Found in:** sphalerite and smithsonite / calamine, often in limestone. **Calamine ore = COMMON** for brass.
- **Baseline brass (COMMON):** cementation with calamine → `Brass.md` (no metallic Zn needed).
- **Metallic Zn (SPECIALTY):**
  1. Roast sphalerite to zinc oxide (smithsonite just needs calcining).
  2. Mix the oxide with charcoal in a sealed retort with a cooled outlet.
  3. Heat above **907 °C**. Zinc boils off as vapor and condenses as liquid metal.
  4. Keep air out entirely or the vapor burns back to oxide.
- **Notes:** Do not assume Zn ingots as street stock. Galvanizing / pure-Zn shop = SPECIALTY. Earth alloy encyclopedia: `EarthAlloys.md`.

### Cadmium

- **Found in:** a trace passenger in zinc ores.
- **Processing:** Cadmium boils at **767 °C** and comes off first during zinc distillation. Collect the early condensate and redistill it.
- **Notes:** Toxic. Soft silvery metal used for plating and bright yellow pigment.

### Vanadium

- **Found in:** vanadinite in the oxidized upper zone of lead deposits.
- **Processing:**
  1. Roast the ore with salt or soda ash.
  2. Leach with water and acidify to precipitate red vanadium oxide.
  3. Reduce with calcium or aluminum or by alchemy.
- **Notes:** Tiny amounts in steel add major toughness and spring quality.

### Chromium (secondary source)

- **Found in:** crocoite, bright orange-red crystals in the same oxidized zones.
- **Processing:** Dissolve in acid and precipitate chromium oxide then reduce as for chromite above. This was the original source when chromium was first isolated.

## Sediments and surface deposits

### Manganese

- **Found in:** pyrolusite and bog ore in layered sedimentary beds and swamps.
- **Processing:**
  1. Smelt the ore with charcoal in a blast furnace alongside iron ore. The product is ferromanganese.
  2. For pure manganese: reduce the oxide with powdered aluminum.
- **Notes:** Added to steel it removes sulfur and makes the steel tough and shock resistant. Pyrolusite was used to decolor glass long before it was recognized as a metal ore.

### Aluminum

- **Found in:** bauxite in tropical laterite with common clay as a low-grade source everywhere.
- **Processing:**
  1. Dissolve crushed bauxite in hot caustic soda (lye). Alumina goes into solution and iron-rich red mud settles out.
  2. Filter and cool the liquid to precipitate white aluminum hydroxide.
  3. Calcine to pure alumina (aluminum oxide).
  4. Dissolve alumina in molten cryolite (a low-melting fluoride mineral) as a solvent.
  5. Strip the oxygen by alchemy. Real-world practice uses a strong electric current.
- **Notes:** Alumina melts at **2072 °C**. Carbon cannot reduce it. Clay needs far more steps to purify.

### Titanium

- **Terra public stock:** **adamantium** (converted Ti). Mundane Ti sponge / Kroll plate is **not** street form (`Metals.md`).
- **Found in:** rutile, ilmenite, leucoxene and titanomagnetite in black beach and river sands; residual Ti-rich laterite on old wet volcanic islands (Earth / invent path). Quantities, grades and volcanic recovery: `Titanium.md`.
- **Processing (SPECIALTY invent):**
  1. Separate the black sand by magnet and washing (titanomagnetite jumps to a magnet; ilmenite is only weakly magnetic; rutile is nonmagnetic).
  2. Heat rutile with carbon in a stream of chlorine gas. The product is titanium tetrachloride, a fuming liquid.
  3. Distill the liquid to purify it.
  4. Reduce with molten magnesium or sodium in a sealed vessel with no air.
  5. Dissolve out the magnesium chloride to leave porous titanium sponge then press and melt.
- **Notes:** Melts at **1668 °C**. Hot titanium absorbs oxygen and nitrogen from air and turns brittle so the whole process must be shielded. Chlorine gas is deadly. Most mined Ti becomes pigment, not metal. Full Kroll and USGS figures: `Titanium.md`. Alloy encyclopedia: `EarthAlloys.md` (Ti-6Al-4V).

### Zirconium

- **Found in:** zircon in the same heavy sands.
- **Processing:**
  1. Heat zircon with carbon and chlorine to make zirconium tetrachloride.
  2. Reduce with molten magnesium in a sealed vessel.
  3. Wash and melt the sponge in an air-free chamber.
- **Notes:** Melts at **1855 °C**. Resists corrosion and acid. Hafnium is always mixed in and hard to separate.

## Volcanic and hot spring zones

### Mercury

- **Found in:** cinnabar in red crusts around vents and old hot springs.
- **Processing:**
  1. Heat crushed cinnabar in a closed furnace to about **600 °C** with air.
  2. Mercury vapor leaves with the exhaust.
  3. Run the exhaust through cooled condensing tubes. Liquid mercury drips out.
- **Notes:** Vapor is poisonous and builds up in the body. Dissolves gold and silver into amalgam.

## What the heat does and does not solve

- Heat alone is enough for tungsten (in powder form) and molybdenum plus platinum group metals and chromium and nickel and cobalt.
- Aluminum and titanium and magnesium and lithium and zirconium bind too tightly to oxygen for smelting with carbon. They need the alchemy to strip the oxygen off.
- Zinc and mercury and cadmium and arsenic boil off as vapor. They need sealed vessels and condensers instead of open furnaces.

## Practical placement for a map

- Mountain belts with exposed dark rock supply the chromium and nickel and platinum group metals.
- Granite uplands supply tungsten and molybdenum and tin and bismuth.
- Old silver districts supply cobalt and arsenic.
- Tropical lowlands supply aluminum and laterite nickel.
- Coastal sands supply titanium and zirconium.
