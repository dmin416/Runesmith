## Solid wire strength

Break load scales with cross-section. The 20 AWG column is annealed wire, 0.81 mm.

| Metal | Annealed (MPa) | Work-hardened (MPa) | 20 AWG break load, annealed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24k gold | 130 | 220 | ~7 kg |
| Fine silver (.999) | 140 | 300 | ~7 kg |
| Copper | 220 | 380 | ~11 kg |
| Titanium Gr 1 / Gr 2 | 240 / 345 | 450 / 550 | ~13 / 18 kg |
| Sterling silver (.925) | 280 | 500 | ~15 kg |
| 18k / 14k yellow gold | 400 / 450 | 750 / 800 | ~21 / 24 kg |
| 316L stainless | 550 | 1,200+ | ~29 kg |
| Titanium Gr 5 (6Al-4V) | 900 | 1,100+ | ~47 kg |

Fine-silver annealed tensile (140 MPa) sits in the pure-silver band in `EarthAlloys.md` (140–170). The planning anchor in `../../Materials/Metals.md` is 150 MPa. Use that anchor for mythril scaling. Use this table for jewelry wire.

24 AWG (0.51 mm) holds about 40% of the 20 AWG figure. 18 AWG (1.02 mm) holds about 1.6×.

A finished chain is only as strong as its weakest link closure. Soldered links come close to wire strength. Unsoldered jump rings pull open at a fraction of it.

### Bendability, easiest to hardest

- **Fine silver and 24k gold:** Bend with almost no springback and work-harden slowly. Too soft for thin chain. Links stretch and wear thin.
- **Copper:** Very easy to bend. Hardens after repeated bending. Tarnishes and can stain skin green.
- **Sterling silver:** Easy when annealed. Half-hard is the usual temper for jump rings and chain because it holds shape. Tarnishes.
- **14k and 18k gold:** Bend cleanly when annealed and harden quickly. 14k is stiffer and more wear resistant than 18k. Nickel white gold is especially springy.
- **Titanium:** High strength and a low stiffness modulus give strong springback, so a bend has to be overbent. Grade 1 is the workable jewelry grade. Grade 5 is barely hand-workable at jewelry gauges. It cannot be torch-soldered. Links stay unsoldered, or they are laser or TIG welded under argon. It galls on steel tools. It anodizes into colors.
- **316L stainless:** Hardens fastest and fights back hardest. It dulls ordinary cutters, so hardened flush cutters are needed. Very hard to solder. Low nickel release.

### Wear

Links and strands grind at the contact points. Fine silver, 24k and 18k wear and stretch fastest. Sterling, copper and 14k sit in the middle. Steel and titanium resist wear best.

## Twisted, braided and spiral constructions

- **Twisted wire:** 2 or more solid wires twisted around each other, often with a drill. Usually soldered or fused at the ends.
- **Braided (plaited) wire:** 3 or more strands woven over-under. Includes kumihimo and viking knit (knitted wire drawn through a drawplate).
- **Stranded cable:** many fine wires laid in a helix (7, 19 or 49 strands). Commercial beading wire is this, usually stainless.
- **Rope chain:** interlocked links set at an angle so the chain reads as a spiral.

### What twisting and braiding change

- **Flexibility:** Splitting one wire into n thinner strands of the same total cross-section cuts bending stiffness to about 1/n. A 7-strand cable is roughly 7× more flexible than a solid wire of equal metal.
- **Strength:** Break load is about 80–90% of the strands added together. Helix angle and uneven strand loading cost the rest.
- **Fatigue:** Thinner strands see less strain per bend, so braids and cables survive far more flex cycles than solid wire. Solid wire cracks where it is flexed over and over.
- **Work hardening:** Twisting hardens wire fast. Anneal after twisting before shaping curves, or the twist cracks.
- **Twist tightness:** Tight twists look better and flex more, and they stretch under load. Loose twists hold more load with less stretch.

### Metal behavior when twisted or braided

- **Fine silver:** Twists very tightly without breaking and fuses to itself without solder. The usual metal for viking knit. Soft enough to crush or stretch over time.
- **Copper:** Takes the most turns before it snaps. Best practice metal. Tarnishes unevenly in the crevices of a twist.
- **Sterling:** Twists well and cracks if overtwisted while hard. Anneal partway through a heavy twist. Fire scale and tarnish build up in the grooves.
- **14k and 18k gold:** Twist cleanly when annealed and harden quickly. 14k holds a crisp twist and resists wear better. Most commercial gold rope chain is 14k.
- **316L stainless:** The usual metal for stranded cable. Strongest and most wear resistant of this list. Hand twisting is difficult because it hardens almost immediately and snaps if pushed. Ends are crimped. It does not solder easily.
- **Titanium:** Springback makes a twist try to unwind, so the ends have to be locked by welding, crimping or wrapping. Strands gall against each other. Very light. Anodizes into colors where strands catch the light.

## Practical picks

| Goal | Pick |
|---|---|
| Easy handmade link chain | Half-hard sterling (copper for practice) |
| Hand-twisted or braided jewelry | Fine silver or sterling |
| Gold rope look | 14k |
| Durable gold | 14k over 18k |
| Strongest thin chain | 316L or titanium Gr 2 |
| Maximum strength and flexibility | 316L stranded cable |
| Lightest | Titanium |
| Lightweight colored twist | Titanium Gr 1 with welded or crimped ends |
