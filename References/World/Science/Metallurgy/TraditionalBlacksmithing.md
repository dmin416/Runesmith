A working guide for a coal-fired, hand-tool shop of the pre-industrial kind: brick forge, lever bellows, anvil on a stump, slack tub, strikers when needed. Measurements are in inches and degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in tables.

## 1. The Shop and Its Layout

A traditional shop is arranged so the smith moves in a tight triangle between **forge, anvil and slack tub**. Steel cools fast; every wasted step costs heat.

| Station | Placement | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Forge (hearth) | Against a wall with chimney or hood | Brick or stone hearth at about hip height. Firepot or clay-lined hollow over the tuyere (air pipe). |
| Bellows | Behind or beside the forge | Great double-chambered lever bellows gives steady blast. Lever handle reachable from the anvil side. |
| Anvil | One step and a pivot from the fire | Horn toward the left for a right-handed smith (horn to the off hand). Face at knuckle height. |
| Slack tub | Next to the forge, within reach | Water for cooling tools and controlling the fire. |
| Quench tubs | Near the anvil | Water or brine for simple tools; oil (kept covered with a metal lid) for blades. |
| Coal bin | Under or beside the hearth | Keep coal damp, not soaked. |
| Tong rack | Along the hearth edge | Tongs hung by reins on a rail. |
| Leg vise | On a heavy bench | The leg runs to the floor so hammering in the vise does not wreck the bench. |
| Swage block and stand | Open floor space | Can be stood on edge or flat. |
| Cone mandrel | Near the anvil | For truing rings, collars and sockets. |
| Grindstone | Near a window | Sandstone wheel turned by treadle or crank, running in a water trough. |
| Workbench | Good light | Files, scribes, dividers, calipers, squares, drills. |

## 2. Morning Startup

### 2.1 Checks before lighting

1. **Clear the firepot.** Yesterday's fire leaves clinker: a glassy, gray-black slag formed from coal impurities that collects over the tuyere and chokes the air. Lift it out with the poker or fire rake. It usually comes out as one dense lump once the fire is cold.
2. **Dump the ash.** Open the ash gate (or turn the clinker breaker) under the tuyere and drop the ash out.
3. **Sort the fuel.**
   - Coke: the gray, porous, light lumps left from yesterday. This is the working fuel. Rake it to the sides and save it.
   - Green coal: fresh coal. It must be cooked into coke before it is good for working heat.
4. **Check the tuyere.** Pump the bellows a few strokes with the pot empty. Air should come up freely and evenly.
5. **Check the bellows.** Look for cracked leather, a sticking valve flap or a loose pivot. Rub neatsfoot oil into dry leather from time to time. Grease the lever pivot.
6. **Fill the slack tub.** Top up the water. Check quench oil level and make sure the lid is at hand.
7. **Check tools.** Hammer heads tight on handles (a loose head is dangerous). Anvil face clean and dry. Tongs on the rack.

### 2.2 Lighting the coal fire

1. Place a loose ball of paper, dry wood shavings or straw over the tuyere.
2. Lay small kindling on top: dry pine splints about pencil thickness, crisscrossed.
3. Light the tinder. Give a slow, gentle blast with the bellows; too much air blows it out.
4. When the kindling catches well, lay saved coke over it. Keep a gentle blast.
5. Pile damp green coal around the outside of the burning coke, not on top. Pat it into a ring.
6. Increase air. Thick yellow-white smoke rises as the green coal around the fire begins to cook. This is normal.
7. As the smoke thins and small blue flames appear over the coal next to the fire, coke is forming. Push that coke inward onto the fire with the rake.
8. Build the fire into a mound: roughly 4 to 6 inches of glowing coke deep, with damp coal packed around the edge as a wall.

### 2.3 Fire structure

A coal fire has zones. Learning them is the difference between clean work and scaled, burnt work.

| Zone | Location | Character | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidizing zone | Low, right over the tuyere | Excess oxygen, very hot, eats steel into scale | Do not put work here |
| Heart (neutral to reducing zone) | 2 to 4 inches above the tuyere, inside the coke | Oxygen mostly consumed, hot, protective | Working heat and welding heat |
| Top layer | Upper coke | Cooler | Warming tools, slow heats |
| Outer ring | Damp coal wall | Coking fuel | Fuel reserve |

**Rule:** the work goes into the heart with at least 2 to 3 inches of hot coke under it and coke banked over it. If the steel is lying on top of a thin fire it scales heavily and heats unevenly.

### 2.4 Running the fire through the day

- **Feed coke inward** from the outer ring as the heart burns down. Push fresh green coal into the ring behind it.
- **Sprinkle water** on the outer coal with a sprinkling can (a can with holes punched in the bottom) to keep the fire from spreading and to control its size. Do not soak the heart.
- **Watch for clinker.** When the fire dulls, blows unevenly or the bellows feel like they are pushing against a wall, clinker is blocking the tuyere. Let the air off, dig down with the poker, lift the clinker out and rebuild the heart. Expect to do this every few hours with average coal.
- **Long fire for long work.** For swords and long bars, rake the fire into a trench (long and narrow) and feed it from both sides.
- **Charcoal fire:** if the shop burns charcoal, the fire must be larger and deeper. Charcoal throws sparks, burns faster and reacts strongly to the bellows. It gives a very clean fire for welding.

### 2.5 Shutting down

Stop the air. Rake the fire apart so it dies, or bank it with damp coal so coke is preserved for tomorrow. Close the quench oil lid. Hang the tongs. Wipe down the anvil face.


## 3. Shop Tools and the Anvil

### 3.1 The anvil

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Face | Hardened steel top. Main hammering surface. |
| Edges | One section of edge left sharp for setting shoulders; one section radiused (rounded) for bending without cutting. |
| Horn | Conical. For bending curves, drawing out and forming rings. |
| Step (table) | Soft, unhardened area between face and horn. For cutting with a chisel so the anvil face is not damaged. |
| Hardy hole | Square hole in the heel. Holds hardy tools (hot cut, bending fork, swages). |
| Pritchel hole | Round hole. Clearance for punching. |
| Heel | Overhanging end past the hardy hole. |
| Feet and waist | Body and base. |

**Height:** stand next to the anvil with arm hanging straight down and hand in a fist. The face should be at the knuckles.

**Weight:** a working rule is about 50 pounds of anvil for every pound of hammer. A 2.5 pound hammer works well on 125 pounds or more. Mass under the hammer is what moves metal; light anvils bounce and waste the blow.

**Mounting:** set on a hardwood stump (oak, elm) with the end grain up. Hold it down with iron staples or chain. A tightly mounted anvil rings less and hits harder.

### 3.2 Hammers

- Hand hammer, cross pein, 2 to 3 lb: general work.
- Ball pein: riveting, dishing, texture.
- Rounding hammer: one flat face, one heavily crowned face. Drawing out and finishing.
- Sledges, 6 to 12 lb: used by the striker.
- Set hammers, flatters, top fullers, top swages: held on the work by the smith with a handle and struck by the striker.

### 3.3 Tongs

Every shop needs many tongs because each one fits a stock size.

- Flat-jaw tongs: flat bar.
- Bolt-jaw tongs: jaws with a crossways offset to hold bolts, rivets and bar ends.
- V-bit tongs: round and square stock.
- Pick-up tongs: loose small pieces.
- Box-jaw tongs: jaws with side lips so flat stock cannot twist out.
- Ring (reins clip): an iron ring slipped over the reins to lock them shut on the work.

### 3.4 Other tools

Punches, drifts, hot and cold chisels, hot cut hardy, bending fork, top and bottom fullers, top and bottom swages, flatter, set hammer, heading tool, nail header, swage block, cone mandrel, leg vise, files (flat, half-round, round, three-square), rasps, scribe, soapstone, dividers, calipers, rule, square, traveler wheel (for measuring circumferences), breast drill or bow drill, grindstone, wire brush.

## 4. Safety and Striker Signals

- Eye protection always. Scale and hot flakes fly at the face.
- Wear wool, cotton, linen or leather. Synthetic cloth melts onto skin.
- Leather apron covering chest and thighs.
- **Treat everything near the forge as hot.** Black steel can still burn at 600°F. Pick up unknown metal with tongs or test by sprinkling water on it.
- Never forge galvanized (zinc-coated) metal. The fumes cause metal fume fever and can do serious harm.
- Ventilate. Coal fires produce carbon monoxide.
- Keep the oil lid at hand. If quench oil flames up, smother with the lid; never pour water on it.
- Grip hammers firmly. Check heads before use.
- When striking heavy work, protect hearing.

**Striker signals (traditional):**
- The smith taps the work with the hand hammer where the striker should hit. The striker hits there.
- The smith's light taps set the rhythm. The striker matches it.
- The smith taps the anvil face (not the work) or rings the hammer on the anvil side: **stop**.
- The smith moves the work, the striker follows the hand hammer, never guessing.

## 5. Materials

| Material | Carbon | Character | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wrought iron | Near zero, with slag fibers | Fibrous, tough, welds easily, forgiving. Work it hot (bright orange to yellow); it can split if worked too cool. | Nails, hinges, gates, anvil bodies, general ironwork |
| Mild / low carbon steel | 0.1 to 0.3% | Will not harden usefully. Easy to forge. | Hinges, brackets, tongs, frames |
| Medium carbon steel | 0.4 to 0.6% | Hardens. Tough. | Hammers, axes, punches, spring tools |
| High carbon steel | 0.75 to 1.0% | Hardens strongly. Holds an edge. Less forgiving of overheating. | Blades, chisels, springs, files |
| Spring steel (5160 type) | About 0.6% with chromium | Very tough and springy. | Swords, springs |
| Shear / blister steel (historic) | High, layered | Made by carburizing iron and folding. | Blade edges, tool faces |

Historic tools were often iron bodies with a steel edge or face forge-welded on. Steel was costly; iron was cheap.

**Reclaimed sources:** leaf and coil springs (spring steel), old files (very high carbon, can be brittle), wagon axles (medium carbon), plow points.

### 5.1 Spark test

Hold a sample against the grindstone or a coarse wheel in dim light.

| Spark pattern | Indicates |
|---|---|
| Long straw-colored streams, very few bursts, streams fade at the end | Wrought iron |
| Long streams, a few small forks | Mild steel |
| Medium streams with moderate bushy bursts | Medium carbon steel |
| Short, bright, heavily branching bursts like starbursts | High carbon steel |

## 6. Heat: Colors and Rules

Judge color in shade, not sunlight. A dim shop is a traditional shop for this reason.

| Color | Approx. °F | Approx. °C | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faint red (visible in dark) | 900 | 480 | Stop forging high carbon steel |
| Blood red / dull red | 1050 | 565 | Lowest working heat for light finishing on mild steel |
| Cherry red | 1375 | 745 | Hardening range for many carbon steels begins near here |
| Bright cherry | 1500 | 815 | Hardening heat for 1084, 1075 type steels |
| Orange | 1650 | 900 | Normal forging |
| Yellow | 1800 to 1950 | 980 to 1065 | Heavy forging, drawing out, punching |
| Bright yellow | 2000 to 2200 | 1095 to 1205 | Heavy forging of mild steel and wrought iron |
| Welding heat (bright yellow-white, surface looks wet) | 2300 to 2500 | 1260 to 1370 | Forge welding |
| White with sparks | Above welding | | Burning. Steel is being destroyed. |

**Rules:**
- Hammer hard while hot. Light finishing blows as it cools.
- Never hammer high carbon steel below a dull red. It cracks.
- Never take high carbon steel to sparkling white heat. It burns and becomes crumbly. Burnt steel must be cut off.
- Wrought iron likes high heat. Worked too cold it splits along its fibers.
- **Magnet test:** steel at the right hardening heat loses its magnetism. Keep a magnet on a rod near the forge.
- Brush scale off with a wire brush before finishing blows so it does not get hammered into the surface.


## 7. Core Forging Techniques

### 7.1 Drawing out

Makes the bar longer and thinner.
1. Heat a section to yellow.
2. Hammer across the bar over the horn or with the cross pein. This pushes metal lengthwise, not sideways.
3. Flatten the ripples with the hammer face.
4. Turn the bar 90° and repeat to keep it square.
5. **Square to round:** draw to square, knock off corners to make an octagon, then roll to round. Always reduce in that order; trying to draw round directly causes internal cracking (a hollow core).

### 7.2 Tapering and pointing

Hammer at an angle while rotating the bar 90° each pair of blows. Keep the cross section square while it tapers. Round it at the end if needed.

### 7.3 Upsetting

Makes the bar shorter and thicker at one point.
1. Heat only the area to be thickened. Cool the rest in the slack tub so it does not bulge too.
2. Stand the bar on the anvil and hammer the end straight down, or drive the hot end against the anvil face.
3. If the bar starts to bend, straighten it immediately before continuing.

### 7.4 Bending

- Over the horn for curves.
- Over the radiused anvil edge for corners.
- In the bending fork in the hardy hole for leverage.
- In the vise for crisp right angles.
- **Sharp outside corners:** upset the bend area first so the corner has metal to fill it.

### 7.5 Punching

1. Heat to yellow.
2. Set the punch and drive it about two-thirds through.
3. Pull the punch, cool it in the slack tub. Drop a pinch of coal dust into the hole; it helps the punch release next time.
4. Flip the work. A dark spot shows where the punch was coming through (the metal there cools against the cold punch tip).
5. Place the work over the pritchel hole or hardy hole. Punch through from the second side. The slug drops out.

### 7.6 Drifting

A drift is a smooth, tapered bar shaped like the final hole. Drive it through a punched hole at yellow heat to enlarge and shape the hole without removing metal.

### 7.7 Slitting

Use a hot slitting chisel to cut a slot instead of punching a hole. Then drift the slot open. This removes no metal so the area around the hole stays strong. Used for hammer eyes, guards and axe eyes.

### 7.8 Cutting off

- **Hot cut:** heat the bar, set it on the hot cut hardy, strike so the edge cuts into the bar. Cut halfway, flip, cut from the other side. Finish with a light blow so the hammer does not hit the hardy edge.
- **Hot chisel:** used on the step of the anvil, never on the hardened face.
- **Cold cut:** nick with a cold chisel and snap. Works on mild steel and wrought iron.

### 7.9 Fullering

A fuller is a round-edged tool that pushes metal aside to make a groove. Used to set up shoulders, to spread metal fast in one direction and to cut grooves (fullers) in blades.

### 7.10 Setting shoulders

Place the bar on the anvil with the shoulder point at the edge. Strike with half the hammer face over the anvil and half off. This creates a step. A set hammer struck by the striker makes a crisper shoulder.

### 7.11 Twisting

Heat the section evenly. Clamp one end in the vise. Twist with a wrench or tongs. Uneven heat gives an uneven twist.

### 7.12 Forge welding

The defining skill of a traditional shop.

1. **Clean deep fire.** Remove clinker first. Deep heart of clean coke. A dirty fire ruins welds.
2. **Prepare scarfs.** Upset the ends to be welded, then forge each into a short wedge (a scarf) so the surfaces meet with the center touching first. Scarfs should be slightly convex so air and slag squeeze out from the middle.
3. **Heat to orange-yellow.**
4. **Flux.** Sprinkle borax (or fine clean sand for wrought iron) on the hot joint. Flux melts and keeps oxygen off the surfaces.
5. **Bring to welding heat.** Return to the heart. Turn the pieces so both heat evenly. The surface looks wet and shiny, bright yellow-white, with a few sparks just starting. Not a shower of sparks.
6. **Move fast.** Knock the work once on the anvil edge to shed loose coal, lay the pieces together on the anvil.
7. **Light first blows** from the center outward to set the weld and push flux out. Then harder blows.
8. **Second heat.** Flux again, bring to welding heat, hammer to consolidate and shape.
9. **Test.** A good weld shows no seam line when brushed. A failed weld shows a dark line or separates when bent.

Types: lap weld (overlapping scarfs), butt weld, cleft weld (one piece split, the other wedged in), faggot weld (several bars bundled and welded into one block).

### 7.13 Hot riveting

1. Punch or drill matching holes.
2. Heat the rivet (which has one formed head) to orange-yellow.
3. Push it through. Support the formed head in a rivet set or on the anvil.
4. Hammer the protruding shank straight down to swell it in the hole, then dome it with a ball pein or a top rivet set. The rivet shrinks as it cools and pulls the joint tight.

## 8. Heat Treatment

### 8.1 Normalizing

Relieves stress from forging and refines the grain.
1. Heat evenly to just past nonmagnetic.
2. Let it cool in still air to black.
3. Repeat three times, each one slightly cooler (the last just at nonmagnetic).

Always normalize blades and tools before hardening.

### 8.2 Annealing

Makes steel as soft as possible for filing and drilling.
1. Heat to just past nonmagnetic.
2. Bury in dry wood ash or slaked lime and leave it overnight.

### 8.3 Hardening

1. Heat evenly to slightly past nonmagnetic (bright cherry). Thick and thin parts must reach heat together; let thin edges wait in cooler parts of the fire while the spine catches up.
2. Quench:
   - **Water or brine:** fast and harsh. Simple shapes in medium carbon steel (hammers, punches). High risk of cracking thin sections or high carbon steel.
   - **Oil:** slower and safer. Use for blades and high carbon steel. Warm the oil to about 120°F before use by dipping a hot scrap bar in it.
3. Enter the quench **straight down**, edge or point first. Move **up and down**, not side to side. Sideways motion warps blades.
4. Test hardness: a file skates over hardened steel without biting.

### 8.4 Tempering

Hardened steel is glass-brittle. Temper **immediately** after hardening, before it can crack.

1. Polish a bright strip on the surface with a stone or sandpaper so oxide colors are visible.
2. Heat slowly from the back, the spine or a thick area so the colors run toward the edge.
3. Watch colors appear in order. Quench or remove from heat when the target color reaches the working edge.
4. Alternative: bake in an oven at the target temperature for two hours, cool, bake again.

| Temper color | °F | °C | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pale straw | 400 | 205 | Scrapers, files, razors |
| Straw | 430 to 440 | 220 to 225 | Knives, small cutting tools |
| Dark straw | 460 to 470 | 240 to 245 | Punch tips, drill bits, knife edges |
| Bronze / brown | 490 to 500 | 255 to 260 | Cold chisels, hammer faces, axes |
| Purple | 520 to 540 | 270 to 280 | Daggers, spear heads, chisels for heavy work |
| Blue | 560 to 590 | 295 to 310 | Swords, springs, saw blades, spines |
| Gray-blue | 600 and above | 315 and above | Soft and springy, too soft for edges |

### 8.5 Differential hardening and tempering

- **Edge-only quench:** dip only the edge or the working end into the quench, keep the rest out.
- **Clay coating:** coat the spine with clay before heating so it cools slower and stays tougher.
- **Differential temper:** harden the whole blade, then temper the spine to blue while the edge stays at straw to bronze.

## 9. Projects

The order below follows skill. Nails and hinges teach basic control. Tools and tongs are needed to make everything after them. Weapons and the anvil come last.

### 9.1 Nails

**Stock:** 1/4 inch square wrought iron or mild steel (nail rod), several feet long so the bar is its own handle.
**Tools needed:** hammer, nail header (see 9.3), hot cut hardy or anvil edge.

**Steps (aim for one heat per nail):**

1. **Heat** about 2 to 3 inches of the bar end to yellow.
2. **Point:** draw a four-sided taper over about 1.5 to 2 inches. Hammer at an angle on the anvil face, rotate 90°, hammer, rotate. Keep the taper square and straight.
3. **Measure** the shank length (for example 2 inches for a 2 inch nail).
4. **Nick:** lay the bar over the hot cut hardy at the shank length plus about 1/4 inch extra for the head. Strike lightly on all four sides to cut a neck most of the way through. Leave enough metal that the bar does not fall off.
5. **Reheat if needed** (a skilled nailer does not need to).
6. **Drop the point into the header** hole. The shank goes through. The nick sits just above the header.
7. **Snap off:** bend the bar back and forth until the bar breaks off at the nick. The nail stays in the header with a stub sticking up.
8. **Head:**
   - **Rose head:** four angled blows, one at each corner, forming a faceted pyramid. Optional fifth light blow on top.
   - **Flat head:** straight blows down until the head is a flat disk.
   - **Clench nail head:** small flat or rose head; the shank is left soft and long.
9. **Knock out:** tap the header's side on the anvil or tap the nail point upward from below. The tapered hole lets it pop free.
10. Toss into a bin. Keep working.

**Variants:**
- **Clench (clinch) nails:** soft wrought iron, long shank. Driven through two boards; the point is bent over and hammered back into the wood or over a rove (a small square washer). Used for doors, boats and shields.
- **Spikes:** 3/8 to 1/2 inch stock, heavy heads, used in timber framing.
- **Horseshoe nails:** thin, wedge-shaped, beveled point so it curves outward through the hoof wall.

A practiced nailer historically made a thousand or more nails a day.


### 9.2 Hinges and Pintles

#### Strap hinge

**Stock:** mild steel or wrought iron flat bar, 1/4 × 1 1/4 inch, 12 to 24 inches long.
**Tools:** hammer, punch for square nail holes, mandrel rod the size of the pintle pin (often 1/2 inch round).

**Steps:**

1. **Decorative end (the far end of the strap):**
   - Taper the end to a point or a spade, or
   - Split it with a hot chisel and curl the two halves into scrolls (ram's horn), or
   - Forge a bean, leaf or ball end by necking with a fuller and spreading the tip.
2. **Taper the strap** gradually from the eye end to the decorative end (optional; looks lighter and more refined).
3. **Start the eye** at the other end:
   - Heat about 3 inches to orange-yellow.
   - Hold the bar so the end hangs over the far anvil edge by about 1/2 inch. Hammer the tip down to bend it about 90°.
   - Turn the bar over so the bent tip points up. Hammer on the tip, driving it back toward the bar. The tip curls over into a loop.
   - Keep driving it until the curl closes most of the way into a round eye.
4. **Close the eye on the mandrel:** slide the mandrel rod into the eye. Hammer the eye closed around it on the anvil or in the vise. The eye should be a smooth tube slightly larger than the pin so the hinge swings freely.
5. **True the strap.** Make sure the eye is square to the strap when sighted down its length.
6. **Nail holes:** punch 3 or 4 square holes at yellow heat, staggered along the strap. Countersink them slightly from the face side with a larger tapered punch so nail heads sit flush.
7. **Finish:** wire brush. Apply beeswax or linseed oil at a black heat (hot enough to smoke the wax).

#### Driven pintle (the pin that goes into a post)

**Stock:** 1/2 inch square mild steel or wrought iron, about 6 to 8 inches.

**Steps:**

1. Set a shoulder about 2 inches from the end with half-face blows.
2. Draw that end to 1/2 inch round (or the pin size). Length should be slightly longer than the hinge eye.
3. Heat at the shoulder. Bend the bar 90° so the round pin stands straight up. Upset the corner first if a crisp corner is wanted.
4. Draw the other leg into a long, square, tapered spike (4 to 5 inches) for driving into a wooden post.
5. Cut to length. Finish.

#### Plate pintle (for masonry or flat mounting)

Forge the pin as above but leave the base as a flat plate. Punch nail or bolt holes in the plate.

#### Butt hinge (two leaves)

1. Forge two flat leaves.
2. On the edge of each leaf, cut alternating tabs with a cold chisel (one leaf gets tabs where the other gets gaps).
3. Roll each tab around a pin-sized mandrel as with the strap eye.
4. Mesh the knuckles together. Drive the pin through. Peen both ends of the pin.

### 9.3 Tools

A smith makes most of his own tools. Medium carbon steel (axle steel, 1045, 1050) is ideal for punches, chisels and hardy tools.

#### Punch

**Stock:** 3/4 inch round or square medium carbon steel, 6 to 8 inches.

1. Draw one end to a long taper. Keep the working tip slightly smaller than the hole wanted. Tip shape: round, square or oval as needed.
2. Square off the tip face flat.
3. Chamfer the striking end (bevel the edges) so it does not mushroom and send chips flying.
4. Normalize three times.
5. **Hot punch:** many smiths leave hot punches normalized and unhardened since forge heat draws the temper anyway. If hardened, temper to blue.
6. **Cold punch (center punch, nail set):** harden the bottom inch. Temper the tip to dark straw or bronze. Let the shank go blue. The striking end must stay soft.

#### Cold chisel

**Stock:** 3/4 inch octagon or square medium-high carbon steel, 6 to 8 inches.

1. Draw one end into a wedge.
2. Grind or file the edge to about 60° included angle for cutting steel (40° for soft iron).
3. Chamfer the head.
4. Normalize. Harden the bottom inch in water. Temper the edge to bronze to purple. Head stays soft.

#### Hot cut chisel

Same as above but thinner and longer with an edge of about 30°. Fit with a handle so the hand stays away from the fire:
- Punch an eye and fit a wooden handle (like a hammer), or
- Wrap a 1/4 inch rod around the shank as a twisted handle (a withy handle).

#### Hot cut hardy

**Stock:** 1 1/4 inch square medium carbon steel.

1. Measure the anvil's hardy hole (often 1 inch square).
2. Set shoulders on all four sides about 1 1/2 inches from the end.
3. Draw that end into a shank that fits the hardy hole snugly but drops in and lifts out freely. Length should not reach the floor of the stump.
4. The shoulders must sit flat on the anvil face so blows go into the anvil body, not the shank.
5. Forge the top into a wedge with the cutting edge running crosswise to the anvil length.
6. Grind the edge to about 30° to 45°.
7. Normalize. Harden the edge. Temper to purple or blue (it touches hot metal constantly).

#### Drift

Forge a long, smooth, gentle taper with the profile of the desired hole (oval for a hammer eye, round, square, or the shape of a sword tang). The longer and gentler the taper, the easier it drives. File it smooth. Leave it soft or temper to blue.

#### Nail header

**Stock:** 1/2 × 1 1/4 inch flat bar, medium carbon steel, about 12 inches.

1. About 1 inch from one end, forge the end slightly into a rounded boss, or leave it flat.
2. Punch a tapered square hole from the top side. The top of the hole should equal the shank size of the nails to be made (1/4 inch square). The bottom should be slightly larger so nails drop through freely.
3. File or drift the hole square and clean.
4. Optionally make a second hole of a different size in the other end.
5. Harden and temper the top face around the hole to bronze. It takes repeated hammer blows from the heading.

#### Tongs (flat jaw)

The most important tool to master; every later project needs tongs that fit.

**Stock:** 5/8 inch square or 3/4 inch round mild steel. Two pieces of about 10 inches each (or work from the ends of a long bar and cut off).

**Steps for each half (both halves are forged the same way; they mirror automatically when assembled):**

1. **Jaw:** heat the end. Lay it on the far anvil edge with about 1 1/2 to 2 inches over the face. Strike half-face blows to set down a step. Flatten the jaw to about 3/8 inch thick.
2. **Boss:** rotate the bar 90° toward you. Move the bar so the area behind the jaw sits at the near edge of the anvil. Strike half-face blows to set a second step in the opposite direction. This creates the offset boss where the pivot goes. The boss should be about 1 inch long and flattened to about 3/8 inch thick.
3. **Reins:** rotate back 90°. Set a shoulder behind the boss. Draw the remaining stock into a rein about 1/2 inch round tapering to 3/8 inch. Length: 14 to 16 inches.
4. **Repeat** for the second half.
5. **Pivot hole:** punch a 3/8 inch hole through the center of each boss.
6. **Rivet:** lay the halves together with the jaws facing each other. Insert a hot rivet. Peen it while supporting the head. While the rivet is still hot, open and close the tongs repeatedly so they do not seize as the rivet shrinks. Quench while working them if they are stiff.
7. **Fit the jaws:** heat the jaws. Clamp them on a piece of the stock size they will hold. Hammer the jaws closed on it so they grip along their full length. The reins should sit about 3/4 inch apart when gripping.
8. **Test:** hold a bar out at arm's length and shake it. It should not twist or slip.

For bolt jaws, V-bit or box jaws, fit the jaws by forging them around the target stock with a fuller or over a suitable mandrel.

#### Fuller, flatter, swages

- **Bottom fuller:** a hardy tool with a round top edge. Same method as the hot cut hardy with a rounded top.
- **Top fuller:** round-edged tool with a handle, struck by the striker.
- **Flatter:** square, flat-faced tool with a handle. Placed on the work and struck to smooth large surfaces.
- **Swages:** top and bottom tools with matching half-round grooves for making round bar and smoothing.

### 9.4 Pliers

Pliers differ from tongs. Tongs hold hot work loosely. Pliers are hardened bench tools for gripping, bending wire and holding cold work. The two halves cross in a **lap joint** so both jaws lie in the same plane and close accurately.

**Stock:** medium carbon or spring steel (1050, 5160), two pieces of 1/2 inch square or 3/8 × 3/4 inch flat, about 8 inches each.
**Tools:** hammer, fuller, files, breast drill or bow drill, rivet.

**Steps:**

1. **Jaw:** on each piece, draw about 1 1/2 inches of the end into the jaw shape.
   - Flat-nose: flat jaw, square end.
   - Needle-nose: long taper to a narrow tip.
   - Round-nose: tapered round jaws for bending loops.
2. **Boss (lap joint):** behind each jaw, forge a section about 1 inch long down to half the stock thickness. On one half, thin it from the top face. On the other half, thin it from the bottom face. When laid together the bosses overlap and the jaws and handles line up flush in one plane.
3. **Handles:** draw the rest of each piece into handles about 5 inches long. Taper them toward the ends and give a slight outward curve for grip. Round the ends.
4. **Normalize** three times. **Anneal** for filing.
5. **Fit the boss faces:** file both mating boss faces flat and smooth so the halves lie together without rocking.
6. **Pivot hole:** clamp the halves together in the closed position with the jaws touching along their length. Drill through both bosses at once (3/16 to 1/4 inch). Drilling both together keeps the holes aligned.
7. **Shape the jaws:** file the gripping faces flat and matching. File cross serrations with a three-square file: evenly spaced shallow grooves across both jaws. Optional: file a notch near the pivot for gripping round stock or cutting wire.
8. **Heat treat:**
   - Heat only the jaws to nonmagnetic. Quench in oil.
   - Clean bright. Temper the jaws to bronze-purple. The handles and pivot stay soft.
9. **Rivet:** use a soft iron rivet. Insert and peen both sides with a ball pein over a rivet set. Peen until the joint is snug.
10. **Free the joint:** wrap the jaws in a wet rag. Heat just the pivot area to dull red with a hot iron or in the edge of the fire. Open and close the pliers continuously while the rivet cools so they move smoothly without play.
11. **Adjust and finish:** file any high spots so jaws meet tip to tip. Oil the pivot.

**Box joint (higher grade):** one half has a slot cut through the boss; the other half is thinned and passed through the slot before the rivet goes in. Stronger and more precise, but requires slot cutting with drill and file before final forging of the second half.

### 9.5 Hammer

**Target:** 2 1/2 lb cross pein hand hammer.
**Stock:** medium carbon steel (axle, 1045 to 1060). Steel weighs about 0.283 lb per cubic inch. A block of 1 1/2 × 1 1/2 × 4 inches is about 9 cubic inches or 2.5 lb finished; start longer (5 inches) to allow for scale and shaping. Work on the end of a long bar for a handle, or hold with heavy box tongs. A striker saves a great deal of time.

**Tools:** eye punch (oval, tapered, about 1/2 × 1 inch tip), oval eye drift, hot cut, top and bottom fullers or set hammer.

**Steps:**

1. **Mark the eye** with a center punch: centered across the width, slightly toward the pein side along the length so the face side carries more weight.
2. **Punch the eye:**
   - Heat the whole block to yellow.
   - Drive the oval eye punch about two-thirds deep. Cool the punch often. Add coal dust to the hole.
   - Flip. Find the dark spot. Punch through from the second side over the pritchel or hardy hole. The slug drops out.
3. **Drift the eye:**
   - Drive the oval drift in from one side, then the other.
   - The cheeks (sides around the eye) bulge. Leave the drift in the eye and hammer the cheeks flat on the anvil to support the eye.
   - Drifting from both sides gives an hourglass eye (wider at both ends than in the middle). This shape locks a wedged handle.
   - Final eye about 1 1/8 × 5/8 inch.
4. **Cheeks (optional):** fuller lightly on either side of the eye to define cheeks and lighten the head.
5. **Cut off** from the parent bar with a hot cut, leaving the face length.
6. **Pein:**
   - Heat the pein end.
   - Draw it into a wedge with the edge running **crosswise** to the handle direction (perpendicular to the handle).
   - Use the cross pein of another hammer, top and bottom fullers or the striker with a set hammer.
   - Round the pein edge to a smooth radius (about 1/4 inch). A sharp pein cuts work.
7. **Face:**
   - Dress the face flat and square.
   - Chamfer the edges about 1/8 inch.
   - Give the face a very slight crown so it does not leave sharp marks.
8. **Normalize** three times.
9. **Clean up:** file or grind the face and pein smooth.
10. **Harden:**
    - Heat the head evenly to nonmagnetic.
    - Quench the face end about 1 inch deep in water or brine, then the pein end. Or quench the whole head in oil (safer for higher carbon steel). Keep the eye area soft if possible.
11. **Temper:**
    - Polish bright spots on the face and pein.
    - Heat a bar to yellow, push it through the eye (or hold the head over the fire by the eye with a hot drift through it).
    - Heat flows outward from the eye. Watch the colors move toward both ends.
    - When straw-bronze reaches the face and pein, quench. The eye area goes blue, which is correct: tough, not brittle.
12. **Handle:**
    - Hickory or ash, straight grain, free of knots.
    - Orient the wood so the growth rings run in line with the long axis of the head (from face to pein).
    - Length 13 to 15 inches.
    - Carve the top to fit the eye tightly. The handle should need hammering to go on.
    - Saw a kerf (slot) about two-thirds of the way down the eye section, running along the long axis of the head.
    - Drive the head onto the handle by striking the butt end of the handle on a block (the head's inertia seats it).
    - Drive a hardwood wedge (with glue) into the kerf. Trim the excess flush.
    - Optional: drive a small steel cross wedge across the wooden wedge.
13. **Finish:** linseed oil on the handle. Oil the head.


### 9.6 Dagger

**Target:** double-edged dagger, 9 inch blade, diamond cross section, through tang with pommel.
**Stock:** high carbon steel (1075, 1084), 3/8 × 1 inch bar, about 8 inches.
**Tools:** hammer, fuller or set hammer, tongs, files, slitting chisel, drift, quench oil.

**Steps:**

1. **Point:**
   - Heat the end to orange-yellow.
   - Hammer the corners down alternately at an angle on the anvil face, flipping often, to form a centered point.
   - Keep the point on the bar's centerline.
2. **Profile:** draw the blade to its length and width, tapering gradually from about 1 inch at the base to the point.
3. **Bevels and ridge:**
   - Hammer each edge at a slight angle (about 15° to 20° off flat), working from the center line toward the edge.
   - Do one edge on one face, flip, do the same edge on the other face. Then the other edge.
   - This produces a raised center ridge with four flat bevels: a diamond cross section.
   - Hammering one edge pushes the blade into a curve away from that edge. Working both edges equally keeps it straight. Straighten every few heats.
4. **Distal taper:** the blade should get thinner toward the point. Spine thickness about 1/4 inch at the base, about 1/8 inch near the tip.
5. **Shoulders and tang:**
   - At the base of the blade, set shoulders on both edges with half-face blows over the anvil edge or with a set hammer.
   - Draw the tang to about 1/4 × 1/2 inch at the shoulder tapering to 1/4 inch round at the end.
   - Tang length: grip length (4 inches) + guard thickness + pommel length + 1/4 inch for peening. About 5 1/2 inches.
6. **Straighten** at a dull red on the anvil face or with a wooden mallet on a wooden block. Sight down the edges from point to tang.
7. **Normalize** three times. **Anneal** if filing.
8. **Rough grind or draw-file:**
   - Clamp the blade on a board. Push a file across the bevel (draw filing) to flatten and refine.
   - Leave the edges about 1/32 inch thick (about the thickness of a dime). Thin edges crack in the quench.
9. **Harden:**
   - Heat evenly to nonmagnetic. Let the thin point sit in a cooler part of the fire while the base catches up.
   - Quench point first, straight down, in warm oil. Move up and down.
   - If it warps, straighten it within the first minute out of the oil while it is still hot.
   - File test: the file should skate.
10. **Temper:**
    - Polish bright.
    - Temper to purple for a fighting dagger (tough), or bronze for a harder edge.
    - Heat the tang area and the base first; let color run to the point. Or oven at 500°F, two cycles of two hours.
    - Draw the tang back to blue so it is not brittle.
11. **Finish grinding** on the grindstone with water. Polish with progressively finer stones. Sharpen.
12. **Guard:**
    - 1/4 × 3/4 inch mild steel bar, 4 inches long.
    - Punch two small holes at the ends of where the slot will be. Hot slit between them. Drift with a tang-shaped drift.
    - File the slot until the guard slides down the tang and seats tight against the shoulders.
    - Shape the quillons: taper, round the ends, or curve them slightly toward the blade.
13. **Grip:** drill a hole through a block of hardwood, antler or bone. Shape to fit the hand. Optional leather or cord wrap.
14. **Pommel:**
    - Forge from round bar (a disk, ball or wheel shape). Punch or drill a hole for the tang.
    - Assemble: guard, grip, pommel stacked on the tang.
    - Make sure the tang end is soft (wrap the blade in wet rags and heat just the tang end to red, let cool).
    - Cut the tang 1/8 to 1/4 inch past the pommel. Peen it over with a ball pein until the whole assembly is locked tight.

### 9.7 Spear

**Target:** socketed leaf-shaped spear head, blade 9 inches, socket for a 1 1/8 inch shaft.
**Stock:** 1 inch square high or medium carbon steel (1060 to 1075), about 6 inches (work on the end of a longer bar or with heavy tongs).
**Tools:** fuller, cone mandrel or socket mandrel, swage block, drift, punch, files, quench oil.

**Planning volume:** the blade takes roughly 3 cubic inches, the socket roughly 1.5 cubic inches, the neck a little more. Start with about 6 cubic inches to allow for scale.

**Steps:**

1. **Neck:** about 5 to 6 inches from the end, fuller a groove all the way around. This marks the division between blade and socket.
2. **Blade:**
   - Heat the end. Draw it out to about 9 inches long and 1 1/2 inches wide at its widest point.
   - Shape the leaf profile: widest about one-third of the way up from the base, tapering to a point.
   - Forge a central midrib: bevel each edge from the center line out on both faces (same method as the dagger). The midrib gives stiffness.
3. **Neck shaping:** draw the area just behind the blade to about 1/2 inch round, 1 to 2 inches long.
4. **Cut off** the bar behind the socket material if working from a long bar.
5. **Socket blank:**
   - Flatten the remaining stock behind the neck into a fan: narrow at the neck, wide at the back.
   - Width at the back should equal the circumference of the socket mouth plus a little overlap. For a 1 1/8 inch inside diameter with about 1/8 inch wall: roughly 3 1/2 to 4 inches wide.
   - Length 4 to 5 inches.
   - Thickness about 1/8 inch at the mouth, a bit thicker toward the neck.
   - Trim the edges straight with a hot chisel and file.
6. **Roll the socket:**
   - Heat the fan.
   - Lay it over a half-round groove in the swage block (or a groove cut in a hardwood block). Hammer it down so both edges curl upward into a U.
   - Turn the U on its side and keep hammering the edges toward each other over the horn tip or a tapered mandrel.
   - Insert the tapered socket mandrel (a forged steel cone matching the shaft taper). Hammer the socket closed around it.
7. **Close the seam:**
   - Overlap the edges slightly and forge weld (flux, welding heat, light blows over the mandrel), or
   - Butt the edges tightly together and leave the seam unwelded. Many historical spears were made this way.
8. **True:** with the mandrel inside, rotate and hammer lightly so the socket is round and evenly tapered. Sight down the blade: the socket must line up with the blade's centerline. Correct with light blows at the neck.
9. **Rivet holes:** punch or drill two holes through the socket on opposite sides, about 1 inch from the mouth.
10. **Normalize** the whole head three times.
11. **Harden the blade only:** heat the blade evenly to nonmagnetic. Quench point first in warm oil, stopping at the neck so the socket stays soft.
12. **Temper** the blade to purple or blue. A spear takes hard impacts and should flex before it breaks.
13. **Grind and sharpen** both edges.
14. **Haft:**
    - Ash shaft, 6 to 8 feet, about 1 1/8 to 1 1/4 inch diameter.
    - Taper the end with a drawknife and spokeshave to fit inside the socket snugly.
    - Drive the head on.
    - Drill through the shaft at the rivet holes. Drive a soft iron nail or rivet through and clench or peen it.

### 9.8 Sword

**Target:** single-handed arming sword, 30 inch blade, about 2 inches wide at the base, lenticular or diamond cross section, cruciform guard, wheel pommel.
**Steel:** 5160 spring steel (tough and forgiving) or 1075/1084. Historic: shear steel or a steel-edged iron core.
**Stock:** a bar about 5/16 × 1 3/4 inches × 18 inches. The blade grows in length as it is drawn.
**Tools:** long trench fire, tongs, fullers (or spring fuller tool), set hammer, flatter, files, grindstone, tall quench tank (a pipe or trough at least 36 inches deep), wooden mallet, slitting chisel, tang drift. A striker is very helpful.

**Planning:**
- Draw the sword full size on a board or the shop floor: blade profile, guard, grip and pommel.
- Blade length 30 inches, grip 4 inches, guard about 3/8 inch thick, pommel about 1 1/2 inches.
- Balance point should end up 4 to 6 inches in front of the guard for a cutting sword.

**Steps:**

1. **Tang first:**
   - Use the long bar itself as a handle (or grip the far end with tongs).
   - About 7 inches from one end, set shoulders on both edges with a set hammer or half-face blows over the anvil edge.
   - Draw the tang: about 3/4 × 5/16 inch at the shoulder, tapering to 3/8 inch round at the end. Length about 7 inches.
2. **Point:**
   - Grip the tang with tongs.
   - Forge the point at the other end with angled blows, keeping it centered.
3. **Draw the blade:**
   - Work in sections of 4 to 6 inches per heat.
   - Keep the work in the heart of the fire. Rake the fire into a long trench and move the blade back and forth so a long section heats evenly.
   - Taper the profile from 2 inches wide at the base to about 1 inch near the point.
   - **Distal taper:** about 1/4 inch thick at the base down to about 1/8 inch near the point. Thickness tapers steadily; this is what makes a sword feel alive in the hand.
4. **Bevels:**
   - Hammer from the center line toward each edge on both faces, as with the dagger.
   - For a lenticular (lens-shaped) section, use slightly rolled blows so the faces are gently convex.
   - For a diamond section, keep a sharp center ridge.
   - Each bevel pass bends the blade away from the edge being worked. Alternate edges and faces constantly. Check straightness after every heat by sighting down the edge.
5. **Fuller (optional):**
   - A fuller groove lightens and stiffens the blade.
   - Use top and bottom fullers struck by a striker, or a spring fuller (two fuller bars joined by a spring so they clamp both faces of the blade at once).
   - Run the fuller from just in front of the guard about two-thirds of the way down the blade.
   - Fuller both faces evenly. Flatten the blade with the flatter afterward.
6. **Straighten:**
   - At a dull red, lay the blade on the anvil face and correct bends with light blows.
   - Cold, use a wooden mallet over a wooden stump.
   - Sight down the edge and the flat. Check for twist by sighting from the point toward the tang.
7. **Normalize** three times. The whole blade must reach temperature evenly; move it continuously through the trench fire. Check with the magnet along the full length.
8. **Anneal** for filing and grinding.
9. **Rough shaping:**
   - Draw-file the bevels. Grind on the grindstone with water.
   - Get the profile, bevels and fuller clean and even.
   - Leave edges about 1/16 inch thick before hardening.
10. **Harden:**
    - Build a long, deep trench fire. Pull out clinker beforehand.
    - Heat slowly and evenly, moving the blade back and forth. The thin point must not overheat while the base catches up.
    - Check with the magnet along the whole length. All of it must be nonmagnetic at the same time.
    - Plunge **point first, straight down** into the tall tank of warm oil. Move up and down only.
    - Pull it out after the oil stops boiling around the blade (but while still hot, roughly 300 to 400°F). Check straightness immediately.
    - Correct any warp within the first minute or two: clamp it against a straight bar with wedges, or twist and bend with gloved hands. Steel is briefly soft enough to correct in this window.
    - File test along the edges.
11. **Temper:**
    - Temper immediately.
    - Target: edges purple to blue (about 540 to 580°F), spine and center blue. A sword must flex and return, not shatter.
    - Methods: run the blade slowly back and forth over a long hot iron bar or a low trench fire; or bake in an oven in two cycles of two hours each.
    - Draw the tang to blue-gray (soft) so it does not snap at the shoulder.
12. **Flex test (careful):** clamp the tang in the vise with soft jaws, flex the point about 2 to 3 inches, release. It should return straight. If it takes a set (stays bent), the temper is too soft; if it cracks, it was too hard or the steel was overheated.
13. **Final grinding and polishing:**
    - Grind the bevels down to near sharp on the wet grindstone. Keep it wet; overheating the edge ruins the temper.
    - Polish with progressively finer stones, then emery and oil.
    - Sharpen to a convex edge. A sword edge is steeper and more robust than a knife edge.
14. **Guard:**
    - Stock: 3/8 × 3/4 inch mild or medium carbon bar, about 8 inches.
    - Mark the center. Punch small holes at the two ends of the slot. Hot slit between them with a slitting chisel.
    - Drift the slot with a drift shaped like the tang's cross section at the shoulder.
    - Draw both arms (quillons) into tapers. Shape the ends: rounded, flared or with knobs.
    - Optional: bend the quillons slightly toward the blade.
    - File the slot until the guard slides down the tang and seats firmly against the blade shoulders with no gap.
15. **Grip:**
    - Carve a wooden core in two halves, each with a channel for the tang. Glue around the tang, or drill and burn the tang through a single piece (heat the tang to fit).
    - Shape to the hand. Slightly oval cross section.
    - Wrap tightly with cord, then glue leather over it. Or wrap with twisted wire.
16. **Pommel (wheel type):**
    - Forge from 1 1/2 inch round. Upset slightly. Flatten into a thick disk about 2 inches diameter and 3/4 inch thick.
    - Punch or drill a hole through the edge-to-edge axis for the tang.
    - Chamfer or bevel the faces for the classic wheel shape.
    - Weigh and adjust: a heavier pommel moves the balance back toward the hand.
17. **Assembly and peening:**
    - Slide on guard, grip and pommel.
    - Check balance. Adjust pommel weight if needed (grind or file it lighter).
    - Wrap the blade in wet rags. Heat the tang end to red with a hot iron to soften it.
    - Cut the tang about 1/8 to 1/4 inch past the pommel.
    - Peen with a ball pein, supporting the sword point down on a padded block, until the whole hilt is locked tight.
18. **Finish:** final sharpening. Oil the blade.


### 9.9 Shield

The smith's part of a traditional round shield is the iron boss, grip, rim fittings and clench nails. The body is wood. An all-iron buckler is covered after.

#### Round shield with iron boss

**Boss stock:** mild steel or wrought iron sheet, 1/16 to 3/32 inch thick (14 to 12 gauge), a disk 9 to 10 inches across.
**Tools:** dividers, cold chisel, swage block or a hollow carved into a hardwood stump, ball pein hammer, rounded set hammer or raising hammer, ball stake (a rounded stake held in the vise or hardy hole), punch.

**Making the boss:**

1. **Mark** a circle on the sheet with dividers. Cut it out with a cold chisel on a soft iron plate or on the anvil step, or hot cut it. File the edge round and smooth.
2. **Mark the flange:** scribe a second circle 1 to 1 1/2 inches in from the edge. This ring will stay flat.
3. **Dish the center (hot method, easiest):**
   - Heat the disk to orange.
   - Center it over the hemispherical depression in the swage block (or the hollow in the stump).
   - Hammer the center down with a rounded set hammer or ball pein, working in spiral rings from the center outward toward the flange line.
   - Reheat as needed. Keep the flange flat by hammering it on the anvil face between heats.
4. **Dish the center (cold method, sinking):**
   - Work cold over the hollow in the stump with the ball pein, in concentric rings.
   - Mild steel and iron work-harden. When the metal stiffens, anneal it (heat to red, let it cool) and continue.
5. **Raise the dome height** until the boss is 3 to 4 inches deep in the center (the hand fits inside).
6. **Planish:** place the boss over the ball stake. Hammer lightly with a smooth, slightly crowned hammer in overlapping blows to smooth out dents and give an even dome.
7. **Flatten the flange** on the anvil face. Check it lies flat on a flat surface.
8. **Rivet/nail holes:** punch 4 to 6 evenly spaced holes around the flange.

**Grip bar:**

1. Stock: 1/8 × 1 inch mild steel strap, about 14 to 16 inches.
2. Heat the center 4 to 5 inches. Hammer it over a rod or in a swage groove to curl the edges into a half-tube shape that fits around a wooden handgrip.
3. Leave the ends flat. Taper them. Punch nail holes in each end.

**Clench nails:** make soft iron nails as in 9.1, long enough to pass through boss flange plus board plus about 1/2 inch.

**Assembly:**

1. **Board:** planks of linden (lime), poplar, spruce or pine, 1/4 to 3/8 inch thick, glued edge to edge. Cut a circle 30 to 36 inches across. Thin the board toward the rim (optional; lighter and better balanced).
2. **Hand hole:** cut a hole in the center a little smaller than the boss's inside diameter (about 4 to 5 inches).
3. **Boss:** set it over the hole on the front face. Drive clench nails through the flange holes and the board. On the back, bend each point over and drive it back into the wood (or over a small iron rove washer).
4. **Grip:** a wooden handle runs across the hole on the back. Lay the iron grip bar over it. Nail through the bar ends and the board. Clench on the front.
5. **Rim:** stitch rawhide strips around the edge (traditional), or use small forged iron rim clamps: short U-shaped strips that fold over the edge and are clenched through.
6. **Facing:** glue leather or linen over the face for strength. Paint.

#### Iron buckler (all-metal)

1. Dish a disk of 1/16 inch sheet, 12 to 14 inches across, into a shallow dome with a raised boss in the center.
2. Turn the edge over a rod (a wire edge) for stiffness: bend the outer 1/2 inch over a 1/4 inch rod around the whole circumference with a hammer, working in small steps.
3. Rivet a single grip bar across the back of the boss.

### 9.10 Anvil

Making an anvil is the hardest job in the traditional shop. It requires a large fire, heavy tools and several strikers. Two methods follow: the full traditional forged anvil and a practical block anvil.

#### Method A: traditional built-up forged anvil (wrought iron body, steel face)

This is how 18th and 19th century English anvils were made.

**Materials:**
- Wrought iron bar and scrap for the body (bundled and forge-welded).
- A steel plate for the face: 1/2 to 3/4 inch thick shear or cast steel, cut to the face size.

**Equipment:**
- A large hearth with a deep fire and strong blast (a second bellows helps).
- A hearth crane or chain hoist to move the work.
- Porter bars: iron bars forge-welded temporarily onto the work to act as handles.
- Strikers with sledges (traditionally two to four).
- Large top tools: flatters, set hammers, a heavy square punch for the hardy hole, a round punch for the pritchel hole.
- A water chute or overhead tank for hardening.

**Steps (for an anvil of roughly 150 lb):**

1. **The core (body block):**
   - Bundle wrought iron bars into a faggot. Bind with iron wire or straps.
   - Bring to welding heat in the big fire. Flux.
   - Weld the bundle into a solid block with strikers, then fold and weld again if needed until sound.
   - Forge to a block roughly 4 × 6 × 10 inches with a porter bar welded on one end.
2. **The feet:**
   - Forge four lumps of wrought iron.
   - Weld one to each lower corner of the body block, one at a time, at welding heat.
   - Shape the base into four splayed feet with a narrower waist above.
3. **The horn:**
   - Forge a separate piece of wrought iron into a long cone.
   - Scarf the thick end and weld it onto one end of the body at the face level.
4. **The heel:**
   - Forge a rectangular block for the heel and weld it to the opposite end.
5. **Hardy and pritchel holes:**
   - While the heel is at yellow heat, punch the square hardy hole with the heavy square punch (struck by strikers) and drift it square. Typical size: 1 inch.
   - Punch the round pritchel hole (about 1/2 inch) near it.
6. **The face:**
   - Grind or file the top of the body flat and clean.
   - Prepare the steel face plate: clean and slightly convex on the underside.
   - Bring the body to welding heat in the fire (top up). Bring the steel plate near welding heat separately (steel welds at a lower heat than wrought iron; it burns if heated as hot as the iron).
   - Flux both. Lay the plate on the body. Strikers hammer from the center outward to drive out flux and air.
   - This weld must be made in one or two heats. A poorly welded face delaminates later; it rings dead and the hammer bounces poorly in that area.
7. **Dress:**
   - Remove the porter bar.
   - Grind and file the face flat (see 9.11 for flattening methods).
   - Leave one section of the near edge sharp. Radius the rest of the edge, more toward the horn.
   - Smooth the horn.
8. **Hardening the face:**
   - Heat the entire anvil to cherry red (bright red) in the large fire.
   - Set it under a heavy stream of falling water, face up, so the water strikes the face directly. A tank above with a chute delivers the volume needed. The body cools slowly while the face cools fast.
   - Keep water on the face until the anvil is no longer glowing.
9. **Tempering:**
   - Let residual heat in the body rise into the face. Watch the face color on a polished spot.
   - When it reaches straw, quench the face again. Or leave the face hard and do not temper further; large anvil faces are tempered by the body's retained heat.
10. **Testing:**
    - **Rebound test:** drop a steel ball bearing from 10 inches onto the face. It should rebound to 7 inches or more on a good face.
    - **Ring test:** tap with a hammer. A clear ring means a sound face weld; a dull spot means a bad weld beneath.

#### Method B: block anvil (practical shop-made anvil)

**Material:** a solid block of medium or high carbon steel, as large as can be found and handled. A 4 × 4 × 12 inch block is about 54 lb; 5 × 5 × 14 is about 100 lb. Old ship shafts, large axles and heavy die blocks all work.

**Steps:**

1. **Hardy hole (before hardening):**
   - Heat one end to yellow.
   - Punch and drift a square hole about 1 inch, 2 inches in from the end.
   - Or forge-weld a separate heel block with a hardy hole onto one end.
   - Or skip the hardy hole and use a separate small hardy block mounted on the stump beside the anvil.
2. **Horn (optional):** forge a separate cone and weld it on at the end, or mount a separate bick (small horn stake) in the stump beside the anvil.
3. **Flatten the face** by filing, grinding on the grindstone and finishing with the scraping method (9.11).
4. **Radius** one edge.
5. **Harden the face:**
   - Heat the top 1 to 2 inches of the block evenly to nonmagnetic (bury the block in a deep fire top-down, or heat with a large fire and turn it).
   - Turn it upside down into a water tank so only the top inch or two goes under, or run a heavy stream of water on the face.
6. **Temper** with residual heat until the face shows straw, then cool.
7. **Mount** on a stump at knuckle height. Chain or staple down.

#### Quick anvil from rail

A section of railway rail stood on end (the rail head as the face) or laid on its side makes a serviceable small anvil. The head can be ground flat and hardened by heating and water quenching the head.

### 9.11 Flat Table

Two parts: (A) building a heavy iron work table, (B) making a true flat surface, either as a reference plate or on the table top itself. A true flat surface is used to check and straighten blades, flatten tool faces, true anvil faces and lay out work.

#### A. Heavy iron work table

**Materials:**
- Top: 3/4 to 1 inch thick iron or steel plate, 24 × 36 inches, or several thick plates riveted to a frame.
- Frame: 1/2 × 2 inch flat bar.
- Legs: 1 1/2 inch square bar, about 30 to 34 inches long (table height near the hip or slightly below).
- Stretchers: 3/4 inch square bar.

**Steps:**

1. **Frame:**
   - Cut four lengths of flat bar to fit under the top's edges.
   - Join corners by forge-welding at 90° (lap weld) or by setting the bars with tenons through punched holes and peening.
2. **Legs:**
   - On the top end of each leg, set shoulders and forge a square tenon about 3/4 inch square, 1 inch long.
   - Punch matching square holes in the frame corners.
   - Insert tenons through the frame from below. Peen the tops over hot, like rivets. Countersink the holes from above first so the peened heads sit flush.
   - Forge the bottom of each leg out into a small foot (upset and flatten) for stability.
3. **Stretchers:**
   - Connect the legs about 6 inches above the floor with stretchers.
   - Forge tenons on each stretcher end. Punch mortise holes in the legs. Peen in place. Or forge-weld.
   - Add a lower shelf of iron bar or wood if wanted.
4. **Top:**
   - Lay the plate on the frame.
   - Punch or drill countersunk holes through the plate and frame together. Hot rivet with countersunk rivets so the top surface stays clean.
   - Optional: punch a square hardy hole and round holes in the top for hold-downs and bending pins.
5. **Level:** set the table on the floor and shim the feet until the top is level.

#### B. Making a true flat surface (three-plate method)

This method makes a surface flat to a very high precision using only hand tools, by comparing three plates against each other. It was used to create the first precision surface plates.

**Materials:** three plates of the same size, cast iron or thick steel, about 12 × 12 inches and 1 inch thick, with ribs or a thick edge on the underside to stop flexing. Label them A, B and C.

**Tools:**
- Files and a straightedge for rough work.
- **Scraper:** an old flat file with the teeth ground off the end, forged flat and slightly curved, ground to a straight, square cutting edge, hardened and tempered to pale straw. A handle.
- **Marking compound:** a thin paste of red lead, Prussian blue or lamp soot mixed with oil.
- A soft cloth.

**Steps:**

1. **Stress relieve:** anneal all three plates after forging or casting so they do not warp later.
2. **Rough flatten:** file or grind each plate as flat as possible. Check with a straightedge in all directions (lengthwise, crosswise and both diagonals).
3. **First pairing (A to B):**
   - Spread a very thin film of marking compound on A.
   - Lay B face down on A. Slide B in small circles and figure-eights, then lift it straight off.
   - The compound transfers to the high spots on B. The high spots on A show as shiny areas where compound was wiped off.
   - Scrape the high spots on both plates with short, controlled push strokes of the scraper.
   - Change scraping direction 90° each round (crosshatching).
   - Repeat until A and B contact evenly across their faces.
   - Note: A and B now match each other, but one could be slightly convex and the other slightly concave. This is why a third plate is needed.
4. **Second pairing (C to A):**
   - Use A as the master. Scrape only C until C matches A evenly.
   - Now C and B are both copies of A's opposite. If A is convex, both B and C are concave.
5. **Comparison (B to C):**
   - Rub B against C. Any error now shows clearly: two concave plates touch only at the edges.
   - Scrape B and C, removing half the error from each, until they match each other.
6. **Rotate the cycle:**
   - Now use B as master; bring A to match B. Then compare A and C and scrape both.
   - Then use C as master; bring B to match. Compare A and B.
   - Each full rotation cuts the remaining error. Repeat until all three pairs show the same even contact.
7. **Twist check:** during each comparison, also rotate the top plate 90° and test, and test along both diagonals. Two plates can match while both are twisted in the same way; rotating them reveals it.
8. **Finish:** the plates are done when every pairing in every orientation shows small, evenly spaced contact spots all across the face (a good working standard is about 10 to 20 spots per square inch).
9. **Using the reference plate on a larger surface (the table top or an anvil face):**
   - File and rough flatten the large surface first.
   - Coat the reference plate with compound and slide it over the large surface as a spotting tool, moving it across every area in overlapping patterns.
   - Scrape the spots marked on the large surface.
   - Repeat until the whole large surface shows even spotting.
10. **Care:** keep the plates lightly oiled and covered. Never hammer on a reference plate. Use the iron work table for hammering and the scraped plate only for checking.

**Using the flat surface in the shop:**
- **Blades:** lay a blade flat on the plate to see gaps under twists or bends.
- **Tool faces:** rub a hammer face, flatter or chisel back over coarse abrasive on the plate to true it.
- **Layout:** set a scriber on a block and slide it across the plate to scribe lines at an exact height on work clamped to an angle block.

## 10. Finishes

| Finish | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Wire brushed | Brush at a dull red heat | Clean dark gray scale texture |
| Beeswax | Heat to a black heat (wax smokes on contact), rub on wax, buff | Dark, satin, rust resistant indoors |
| Linseed oil | Apply to warm metal, let it cure, repeat | Dark brown-black, durable |
| Oil blackening | Dip hot work (about 600°F) in oil | Black finish |
| Heat bluing | Polish bright, heat evenly to blue, let cool | Blue oxide, decorative, mild rust resistance |
| Bright polish | Grind, stone, emery, oil | Clean metal; must be oiled to resist rust |

## 11. Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy scale | Work in the oxidizing zone; fire too shallow | Deeper fire; keep work in the heart |
| Cracks while forging | Hammering below working heat; high carbon steel worked too cold | Reheat more often; stop at dull red |
| Crumbly, sparkling end | Steel burned (overheated) | Cut off the burnt section; it cannot be saved |
| Cold shut (a fold or seam in the surface) | Metal folded over itself during drawing or bending | Grind it out before it spreads; avoid sharp hammer edges; keep corners radiused |
| Forge weld fails | Dirty fire, not hot enough, too slow to the anvil, scarf shape wrong, scale on surfaces | Clean fire, more flux, correct welding heat, move faster, convex scarfs |
| Hollow core when drawing round | Drew round directly instead of square to octagon to round | Always reduce square first |
| Blade warps in quench | Uneven heating; sideways motion in the quench; uneven bevels | Even heat; straight plunge; up and down motion; balanced grinding |
| Blade cracks in quench | Water quench on high carbon; edges too thin; overheated | Use oil; leave edges thicker; normalize; correct heat |
| Edge chips in use | Temper too hard | Re-temper one color hotter |
| Edge rolls or bends | Temper too soft or not hardened | Re-harden and temper |
| Tongs slip | Jaws not fitted to stock | Reheat jaws and fit to the exact stock size |
| Hammer head loosens | Eye not hourglass; handle too dry; wedge too small | Re-wedge with glue; drift eye hourglass shape |
| Anvil rings dead in one area | Face weld failed beneath | Use the sound areas; a delaminated face needs re-welding |
