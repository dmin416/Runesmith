# Vacuum Forcefield Heat Processing

Forcefield turned into a vacuum around metal over a fire (or with conductive walls). How heat still reaches the piece. Pump-down costs: `Vacuum.md`. Barrier / mythril inert forge: `CraftMetal.md`. Physical chamber analog: `SteelVacuumChamber.md`.

## Premise

A forcefield can be turned into a vacuum. Metal is placed inside the vacuum field over a fire so that heat can be applied for processing. The question: how does heat reach the metal if the field is a vacuum.

## Core principle: radiation

A vacuum blocks conduction and convection, since both need matter to carry heat. Thermal radiation needs no medium, which is how the Sun heats Earth across empty space.

### How it works over a fire

- The fire and its hot gases emit infrared radiation (plus some visible light) in all directions.
- That radiation passes through the vacuum field unimpeded, as long as the forcefield itself is transparent to infrared.
- The metal absorbs the radiation, its atoms vibrate harder and it heats up.
- As the metal gets hot, it radiates energy back out. In a vacuum this is its only way to shed heat, so it cools slowly once the heat source is removed.

### What changes compared to open air

- No convection means no hot air currents carrying heat around the piece, so heating is slower and more directional. Only surfaces facing the fire get direct radiant heat and other surfaces warm by conduction through the metal itself.
- Radiant power scales with the fourth power of temperature, so a hotter fire transfers heat dramatically more efficiently. A glowing-hot fire works far better than a smoldering one.
- Dark, rough surfaces absorb radiation better than shiny polished ones. Polished metal reflects a lot of it, so a mirror-finish piece heats slowly.
- Uneven heating is a risk. The side facing the fire gets hotter than the far side, so rotating the piece or surrounding it with heat sources gives better results.

### Real-world parallels

- Vacuum furnaces are real and widely used for heat treating, brazing and sintering. They use electric heating elements that radiate onto the workpiece, exactly this principle.
- A thermos works the opposite way, using a vacuum gap to minimize heat transfer, with reflective coatings to block radiation too.

### Benefits for metalworking

- No oxygen means no oxidation or scale, so the metal comes out clean and bright.
- Gases dissolved in the metal can be pulled out, which improves purity in alloys and reduces porosity.
- Reactive metals like titanium (parent of **adamantium**) can be processed without contamination.

### Fire-specific problem

A fire needs oxygen to burn. If the vacuum field surrounds the metal only, with the fire outside it, the fire keeps burning and radiates in. If the vacuum field encloses the fire too, the fire goes out. The setup needs the fire outside the field and the field's boundary transparent to infrared (a "window" in the forcefield) for this to work.

## Variant: one-way vacuum with conductive walls

### Conductive walls change the mechanism

- Heat from the fire conducts through the wall material from the outer face to the inner face.
- The inner face then radiates across the vacuum to the metal, so the wall acts as a radiator surrounding the piece.
- Heating becomes more even than with a bare fire, since the whole inner surface glows and radiates from all sides rather than just the side facing the flames.
- If the metal touches the wall, conduction takes over at the contact points and heats it much faster there. Suspending the piece on minimal supports keeps heating radiative and even.

### One-way behavior

- If heat can enter but not leave, the metal and wall keep gaining energy and nothing sheds it. Temperature climbs until the metal passes the fire's own temperature, which real physics forbids (a heat flow that only goes one direction would break the second law of thermodynamics) but a magic forcefield can allow.
- The consequence is runaway heating. The piece needs a control such as a shutoff for the one-way property, a vent or a timed field collapse to stop it from melting or vaporizing.
- Without a shutoff the metal can only cool by moving to a two-way field or by touching something conductive outside the vacuum.

### Practical setup for processing metal

- Wall material with high thermal conductivity (copper or graphite analogs) moves fire heat to the inner face quickly.
- A dark, rough inner wall surface radiates and absorbs better than a polished one.
- Radiant transfer from wall to metal scales with the fourth power of temperature, so the metal heats fast once the wall is glowing.
- The sealed vacuum keeps the metal free of oxidation, so it stays clean through heat treatment, annealing or brazing.

### Effects of the one-way property on specific processes

- Annealing and tempering need controlled soak temperatures, so a one-way field needs an adjustable cutoff.
- Melting and casting work well, since the metal reaches fire temperature or beyond with no loss.
- Quenching becomes difficult, since heat cannot leave. The field must flip to two-way or collapse to let the piece cool or be moved.
