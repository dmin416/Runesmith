### Processing

1. **Melt copper** in a graphite or clay crucible at about 1150-1200 °C under a charcoal cover or borax flux to block oxidation.
2. **Add tin** after the copper is fully liquid. Preheat the tin to drive off moisture. Stir with a graphite or dry wooden rod.
3. **Add lead or zinc last** if the recipe calls for them. Zinc goes in just before pouring to limit fuming.
4. **Deoxidize** with phosphor copper master alloy (Cu-15P) at roughly 0.02-0.05% P of the melt. Retained phosphorus is what makes phosphor bronze.
5. **Skim and pour** at 1100-1200 °C. Thin sections need the higher end.
6. **Cold working:** bronzes under about 10% Sn can be hammered cold. They work-harden quickly. Anneal at 500-700 °C between passes. Quenching does not harden tin bronze; hammering does.
7. **High-tin bronze (20-23% Sn):** brittle when slow-cooled. Traditional Korean and Indian method: hot-forge at 600-700 °C then water-quench to keep the tough beta phase.
8. **Aluminum bronze heat treatment (C95400):** solution treat at 870-900 °C, water quench, temper at about 620 °C.
9. **Historical smelting:** copper ore smelted with cassiterite (tin oxide) and charcoal in one furnace, or copper ingots alloyed with metallic tin in crucibles.

### Phase Behavior

| Zinc content | Structure | Workability |
|---|---|---|
| Up to ~35% | Alpha | Cold-workable, deep-drawable |
| ~35-45% | Alpha-beta (duplex) | Hot-work at 600-800 °C; poor cold working |
| Over ~45% | Beta | Brittle; limited use |

### Processing

1. **Melt copper** under charcoal at about 1100 °C.
2. **Lower temperature** to about 1050 °C.
3. **Plunge preheated zinc** below the surface with tongs. Add 2-5% extra zinc to cover vaporization loss.
4. **Pour quickly** at 1000-1100 °C. Zinc oxide fume causes metal fume fever; strong ventilation required.
5. **Cementation method (pre-1700s):** crucible packed with granulated copper, powdered calamine (zinc carbonate) or roasted zinc oxide and charcoal. Lidded and heated to about 1000 °C. Zinc vapor diffuses into the solid copper. Maximum reachable is about 28-33% Zn. The resulting brass is then melted and cast.
6. **Annealing:** 425-750 °C depending on thickness and target softness.
7. **Stress relief:** 250-300 °C for 1 hour to prevent season cracking (stress corrosion from ammonia).

### Processing

- **Copper smelting (sulfide ores, chalcopyrite):** roast to drive off sulfur, smelt with silica flux at about 1200 °C to copper matte (Cu₂S-FeS), blow air through matte in a converter to blister copper (98-99%), fire refine by poling, then electrolytic refining to 99.99% cathode copper.
- **Copper smelting (oxide ores, malachite and azurite):** direct reduction with charcoal at about 1100 °C.
- **Beryllium copper:** solution anneal at 760-800 °C, water quench, age at 315 °C for 2-3 hours. Reaches roughly HRC 38-45. Beryllium dust is highly toxic; dry grinding requires extraction.
- **Chromium copper:** solution treat at 980-1000 °C, quench, age at 450-500 °C for 2-4 hours.
- **Cupronickel:** melt at 1250-1300 °C (nickel raises the liquidus). Deoxidize with manganese or magnesium.
- **Copper-tungsten:** press and sinter tungsten powder into a porous skeleton then infiltrate with molten copper at about 1200 °C.
- **Shakudo and shibuichi patina:** pickle clean then boil in rokushō solution (copper acetate with copper sulfate). Shakudo turns blue-black; shibuichi turns silver-gray to olive.

### Niello (Decorative Black Inlay)

Sulfides of silver, copper and lead. Traditional recipe is roughly 1 part Ag : 2 parts Cu : 3 parts Pb fused with excess sulfur, ground to powder, fused into engraved recesses then filed and polished flush.

### Parts-by-Weight Ratios

| Alloy | Ag : Cu |
|---|---|
| Britannia | 23 : 1 |
| Sterling | 37 : 3 |
| Coin silver | 9 : 1 |
| 800 silver | 4 : 1 |

### Processing

1. **Melt** in a graphite crucible or on a charcoal block at 1000-1100 °C under borax or boric acid flux. Sterling liquidus is about 893 °C.
2. **Alloy order:** melt the silver first then add copper.
3. **Pour** into a preheated, oiled or sooted steel ingot mold.
4. **Anneal** sterling at 600-650 °C for a few minutes, then water quench.
5. **Precipitation hardening (sterling):** solution treat at 745 °C for 15-30 minutes, water quench, age at 300 °C for 30-60 minutes.
6. **Argentium hardening:** heat to 300 °C for 45 minutes, air cool. No firescale forms.
7. **Firescale control:** coat with boric acid in alcohol before heating, or remove afterward by pickling in warm 10% sodium bisulfate or dilute sulfuric acid.
8. **Depletion silvering (low-silver alloys):** heat to oxidize surface copper, pickle, repeat several cycles to leave a silver-rich skin.
9. **Refining:** cupellation (melt with lead on a bone-ash cupel at about 1000 °C; lead oxidizes and soaks into the cupel, leaving silver and gold). Separate gold from silver by parting in nitric acid.

Dental classification: high noble is 60% or more noble metal with at least 40% gold; noble is 25% or more noble metal; predominantly base is under 25%.

### Processing

1. **Melt** in a graphite or fused-silica crucible under a light borax flux. 18K yellow melts at about 900-930 °C; pour at 1000-1050 °C.
2. **Alloy order:** gold and silver first, copper next, zinc last (zinc fumes off if added early).
3. **Annealing:** 600-700 °C.
   - Yellow: water quench once the red glow fades.
   - Red and rose: water quench immediately from above 600 °C. Slow cooling below about 410 °C causes AuCu ordering and brittleness.
   - Nickel white: anneal 700-750 °C and quench; prone to stress cracking, so anneal often during working.
4. **Age hardening (14K-18K):** 250-350 °C for 1-2 hours.
5. **Depletion gilding (mise en couleur) for tumbaga:** heat to oxidize surface copper, pickle in plant acid or alum-salt solution, burnish, repeat until the surface is gold-rich.
6. **Refining:**
   - Cupellation for base metals.
   - Parting with nitric acid ("aqua fortis") to remove silver; requires at least 3 parts silver per 1 part gold ("inquartation").
   - Miller process: chlorine bubbled through molten gold to 99.5%.
   - Wohlwill electrolysis to 99.99%.
   - Aqua regia (3 HCl : 1 HNO₃) dissolution then reduction for small-scale refining.

### Steelmaking Processes

1. **Cementation (blister steel):** wrought iron bars packed in charcoal inside sealed stone chests, held at about 1000 °C for 7-10 days. Carbon diffuses in to about 0.5-1.5%. Surface blisters from slag gas give the name.
2. **Crucible steel (Huntsman / wootz):** iron and charcoal (or blister steel) sealed in clay crucibles, melted at 1200-1400 °C, slow cooled. Wootz is forged at low heat (650-850 °C) to keep the carbide banding.
3. **Bessemer:** air blown through molten pig iron burns out carbon and silicon in 15-20 minutes.
4. **Open hearth (Siemens-Martin):** regenerative furnace refines pig iron and scrap over 8-12 hours.
5. **Basic oxygen furnace:** pure oxygen lanced into molten pig iron with 20-30% scrap; about 40 minutes per heat.
6. **Electric arc furnace:** up to 100% scrap melted by graphite electrodes.
7. **Ladle alloying:** alloy content set by adding ferroalloys (FeMn, FeSi, FeCr, FeV, FeMo) after the melt.

### Surface Hardening

- **Pack carburizing:** part buried in charcoal with 10-20% barium or sodium carbonate energizer, sealed in a steel box, held at 900-950 °C. Roughly 0.8-1.2 mm case in 8 hours at 925 °C. Quench after.
- **Nitriding:** ammonia atmosphere at 500-550 °C for 10-80 hours. No quench needed.
- **Pattern welding (Damascus):** stack alternating 1095 and 15N20 (or 1084 and 15N20), forge weld at 1200-1260 °C with borax flux, fold or twist, etch in ferric chloride to show layers.

### Processing

**Bloomery (solid-state reduction):**
1. Roast ore to drive off water and CO₂, crush to pea size.
2. Preheat a clay shaft furnace with charcoal.
3. Charge alternating layers of charcoal and ore at roughly 1 : 1 by weight per charge.
4. Air blast by bellows; furnace interior reaches about 1200-1300 °C. Ore reduces to spongy iron without fully melting. Slag runs out as liquid.
5. Extract the bloom, compact it by hammering while hot to squeeze out slag.
6. Yield is commonly 10-30% of ore weight as bloom.

**Blast furnace (liquid iron), per tonne of hot metal (modern):**

| Input | Amount |
|---|---|
| Iron burden (sinter, pellets, lump ore) | 1.5-1.7 t |
| Coke | 0.30-0.40 t |
| Pulverized coal injection | 0.15-0.20 t |
| Flux (limestone, dolomite) | 0.10-0.20 t (mostly in sinter) |
| Hot blast air | ~1.3 t |
| **Output slag** | 0.25-0.30 t |

Historical charcoal blast furnaces used roughly 1-1.5 t charcoal per tonne of iron.

**Puddling (pig iron to wrought iron):** pig iron melted in a reverberatory furnace lined with iron oxide. Stirred so oxygen burns out carbon. The iron becomes pasty as it purifies, is gathered into balls and hammered (shingled) to expel slag, then rolled.

**Cast iron melting:** cupola furnace charged with pig iron, scrap, coke (roughly 1 : 8 to 1 : 12 coke to metal by weight) and limestone. Pour at 1350-1450 °C.

**Ductile iron treatment:** add magnesium ferrosilicon at about 1-2% of melt weight using the sandwich or tundish-cover method at about 1450-1500 °C. Inoculate with 0.2-0.5% FeSi immediately after. Pour within 10-15 minutes before magnesium fades.

**Malleable iron:** cast as white iron, anneal at 900-970 °C for 10-20 hours, slow cool through 760-700 °C for ferritic (black-heart) structure.

**Austempered ductile iron (ADI):** austenitize ductile iron at 850-950 °C, quench into a salt bath at 230-400 °C, hold 1-2 hours, air cool.

### Processing

1. **Smelting:** cassiterite (SnO₂) reduced with charcoal at 1100-1250 °C: SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO.
2. **Liquation refining:** heat crude tin just above 232 °C on an inclined hearth. Pure tin drains away; higher-melting iron and arsenic compounds stay behind.
3. **Poling:** stir molten tin with green wood poles to oxidize remaining impurities into dross.
4. **Pewter alloying:** melt tin in a stainless or cast-iron pot at about 300 °C, add copper as a Sn-Cu master alloy (pure copper will not dissolve at this temperature), then antimony.
5. **Pewter casting:** pour at 300-330 °C into preheated bronze, steel or high-temperature silicone (RTV) molds. Skim dross before each pour.
6. **Tin pest prevention:** antimony, bismuth or lead in the alloy blocks the low-temperature transformation to gray tin.
7. **Soldering flux:** rosin for electronics; zinc chloride or ammonium chloride for plumbing and sheet metal.

### Processing

1. **Sources:** titaniferous magnetite slag, vanadinite, carnotite, petroleum residues and fly ash.
2. **Salt roasting:** roast with sodium carbonate or sodium chloride at 800-900 °C to form water-soluble sodium vanadate.
3. **Leach** in water, precipitate ammonium metavanadate (NH₄VO₃) with ammonium salts.
4. **Calcine** to vanadium pentoxide (V₂O₅) at about 500 °C.
5. **Aluminothermic ferrovanadium:** V₂O₅ + aluminum powder + iron scrap + lime flux, ignited in a refractory-lined vessel.
   - Reaction: 3V₂O₅ + 10Al → 6V + 5Al₂O₃.
   - Stoichiometric ratio: about 1 kg V₂O₅ : 0.49 kg Al.
   - Iron scrap amount sets the final V % (FeV50 vs FeV80).
6. **V-Al master alloy:** same aluminothermic reaction with excess aluminum and no iron.
7. **Pure vanadium metal:** calcium reduction of V₂O₅ or aluminothermic reduction followed by electron-beam refining.
8. **In steel:** FeV added to the ladle at 0.05-0.3% V. Vanadium carbide and nitride precipitates pin grain boundaries, refine grain size and add wear resistance.

### Processing

2. **Electrolytic route (most modern zinc):** leach ZnO in sulfuric acid, purify with zinc dust to precipitate Cu, Cd and Co, then electrowin onto aluminum cathodes at about 3.3-3.5 V.

|---|---|

| NaK-56 | 11 : 14 |

### Processing

1. **Downs cell (sodium production):**
   - Electrolyte: NaCl mixed with CaCl₂ (often with BaCl₂) to lower melting point to about 580-600 °C.
   - Iron cathode, graphite anode, 6-7 V.
   - Molten sodium rises and is collected; chlorine gas is a by-product.
   - Mass balance: 2.54 kg NaCl → 1 kg Na + 1.54 kg Cl₂.
2. **Handling:** store under dry mineral oil or argon. Contact with water produces hydrogen and heat that can ignite.
3. **NaK preparation:** clean sodium and potassium pieces placed together under dry argon or mineral oil at room temperature. Liquid alloy forms on contact. NaK develops a potassium superoxide crust on air exposure that can detonate when scraped or cut.
4. **Sodium amalgam preparation:** small sodium pieces dropped one at a time into mercury under mineral oil or inert gas. Strongly exothermic with flashes; add slowly.
5. **Sodium-lead:** sodium added to molten lead under inert cover at about 400 °C.
6. **Al-Si modification:** metallic sodium (vacuum-packed in aluminum foil) or sodium fluoride salts plunged into molten Al-Si alloy just before pouring. Effect fades within 15-30 minutes.
