## Where Titanium Is Found

Titanium is the 9th most abundant element in Earth's crust. Crustal averages run about **0.6 to 0.7 wt% TiO₂**, which equals roughly **0.4 wt% titanium metal**. It never occurs native. It sits locked in oxide minerals:

- **Ilmenite** (FeTiO₃): about 90% of world titanium mineral consumption. Pure ilmenite is about **53% TiO₂**. Commercial concentrates run roughly **35 to 65% TiO₂** depending on weathering and impurities. It comes from hard-rock deposits in gabbro and anorthosite (Norway, Canada, China) and from heavy mineral sands.
- **Rutile** (TiO₂): the high-grade mineral at about 95% TiO₂. It mostly comes from beach and dune sands (Australia, Sierra Leone, South Africa).
- **Leucoxene**: weathered, iron-depleted ilmenite. Grade sits between ilmenite and rutile.
- **Titanomagnetite** (Fe₃O₄ with Ti in solid solution): the common titanium-iron oxide in basalt and volcanic sands. Lower Ti grade. Usually mined as iron ore with titanium recovered in slag.

**Deposit types:**
- **Heavy mineral sands (placers):** erosion breaks down igneous rock. Waves and wind sort the dense Ti grains into concentrated layers on coastlines.
- **Hard rock (magmatic):** ilmenite and titanomagnetite layers in mafic intrusions.
- **Residual laterite:** tropical weathering leaves immobile Ti behind while other elements leach out (see the volcanic section).

**Major producers:** China (largest producer and consumer), Mozambique, Australia, South Africa, Madagascar, Senegal, Kenya. Canada and Norway are hard-rock ilmenite sources rather than sands.

### Pigment vs metal

Most "titanium mining" never becomes metal. Concentrates go through the **chloride or sulfate process** to make **TiO₂ white pigment** for paint, plastics and paper. Neither route passes through sponge. The chloride route shares only its front end with metal making: both start by chlorinating concentrate to TiCl₄. Pigment plants then oxidize TiCl₄ back to pure TiO₂. Metal plants reduce it instead. Only a small fraction of mined titanium takes the metal path.

### Why the metal is expensive

Ore supply is not the bottleneck. Refining is. The **Kroll process** chlorinates concentrate to TiCl₄ and then reduces it with molten magnesium in slow batches at high temperature. The product is porous "sponge" that must be remelted into ingot. That chain is why titanium metal costs far more than steel despite titanium being common.

## Grade Contrast

| Material | Typical TiO₂ |
|---|---|
| Average crust | ~0.6 to 0.7% |
| Bulk basalt | ~1 to 3% (ocean-island basalts up to ~4%) |
| Volcanic titanomagnetite ironsand (Taranaki, NZ) | ~8% |
| Strongly weathered tropical laterite on basalt | can exceed 10% in the most weathered soils |
| Ilmenite, pure mineral | ~53% |
| Ilmenite concentrate | ~35 to 65% |
| Rutile concentrate | ~95% |
| Lunar high-Ti mare basalt (aside, not an Earth source) | up to ~13% |

Bulk basalt is background rock, not ore. Economic grade comes from **mineral concentrates** produced by natural sorting or weathering.

## Farming It from Volcanoes

**Yes, through the rock and its breakdown products. No through gas or eruptions.**

### Not workable: lava and volcanic gas

- **Fresh lava/basalt:** 1 to 3% TiO₂ is too low to compete with concentrated deposits.
- **Fumaroles and volcanic gas:** Titanium is lithophile and refractory. It does not dissolve or travel in volcanic fluids, so it stays in the solid rock. Volcanic gases carry trace copper, zinc, lead and gold. Titanium is not among them in useful quantities.

### Workable path 1: black sand placers

When volcanic rock erodes, dense titanomagnetite and ilmenite grains concentrate on beaches and in river beds.

- **Taranaki, New Zealand:** ironsands eroded from Mount Taranaki contain titanomagnetite at roughly 8% TiO₂. They are dredged and smelted into steel at NZ Steel. Titanium reports to the slag.
- Similar black sands exist in Hawaii, Iceland, Indonesia, Japan and the Philippines.
- Placers on mature coastlines can also hold true **ilmenite** where the source rocks are older and more evolved.

### Workable path 2: residual laterite enrichment

On an **old, wet, tropical volcanic island**, intense chemical weathering of basalt strips out silica, calcium, magnesium, sodium and potassium. Titanium is essentially immobile in surface water, so it stays behind and concentrates in the residual soil.

- Aluminum and ferric iron often stay too, producing bauxitic or ferruginous laterite. Where iron is mobilized under waterlogged or reducing conditions, titanium enriches further.
- The most weathered soils on older Hawaiian islands (Kauai type) can exceed 10% TiO₂. Treat that as an upper-end figure for long-weathered uplands, not a typical value.
- The richest zones are the upper laterite horizons and ironstone crusts on stable, gently sloping uplands that have weathered for a long time.
- Laterite can also feed placers: streams cutting through it carry the heavy Ti grains to the coast.
- **Setting cross-reference:** see `../../Geography/VolcanicIslandResources.md` (Ti-rich laterite, never mined there).

So volcanoes produce the source rock. **Erosion** sorts it into black sand. **Weathering** leaves residual Ti in laterite. Both are real recovery paths. Pulling titanium directly from an active volcano is not.

## Preindustrial / Setting Notes

- **Black sand and laterite are easy to gather by hand.** Panning and sluicing concentrate the heavy grains by density.
- **Magnetic sorting splits the minerals.** Titanomagnetite is strongly magnetic and jumps to a lodestone or magnetized iron. Ilmenite is only weakly magnetic and mostly stays behind with the gravity concentrate. Rutile is nonmagnetic. A lodestone pass followed by panning gives a crude three-way separation.
- **Direct uses without metal:** ground Ti-rich concentrate or roasted residue can serve as a white or off-white pigment base. Smelting titanomagnetite gives usable iron plus a Ti-rich slag that a later industry could process.
- **Metal still needs heavy chemistry.** Sponge requires chlorination to TiCl₄ and reduction with magnesium or sodium in an inert atmosphere. A preindustrial society cannot do that without a magic or alchemical equivalent for the chlorination and oxygen-free reduction steps.
