## Earth research (reference only)

### Part 1: Core abilities

#### Weight by lifting

The brain has no direct weight sensor. It infers weight from the effort needed to lift and hold an object: the force commands sent to muscles plus feedback from muscle spindles and tendon sensors. Side by side, most people detect a difference of about 2 to 3 percent (Weber's law), so 100 g vs 103 g is noticeable. Naming an absolute weight cold is much harder, with untrained errors of 10 to 20 percent being common. Expectations distort it too. In the size-weight illusion a small object feels heavier than a large one of identical mass because the brain prepared less force for it.

#### Length, area and volume

Vision handles length well. Perceived length scales almost one-to-one with real length, with discrimination around 2 to 4 percent. Area and volume get compressed. Perceived volume grows at roughly the 0.6 power of actual volume, so an object doubled in every dimension holds 8 times as much yet looks only 3 to 4 times bigger. This is why a tall narrow glass looks fuller than a short wide one holding the same amount.

#### The mind's eye

Mental images preserve scale. In Kosslyn's classic experiments, scanning a longer distance across an imagined map took proportionally longer, meaning imagery behaves like a scaled sketch. Vividness varies a lot between people. Even people with aphantasia (no voluntary imagery) can estimate sizes from stored knowledge rather than pictures.

#### How highly accurate people do it

- **Anchors:** body units (finger width about 2 cm, hand span about 20 cm) and memorized references (a stick of butter at 113 g, a U.S. dollar bill at 6.14 in).
- **Feedback volume:** deli slicers, bakers portioning dough and carpenters eyeballing cuts land within a few percent because they have checked against a scale or tape thousands of times.
- **Experience alone is not enough:** orthopaedic surgeons' length estimates showed no improvement with years of practice. Repetition without measured feedback does not calibrate.
- **Domain specificity:** a butcher precise with meat is usually not precise with lumber.
- **Fine touch:** fingertips feel surface features of a few micrometers. Teeth reliably detect foil 8 micrometers thick between them.

#### Reproducing it in reality

Drawing a 10 cm line or pouring 250 ml freehand combines a remembered standard with motor memory. In a study of 101 veterinary staff working with lengths from 3 to 120 mm, median error was 24 percent when drawing a requested length and 33 percent when judging a line by eye. Producing a length with the hand beat judging one by sight. Errors pull toward the middle: small amounts get overestimated while large amounts get underestimated.

### Part 2: Processing vs experience scales

**Processing** = the fixed sensory and neural hardware (receptor spacing, Weber fractions, tremor).
**Experience** = calibration built from feedback against real measurements.

#### Scale A: Levels of measurement grasp

| Level | Name | What the person can do | Typical error | Driven by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Unanchored | Guess with no reference | 30 to 100%+ | Neither |
| 1 | Relative | Tell which is longer or heavier side by side | 2 to 5% | Processing |
| 2 | Anchored | Estimate using body units and known objects | 15 to 30% | Mostly experience |
| 3 | Calibrated | Estimate in a domain checked against tools thousands of times | 3 to 10% | Experience |
| 4 | Embodied | Produce the amount with the hands without a tool (cut, pour, portion, draw) | 1 to 5% in domain | Experience over processing |
| 5 | Instrument-extended | Work below the hand's native limit with magnification, scale bars and motion scaling | under 0.1 mm absolute | Processing amplified by tools plus heavy training |

#### Scale B: Measure by measure

| Measure | Built-in limit | Untrained absolute error | Trained ceiling | Processing / Experience |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Length, side-by-side comparison | 2 to 4% | n/a | about 2% | 85 / 15 |
| Edge alignment (vernier) | 3 to 5 arcseconds, ideal conditions | n/a | improves with practice | 75 / 25 |
| Absolute length (naming mm or cm) | none | 25 to 35% | 3 to 5% in trades | 20 / 80 |
| Distance across a room or field | none | 20 to 30% | 5 to 10% (golfers, surveyors) | 30 / 70 |
| Angle | none | 5 to 10 degrees | 1 to 2 degrees | 30 / 70 |
| Area | compressed perception | 30 to 50% | about 10% | 25 / 75 |
| Volume or liquid | strongly compressed (about 0.6 power) | 30 to 50% | 5 to 10% (free-pour bartenders, bakers) | 20 / 80 |
| Weight, side by side | 2 to 3% | n/a | about 2% | 80 / 20 |
| Weight, absolute | none | 10 to 20% | 2 to 5% (deli, butchers) | 25 / 75 |
| Thickness by bite | about 8 micrometers, some people lower | n/a | n/a | 90 / 10 |
| Thickness or texture by fingertip | a few micrometers | n/a | machinists feel about 0.01 mm with a gauge | 70 / 30 |

Splits are synthesized across the cited research. No single study measures this ratio directly.

**Pattern:** comparing two things is hardware. Naming an absolute amount is learned. The compressed dimensions (area and volume) need the most calibration because perception actively misreports them.

### Part 3: Precision at small scales

#### Imagining a millimeter

Mental imagery has a grain. Kosslyn found that when people imagine a small animal next to a large one, they take longer to "see" the small animal's details, as if the image has limited resolution. They mentally zoom in to inspect it. Raw imagery cannot reliably separate 1.0 mm from 1.2 mm. The millimeter is grasped through anchors:

- Human hair: about 0.05 to 0.1 mm
- Printer paper: about 0.1 mm
- Mechanical pencil lead: 0.5 or 0.7 mm
- Credit card thickness: 0.76 mm
- U.S. dime thickness: 1.35 mm

**Naked-eye limits at reading distance (25 cm):**
- Resolving two separate points: about 1 arcminute, roughly 0.07 to 0.1 mm.
- Detecting misalignment of two edges (hyperacuity): 3 to 5 arcseconds under ideal conditions, roughly 4 to 6 micrometers. This beats the spacing of the eye's own cone cells (about 30 arcseconds). The brain interpolates across many receptors to get there.

Hyperacuity is a detection ability. It tells someone that two edges are misaligned. It does not let them name the gap in micrometers.

#### Magnifying glass and surgical loupes

- Loupes run from 2.5x to 4.5x.
- Visible detail shrinks to roughly 0.015 to 0.04 mm.
- The hand does not improve. Tremor that was invisible becomes visible.
- Depth of field narrows with a fixed working distance, so the brain loses some depth cues.
- Loupes match the operating microscope for some procedures. Hand surgeons use them for nearly all cases.

#### Light microscope

- Magnification up to about 1000x. The diffraction limit of light caps resolution near 0.2 micrometers no matter how much magnification is added.
- The viewer has no felt sense of scale. Students judge size by how big something looks in the picture (visual size bias) instead of reading the scale bar.
- Given a scale bar, field-of-view width or a known object, students estimate size reasonably well.
- Experienced microscopists reason in reference units: a red blood cell at about 7 to 8 micrometers, the known field diameter of each objective.

#### Surgery under magnification

**What gets operated on:**
- Supermicrosurgery joins vessels of roughly 0.3 to 0.8 mm diameter with 11-0 or 12-0 sutures.
- A common training model uses mouse tail arteries averaging 0.22 mm wide under a 13x microscope, with forceps tips of 0.15 mm.
- Stitches in that model are spaced one to two vessel-wall thicknesses apart.

**What the hand does:**
- Physiological tremor oscillates at about 6 to 12 Hz.
- During the most delicate part of retinal surgery, surgeon tremor measured 30 to 38 micrometers RMS.
- In a retinal pointing task, surgeons' 3D positioning error was 144 micrometers RMS unconstrained, 258 in an artificial eye and 285 in a live rabbit eye, with maximums near 0.7 mm.
- Resting the wrist on a gel support cut tremor by a factor of 2.67.
- An experienced surgeon transmitted less tremor into tissue than trainees.

**What magnification does to outcomes:**
- In nerve and vessel repairs, errors were highest with the unaided eye, lower with loupes and lowest with the operating microscope.

**What robots add:**
- Motion scaling, more than tremor filtering, drives the accuracy gain. Tremor filtering alone at 1:1 matched plain laparoscopy.
- At 5:1 scaling a 1 mm target becomes a 5 mm hand movement.
- Robotic retinal systems report tremor reduced below 10 micrometers.

**The core gap:** under a microscope the eye can see errors around 5 micrometers while the hand moves with 30 to 150 micrometers of error. Vision outruns the hand by more than an order of magnitude. The limiting factor shifts from seeing to moving.

#### How the brain processes the magnified view

1. **No magnification sense.** Perceived size comes from retinal image size combined with perceived distance and familiar-size knowledge. Through eyepieces, normal distance cues are fixed or absent, so the brain treats the scene as an ordinary arm's-length world. Tissue feels large. Hand movements feel like big sweeps.

2. **Scale distortion persists.** In a robotic suturing study, surgeons aiming for 10 mm spacing placed sutures 6.5 mm apart and 4.1 mm from the incision. Estimating a 10 mm suture outside the body was accurate (9.6 mm). The most experienced surgeons were the least accurate on suture-to-incision distance. Magnified scale judgment is not fixed by experience alone. It needs references inside the field.

3. **Fast visuomotor remapping.** The brain maps each hand movement onto a magnified visual movement. With continuous visual feedback, new movement gains are absorbed almost immediately without full adaptation.

4. **Magnification amplifies error signals.** Every hand error appears several times larger. In reaching experiments, artificially magnified errors made motor learning faster. Surgeons respond by slowing down, bracing and making small feedback-driven corrections between tremor cycles.

5. **Hyperacuity scales with the optics.** A 5 arcsecond alignment threshold becomes roughly 0.5 micrometers at the tissue under 10x in theory. This is why surgeons can see errors they cannot physically prevent.

6. **Experts work in relative units.** Microsurgeons think "one wall thickness between stitches" or "half a needle length," not "0.15 mm." Relative judgment is the hardware's strength (2 to 3 percent). Absolute millimeter conversion is its weakness. Microsurgical precision runs mostly on relative judgments inside the magnified field.

#### Summary ranges

| Context | Finest detail perceived | Hand placement achieved |
|---|---|---|
| Naked eye | 0.07 to 0.1 mm detail; about 5 micrometer misalignment | about 0.2 to 0.5 mm deliberate placement (estimate) |
| Loupes 2.5x to 4.5x | 0.015 to 0.04 mm | about 0.1 to 0.3 mm braced (estimate) |
| Operating microscope 10x to 25x | about 0.005 mm | 0.03 mm tremor; 0.14 to 0.29 mm positioning RMS |
| Robot with motion scaling | about 0.005 mm | tremor under 0.01 mm |
| Light microscope up to 1000x | about 0.0002 mm (diffraction limit) | hand not used directly |
