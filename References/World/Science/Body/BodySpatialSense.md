Test case: đá cầu (Vietnamese shuttlecock kicking), including back-heel and behind-the-back kicks. Figures below are for healthy, untrained adults unless noted.

Terra **Agility** / **Acrobatics** / **Dodging** shrink placement and timing variance; they do not remove the ~100–200 ms blind window on visually guided correction. Sensors stay the same; skill buys earlier prediction and tighter foot error.

---

## 1. Proprioception (knowing where your own body is)

| Measure | Average human limit |
|---|---|
| Detecting slow passive joint movement | Hip/shoulder ~0.2–0.8°, knee ~0.5–1°, ankle ~1–2°, finger joints ~1–3° |
| Actively repositioning a joint to a remembered angle | Error ~2–5° (knee, elbow, shoulder); worse with age, fatigue and injury |
| Locating your hand with eyes closed | Error ~1–4 cm |
| Drift without vision | Your sense of where an unseen hand is drifts several cm within 15–60 seconds of keeping still. Moving it "refreshes" the signal |
| Foot placement without looking | Error ~2–5 cm at the toe for a familiar movement. Novel positions are much worse |
| Force/weight discrimination | ~7–10% difference needed to tell two weights apart |
| Touch resolution (two-point) | Fingertip 2–3 mm, palm ~10 mm, back/thigh 40–70 mm |

Proprioception is good at joint angles and poor at absolute position in space. The brain gets the absolute position by chaining joint angles together, so small errors add up from hip to foot.

## 2. Vestibular (balance and head motion)

| Measure | Limit |
|---|---|
| Rotation detection | ~0.5–2°/s depending on frequency |
| Linear acceleration detection | ~0.05–0.1 m/s² (about 0.5–1% of gravity) |
| Judging true vertical | Within ~1–2° when upright. Error rises sharply when tilted or spinning |
| Constant-velocity motion | Undetectable. Steady rotation fades from perception in ~20–30 s; steady straight-line motion is never sensed |
| Distinguishing tilt from acceleration | Not possible from the inner ear alone. Vision and proprioception resolve it, which is why pilots get spatial disorientation in clouds |

## 3. Vision and 3D object tracking

| Measure | Limit |
|---|---|
| Stereoacuity (depth from two eyes) | ~20–60 arcseconds typical |
| Useful stereo depth | Strong within ~2 m, useful to ~10–20 m, nearly gone past ~100 m. Depth error grows with the square of distance |
| Depth error at arm's length | A few mm |
| High-acuity vision | Only the central ~2° (thumbnail at arm's length). Everything else is low resolution |
| Field of view | ~200° horizontal, ~120° binocular overlap |
| Smooth eye tracking | Accurate to ~30°/s, degrades beyond, breaks down ~60–100°/s (eyes switch to jumpy catch-up saccades) |
| Objects tracked at once | ~4 (range 3–5). Up to ~8 if slow, 1 if fast |
| Time-to-contact judgment | ~5–10% error on approach timing |
| Interception timing (catch/hit) | Untrained ~±20–50 ms. Elite batters ~±5–10 ms |

## 4. The processing-speed ceiling

This is the hardest limit and the one that governs everything in the đá cầu / interception case.

| Process | Time |
|---|---|
| Simple visual reaction | ~200–250 ms |
| Auditory reaction | ~150–170 ms |
| Fastest visually guided correction of an ongoing movement | ~100–150 ms |
| Spinal stretch reflex | ~30–50 ms |

Practical consequence: the last ~100–200 ms of any interception is blind. Whatever the eyes see in that window cannot change the movement. The body is executing a prediction made earlier. A 90 mph fastball takes ~400 ms to reach the plate, so a batter commits to the swing with roughly half the flight left.

## 5. Spatial sense of the environment

| Measure | Limit |
|---|---|
| Blindfolded walking to a previously seen target | Accurate to ~20 m with ~5–10% error |
| Walking straight with no visual landmarks | Fails. People walk in circles, sometimes as tight as ~20 m across |
| Distance estimates outdoors | Compressed beyond a few tens of meters. Heights looking down are overestimated ~30–50% |
| Sound localization | ~1–2° straight ahead, ~10° or worse to the sides, poor for elevation and distance |
| Gravity model | The brain has a built-in 1g expectation. Catching timing is wrong in microgravity until relearned |

## 6. Applying it to đá cầu

- **Why it's possible:** A shuttlecock is a high-drag object. It decelerates hard and falls at a few m/s near the end. That gives ~0.5–1 s of predictable flight versus ~0.4 s for a fastball, keeping most of the trajectory inside the window where visual correction works.
- **Behind-the-back kicks:** Once the shuttlecock leaves the visual field, the player is running a prediction for the last ~200–400 ms. The foot gets there by proprioception alone, with that ~2–5 cm placement error for an untrained person.
- **What training changes:** The sensors stay the same. Skilled players cut their timing variance from ~±30 ms toward ~±10 ms, shrink foot-placement error to roughly the size of the sole's sweet spot, and read the trajectory earlier (from the kicker's body before the shuttle is even struck). That earlier read buys time against the fixed ~100–200 ms delay.
- **The average person's failure point:** It's mainly not seeing the shuttlecock. It's (1) committing to the kick too late because they react instead of predicting, and (2) foot-position error on an unseen, unfamiliar limb position that's larger than the contact surface.

## Terra readout (soft)

| Piece | Effect on this baseline |
|---|---|
| **Agility / Acrobatics** | Tighter balance and foot placement; less drift on familiar patterns |
| **Dodging** | Earlier commit and tighter timing variance under threat |
| **Marksmanship / Dexterity** | Interception timing toward elite band; does not shorten spinal / visual pipelines |
| **Vision360 / Echolocation** | More continuous track when the target leaves forward FOV; last ~100–200 ms still blind without prediction |
| **Combat `t_r`** | Live ~0.2 s visual reaction sits inside the simple visual reaction band above |
