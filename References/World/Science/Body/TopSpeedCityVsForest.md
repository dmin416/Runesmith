# Top Speed: City vs Dense Forest

> Earth invent loot. Environment ceilings for a reinforced runner (body and reflexes already scaled). Not Caldris law. Drag / friction physics: `RunningBladesSpeedLimits.md`. Surface shear catalog: `GripBiteBracing.md`. Human records: `HumanSpeed.md`. Perception / RT: `SpeedPerception.md`. G-load: `GForceTolerance.md`. Falling: `Falling.md`. Nav: `Index.md`.

Assumes a body reinforced enough to survive its own forces (tendons, bones, organs scaled up) with reflexes fast enough to steer. The environment sets the limits below.

---

## City

### Straight-line ceiling

- Rubber soles on concrete or asphalt: **~50 m/s (115 mph)**, where drag equals grip.
- Enhanced grip (microspines on concrete, gecko pads on glass and polished stone): **~80 m/s (180 mph)**.
- Pavement survives these loads. Drag and grip set the cap.

### The grid is the real problem

Cornering on the ground alone: speed = √(grip acceleration × turn radius). A typical intersection allows a ~20 m radius.

| Grip | Max corner speed (20 m radius) |
|---|---|
| Rubber, μ 0.8 | 12.5 m/s |
| Spikes, μ_eff 1.8 | 19 m/s |
| Gecko pads, ~5 g | 31 m/s |

A runner has to brake for every corner and re-accelerate after it. On 150 m blocks with rubber soles, peak speed between corners only reaches ~36 m/s and the **average falls to ~25 m/s (55 mph)**.

### Wall-assisted turns

Building faces on the outside of each turn become a banked track. Pushing off them supplies cornering force without relying on ground grip.

| Lateral load tolerated | Corner speed (20 m radius) |
|---|---|
| 10 g | 44 m/s |
| 20 g | 63 m/s |
| 40 g | 89 m/s |

- Brick and concrete facades take the push easily (a foot at 20 g loads them at under 1 MPa).
- Glass storefronts shatter. Thin cladding panels tear off.
- With wall carving the grid stops mattering. **Average speed climbs to ~45 to 60 m/s.**

### Alleys (3 to 6 m wide)

- Two close walls act like a forest. Pushing backward off a wall already passed adds forward speed without any ground grip. Propulsion is no longer capped by friction.

### Rooftops

- Straight lines regardless of street layout. A 25 m street gap at 50 m/s takes 0.5 s and drops 1.2 m.
- Parapets (brick, concrete) make good launch blocks.
- Roof surfaces fail first: membranes tear, gravel ballast sprays, sheet metal buckles, skylights break.

### Hard limits in a city

- **People and cars.** Stopping from 50 m/s on rubber takes ~160 m. Anything that steps out inside that distance gets hit. At 50 m/s, impact with a pedestrian is fatal to them.
- **Glass and street furniture.** Lamp posts, signs and bus shelters bend or break under push loads.
- **Practical ceiling:** ~60 to 80 m/s with wall carving and enhanced grip. Street-level crowds force much lower speeds in practice.

## Dense forest

### Ground only

- Leaf litter, moss and damp soil give μ ~0.3 to 0.5. Roots, logs and holes break stride.
- A dense stand (~600 trees per hectare, ~4 m spacing) leaves a straight clear path of only ~15 to 20 m on average before a trunk is in the way.
- Dodging 1 m within 18 m needs lateral acceleration of v² ÷ 160. Ground grip of ~4 m/s² caps speed at **~20 to 25 m/s (45 to 55 mph)**.

### Kicking off trees

Trees become starting blocks everywhere. Each kick can push backward for propulsion and sideways for steering. Ground grip stops mattering entirely.

Trunk capacity for a kick ~1 m above ground (green wood ~50 MPa bending strength; root plates often tip before the trunk snaps):

| Trunk diameter | Approx. kick load before failure |
|---|---|
| 10 cm | ~0.5 t (springs and bends) |
| 20 cm | ~4 t |
| 40 cm | 10 to 20 t (root plate tips first) |
| 80 cm | 100 t+ |

- Kicking low is stronger. Kicking high gives more leverage to snap or uproot.
- Saplings act as springboards that return energy too slowly to use at speed.
- Bark strips off under heavy shear, especially in spring when the bark layer is loose. Spikes into the wood (μ_eff 1.2 to 1.8) or claws solve this. Gecko pads fail on bark.

### Load needed per kick at 100 m/s

- Drag at 100 m/s: ~2.4 kN (needs ~240 kW to sustain).
- At 100 m/s a tree passes every ~0.1 s. A 0.02 s contact delivering that average needs ~12 kN peak plus steering. Any trunk 20 cm or thicker handles it.

### Steering load at speed

| Speed | Time between obstacles | Lateral load to dodge |
|---|---|---|
| 50 m/s | 0.36 s | ~1.6 g |
| 100 m/s | 0.18 s | ~6 g |
| 150 m/s | 0.12 s | ~14 g |
| 200 m/s | 0.09 s | ~25 g |

The trees can supply all of these. Propulsion and steering stop being the limit.

### What actually caps forest speed

- **Perception.** At 150 m/s a new obstacle arrives every ~0.1 s. Ordinary human reaction time is ~0.2 s.
- **Branches and understory.** A 1 cm twig at 100 m/s strikes like a whip. Eyes are the most vulnerable point. Brush, vines and deadfall add constant drag and impacts.
- **Drag.** At 200 m/s drag reaches ~9.6 kN and sustaining it needs ~1.9 MW.
- **Practical ceiling:** **~80 to 120 m/s (180 to 270 mph)** for a durable runner with superhuman perception. Faster than the city because push points sit on every side at every moment.

## Comparison

| Environment | Ground grip only | Using walls or trees |
|---|---|---|
| City grid | ~25 m/s average | ~45 to 60 m/s average |
| Long straight road | ~50 m/s | ~50 m/s (no walls) |
| Alley or rooftops | ~50 m/s | ~60 to 80 m/s |
| Dense forest | ~20 to 25 m/s | ~80 to 120 m/s |

The forest is the faster environment. Trees provide unlimited push points in every direction. The city provides strong push points only at its edges while filling the center with people, cars and glass.
