## 1. What Blunt Force Does

Blunt force transfers energy into tissue without breaking the skin (or with only minor splitting). Damage comes from three mechanisms:

- **Compression:** tissue is crushed between the impact and bone or a hard surface.
- **Shear:** layers slide against each other and tear small vessels and connective tissue.
- **Acceleration/deceleration:** organs and the brain move inside their containers and strike or tear against fixed points.

Injury types produced: contusions (bruises), hematomas, muscle contusions, fractures, sprains, joint injuries, concussion, internal organ injury and crush injury.

## 2. The Universal Tissue Repair Sequence

Every blunt injury follows the same four overlapping phases.

| Phase | Timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Hemostasis** | Seconds to hours | Torn vessels constrict; platelets plug them; a fibrin clot forms |
| **Inflammation** | Hours to 5 days | Neutrophils arrive in hours (peak 24-48 h), macrophages by day 2-3 (peak day 3-5); dead tissue and blood are cleared; swelling, heat, redness, pain |
| **Proliferation** | Day 3 to week 3-6 | Fibroblasts lay down collagen; new capillaries grow; damaged muscle fibers regenerate from satellite cells |
| **Remodeling** | Week 3 to 12-24 months | Collagen reorganizes along stress lines; scar tissue matures; strength slowly rises |

**Swelling** peaks at 24-72 hours and usually subsides over 1-2 weeks.

## 3. Bruise (Skin and Subcutaneous Contusion)

Blood leaks from ruptured capillaries into tissue. The color sequence is the visible breakdown of hemoglobin by macrophages.

| Time after injury | Color | Chemistry |
|---|---|---|
| 0-2 days | Red, purple, dark blue | Fresh oxygenated then deoxygenated hemoglobin |
| 1-5 days | Blue/purple/black | Deoxygenated hemoglobin pooling |
| 5-7 days | Green | Biliverdin |
| 7-10 days | Yellow | Bilirubin |
| 10-14 days | Light brown | Hemosiderin (iron storage) |
| 2-3 weeks | Gone | Fully absorbed |

Larger and deeper bruises run longer: 3-4 weeks. Bruises on the legs heal slower than on the face because of lower blood flow and gravity pooling. Blood also tracks downward under gravity, so a bruise can appear inches below the impact point a day or two later.

## 4. Hematoma

A pooled collection of blood rather than diffuse staining.

- Small hematomas absorb in 1-4 weeks.
- Large hematomas can take 1-3 months or need drainage.
- Some calcify or become encapsulated in a fibrous shell if not drained.
- A tense, expanding hematoma in a limb can trigger compartment syndrome (see section 10).

## 5. Muscle Contusion

Common in thighs (quadriceps "charley horse"), calves and upper arms.

| Grade | Signs | Return to full function |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Local tenderness; knee bend over 90° (thigh) | 1-2 weeks |
| Moderate | Noticeable limp; knee bend 45-90° | 2-4 weeks |
| Severe | Cannot bend knee past 45°; severe limp | 4-8+ weeks |

**Muscle repair process:**
- Days 1-3: damaged fibers die (necrosis); inflammatory cells clear debris.
- Days 3-7: satellite cells (muscle stem cells) activate and multiply.
- Days 7-21: new muscle fibers form and fuse with surviving fibers.
- Weeks 3-6: fibers mature; competing scar tissue forms in the gap.
- Months: strength and endurance return with loading.

**Complication: myositis ossificans.** Bone forms inside the injured muscle. Occurs in about 9-20% of severe thigh contusions. Detectable on X-ray at 3-4 weeks. Matures over 3-6 months and often partly reabsorbs over 1-2 years.

**Permanent residue:** a dense scar within the muscle that is less elastic and slightly weaker than original tissue.

## 6. Fractures

### Healing phases of bone

| Phase | Timing | Process |
|---|---|---|
| Hematoma and inflammation | Days 1-7 | Blood clot fills the break; inflammatory cells and stem cells arrive; bone ends at the break die back slightly |
| Soft callus | Weeks 2-3 | Cartilage and fibrous tissue bridge the gap; bone is "sticky" but bends easily |
| Hard callus | Weeks 3-12 | Cartilage converted to woven bone; fracture becomes clinically stable; visible on X-ray |
| Remodeling | Months to 2+ years | Woven bone replaced by organized lamellar bone; the bump of callus is gradually reshaped |

### Typical time to clinical union (adult man)

| Bone | Weeks |
|---|---|
| Finger/toe | 3-5 |
| Metacarpal/metatarsal | 4-6 |
| Rib | 6 (pain often 3-6 weeks) |
| Clavicle | 6-12 |
| Distal radius (wrist) | 6-8 |
| Forearm shaft | 8-12 |
| Humerus | 8-12 |
| Scaphoid | 8-12; can be 6 months; high nonunion risk due to poor blood supply |
| Ankle | 6-10 |
| Tibia | 12-20 |
| Femur shaft | 12-24 |
| Pelvis | 8-12 |
| Vertebral compression | 8-12 |
| Skull (linear, nondisplaced) | 3-6 months |

**Full strength:** bone typically reaches near-original strength 1-2 years after the break. Muscle wasting around an immobilized limb (up to about 1% of muscle mass per day in the first weeks of disuse) takes 2-3 times the immobilization period to rebuild.

**Slowing factors:** smoking (up to doubles healing time), poor blood supply, movement at the fracture, infection, older age, diabetes, poor protein/calcium/vitamin D intake, NSAID use in high doses.

**Nonunion:** a fracture not healed by 6-9 months. Occurs in roughly 5-10% of long-bone fractures.

## 7. Sprains (Ligament) and Joint Injury

| Grade | Damage | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| I | Stretched, microtears | 1-3 weeks |
| II | Partial tear; some laxity | 3-6 weeks |
| III | Complete tear | 6-12+ weeks; may need surgery |

Ligament healing is slow because ligaments have poor blood supply. Healed ligament tissue regains about 50-70% of original strength at 1 year and rarely returns to 100%. Reinjury rates are high in the first year.

**Articular cartilage:** essentially does not regenerate in adults. Cartilage bruises and cracks lead to early arthritis years later.

**Meniscus:** the outer third (red zone) has blood supply and can heal in 6-12 weeks. The inner two-thirds generally do not heal.

## 8. Concussion and Brain Contusion

### Concussion (mild traumatic brain injury)
Rotational force stretches axons and triggers a chemical cascade: a flood of potassium out of cells, glutamate release, calcium influx and an energy crisis in which the brain needs more fuel while blood flow is reduced.

| Time | State |
|---|---|
| 0-24 h | Headache, confusion, nausea, light/noise sensitivity, memory gap around the event |
| Days 1-10 | Metabolic crisis; brain most vulnerable to a second hit |
| 10-14 days | Symptoms resolve in most adults |
| Up to 30 days | Brain metabolism and blood flow fully normalize; cognitive testing can still lag behind symptom relief |
| Over 1 month | Persistent post-concussion symptoms in roughly 15-30% |
| Months to years | Small group with lasting headaches, sleep disruption, mood changes |

**Second-impact risk:** a second concussion before recovery causes larger, longer and sometimes catastrophic injury.

**Repeated concussions:** each subsequent concussion tends to occur with less force and resolve slower. Repeated head impacts over years are linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy.

### Cerebral contusion (bruised brain tissue)
- Bleeding and swelling peak 24-72 hours after injury.
- Dead neurons are not replaced. Function is recovered by rewiring surrounding tissue (neuroplasticity).
- Most functional recovery occurs within 6 months; slower gains continue to about 2 years.

## 9. Internal Organ Injury

| Organ | Typical injury | Healing |
|---|---|---|
| **Spleen** | Laceration, capsule tear, delayed rupture | Nonoperative healing 6-12 weeks; avoid contact 2-3 months; delayed rupture possible days to weeks after injury |
| **Liver** | Laceration, hematoma | 6-12 weeks for most grades; liver regenerates lost tissue |
| **Kidney** | Contusion, laceration | Minor: 2-6 weeks; blood in urine clears in days |
| **Lung** | Pulmonary contusion | Worsens over first 24-72 h; resolves in 5-7 days; can progress to pneumonia |
| **Heart** | Cardiac contusion | Arrhythmia risk mostly in first 24-48 h |
| **Bowel** | Contusion, delayed perforation | Perforation can appear 24-72 h after impact |

## 10. Crush Injury and Compartment Syndrome

**Compartment syndrome:** swelling inside a closed muscle compartment (lower leg, forearm, thigh) raises pressure until blood flow stops.
- Muscle damage becomes irreversible after about 4-6 hours of ischemia.
- Nerve damage begins within about 4 hours and becomes permanent around 6-8 hours.
- Treatment is surgical release of the compartment (fasciotomy).

**Rhabdomyolysis:** crushed muscle releases myoglobin and potassium into the blood. Myoglobin can clog kidneys within hours to days. Potassium surge can stop the heart, especially when a crushed limb is suddenly released after a long time trapped (crush syndrome).

## 11. What Recovers Fully and What Does Not

| Fully recovers | Partially recovers | Does not recover |
|---|---|---|
| Bruises | Muscle (scar replaces part) | Articular cartilage |
| Most hematomas | Ligaments (50-70% strength at 1 year) | Dead neurons |
| Bone (near-full strength in 1-2 years) | Brain function (via rewiring) | Inner meniscus |
| Liver tissue | Nerve function after compression | Muscle dead from prolonged ischemia |
| Minor kidney and lung contusions | Joint stability after grade II-III sprains | Cartilage-derived arthritis once started |

## 12. Factors That Speed or Slow Recovery

**Faster:** adequate protein (1.6-2.0 g/kg/day during repair), sleep, early gentle movement after the inflammatory phase, good blood supply, younger age, nonsmoker.

**Slower:** smoking, diabetes, alcohol, poor nutrition, sleep restriction, repeated reinjury, immobilization beyond what is needed, older age, steroids.
