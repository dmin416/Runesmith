Terra **Cold Resistance** cuts cold / frostbite harm (−10%/level). **Recovery** speeds soft-tissue knit after thaw. Much tissue death still happens in the **post-rewarming** clot window; resistance that halves freeze depth helps more than Recovery alone. Does not regrow lost digits.

## 1. How Cold Damages Tissue

### Body response to cold
1. **Vasoconstriction:** blood vessels in hands, feet, ears and nose clamp down to protect core temperature.
2. **Hunting response:** vessels open and close in cycles every 5-10 minutes to warm extremities briefly. This response fades with prolonged or severe cold.
3. **Extremities sacrificed:** when core temperature is threatened, blood flow to hands and feet nearly stops.

### Freezing mechanism
Skin freezes when tissue temperature drops below about -0.5 to -2°C (31 to 28°F).

| Stage | Process |
|---|---|
| **Extracellular ice** | Ice crystals form between cells first. Water is drawn out of cells; cells shrink and dehydrate |
| **Intracellular ice** | With rapid freezing, ice forms inside cells and ruptures them directly |
| **Vessel injury** | Endothelial lining of small vessels is damaged |
| **Rewarming/reperfusion** | Blood returns to damaged vessels; inflammatory chemicals (thromboxane, prostaglandins) cause clotting and constriction; tissue dies from lack of blood flow after thawing |

**Much of frostbite tissue death happens after rewarming, not during freezing.** Microvascular clotting over the following hours to days determines the final extent.

### Freeze-thaw-refreeze
Thawing tissue then letting it refreeze causes far worse damage than staying frozen until rewarming is permanent. Field rule: do not thaw if refreezing is possible.

## 2. Classification by Depth

| Degree | Tissue | Appearance after rewarming | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frostnip** | Surface only; no ice in tissue | Pale, numb; recovers with warming | Full recovery in hours |
| **1st degree** | Epidermis | Numb white or yellow plaque; redness and swelling after warming; no blisters | Peeling at 7-10 days; heals in 1-2 weeks |
| **2nd degree** | Full epidermis, part of dermis | Clear or milky blisters within 6-24 hours | Heals in 3-4 weeks; no tissue loss |
| **3rd degree** | Full dermis, subcutaneous tissue | Blood-filled (hemorrhagic) blisters; skin turns blue-gray then black | Hard black eschar forms over 1-2 weeks; tissue loss; healing over months |
| **4th degree** | Muscle, tendon, bone | Mottled, cold, deep purple/gray then black and mummified; no blisters or late small ones | Dry gangrene; amputation |

**Grading is uncertain for days.** The true depth often becomes clear only after 1-3 weeks.

## 3. Rewarming

- Water bath at 37-39°C (98.6-102°F).
- Usually takes 15-30 minutes.
- Endpoint: skin turns red or purple and becomes soft and pliable.
- Rewarming is intensely painful as nerves reperfuse.
- Dry heat (fire, heater) is avoided because numb tissue burns without the victim feeling it.
- Rubbing is avoided because it breaks frozen cells.

## 4. Healing Timeline

| Time after rewarming | Events |
|---|---|
| 0-1 hour | Severe burning pain; skin red, purple or mottled |
| 1-24 hours | Swelling begins; blisters form (2nd and 3rd degree) |
| 24-72 hours | Swelling peaks; blisters at full size; the window for clot-dissolving treatment (tPA within about 24 h; iloprost within about 72 h) to save tissue |
| Days 3-7 | Swelling begins subsiding; blisters dry or rupture; dead tissue starts to darken |
| Weeks 1-3 | Black hard eschar (carapace) forms over deep injury; shallow injuries peel and heal |
| Weeks 2-4 | 1st and 2nd degree healed; new skin pink and thin |
| Weeks 3-6 | Line between living and dead tissue becomes visible (demarcation) |
| Months 1-3 | Dead tissue mummifies (dry gangrene); dead digits shrink and blacken; living tissue heals beneath the shell |
| Months 1-3 | Amputation of clearly dead parts if needed; dead toes or fingertips sometimes fall off on their own (autoamputation) |
| Months 3-12 | Remodeling of healed skin; nerve sensation slowly returns |
| Years | Long-term effects persist |

**Surgical principle:** "Frozen in January, amputate in July." Surgery is delayed 1-3 months unless infection (wet gangrene) forces earlier action. The black shell often hides healthy tissue underneath, so early amputation removes more than necessary.

**Bone scan or angiography** at 2-5 days can predict which tissue will die weeks earlier than visual appearance.

## 5. Dry vs Wet Gangrene

| | Dry gangrene | Wet gangrene |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Black, hard, shriveled, mummified | Swollen, dark, foul-smelling, oozing |
| Infection | No | Yes |
| Spread | Stays at demarcation line | Spreads; can become sepsis |
| Urgency | Wait for demarcation | Urgent surgery |

## 6. What Recovers and How Fast

| Tissue | Recovery |
|---|---|
| **Epidermis** | 1-4 weeks |
| **Dermis (partial)** | 3-6 weeks |
| **Blood vessels** | Small vessels regrow over weeks; vessel tone control can remain abnormal for years |
| **Sensory nerves** | Return over months; numbness, tingling or burning can persist 6-12 months or permanently |
| **Nails** | Often lost after 2nd-3rd degree; regrowth takes 4-6 months (fingers) or 12-18 months (toes); often deformed |
| **Sweat regulation** | Often permanently abnormal (excess sweating) |
| **Dead digits** | No recovery; lost |

## 7. Long-Term Effects (Sequelae)

Common for years or permanently after moderate to severe frostbite:

- **Cold sensitivity:** affected areas get cold faster and hurt more in cold. Reported by most survivors of significant frostbite. Often lifelong.
- **Increased risk of repeat frostbite** in the same areas.
- **Chronic pain** and burning sensations.
- **Numbness** and reduced fine touch.
- **Hyperhidrosis** (excess sweating) of hands or feet.
- **Color changes:** skin pale or bluish in cold.
- **Frostbite arthritis:** joint damage and stiffness in affected fingers or toes, appearing months to years later.
- **Skin thinning** and scarring.
- **Nail deformity.**
- **Gait changes** after toe or forefoot loss.

## 8. Nonfreezing Cold Injury (Trench Foot / Immersion Foot)

Occurs from prolonged exposure to cold, wet conditions above freezing (roughly 0-15°C / 32-60°F). The defining cold injury of World War I trench warfare.

**Required exposure:** about 12 hours or more of continuous wet cold; worsened by tight boots, immobility and dehydration.

| Stage | Timing | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Exposure** | During cold | Feet cold, numb, pale or mottled; clumsy walking |
| **Prehyperemic** | Hours after removal from cold | Feet pale, cold, numb, pulses weak |
| **Hyperemic** | 24 hours to 6-10 weeks | Feet hot, red, swollen; severe burning or shooting pain, worse at night; blisters; skin breakdown |
| **Post-hyperemic** | Weeks to months, sometimes years | Cold sensitivity; persistent pain; excess sweating; recurrent swelling |

**Pathology:** nerve and blood vessel damage without ice formation. Nerves are the most cold-sensitive tissue and suffer the worst lasting harm.

**Outcome:** severe cases develop infection, gangrene and amputation. Many survivors have lifelong cold sensitivity and neuropathic pain.

## 9. Systemic Hypothermia (Often Accompanies Frostbite)

Normal core temperature: 37°C (98.6°F).

| Stage | Core temp | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 32-35°C (90-95°F) | Shivering; clumsiness; slurred speech; poor judgment |
| Moderate | 28-32°C (82-90°F) | Shivering stops; confusion; slow heart rate; drowsiness |
| Severe | Under 28°C (82°F) | Unconscious; heart arrhythmias; may appear dead |
| Profound | Under 24°C (75°F) | Cardiac arrest risk very high |

**Paradoxical undressing:** in moderate to severe hypothermia, victims may feel hot and remove clothing.

**Afterdrop:** core temperature can continue falling after rescue as cold blood from limbs returns to the core.

**Recovery:** mild hypothermia reverses in hours with rewarming. Moderate to severe cases recover over 1-3 days if no cardiac arrest or organ damage occurred. Hypothermia slows brain metabolism, so full neurological recovery has occurred after long cardiac arrests in very cold victims.

## 10. Risk Factors

**Worse injury:** wind, wet clothing, high altitude, tight boots or gloves, immobility, dehydration, exhaustion, alcohol, smoking, diabetes, poor circulation, prior cold injury, contact with metal or fuel at subzero temperatures.

**Wind chill reference:** at -20°C (-4°F) air temperature with 30 km/h (19 mph) wind, exposed skin can freeze in about 30 minutes. At -35°C (-31°F) with similar wind, skin can freeze in 5-10 minutes.
