## Baseline (all mammals and birds)

- **Color:** Oxygenated (arterial) blood is bright cherry red. Venous blood is dark maroon. Spilled blood dries rust brown within hours and goes nearly black over days as the hemoglobin oxidizes.
- **Consistency:** About 3 to 4 times thicker than water. Slick when fresh, tacky as it dries.
- **Smell:** Metallic. The "iron" smell is partly a compound formed when blood's iron reacts with oils on skin.
- **Clotting:** Sets into a dark red jelly within minutes. Over the next hour the clot shrinks and squeezes out clear straw-yellow serum.

## Cow

- **Color:** Deep red, very close to human. "Oxblood" as a color name refers to the dark brownish red that dried cattle blood takes on.
- **Consistency:** Slightly thinner than human blood (hematocrit around 30 to 35% vs human 40 to 45%). Red cells are small and don't stack much, so blood in a bucket stays evenly mixed longer before settling.
- **Volume:** About 55 to 65 mL per kg. A 600 kg steer holds roughly 35 L; slaughter bleeding recovers about 15 to 20 L.
- **Clotting:** Moderate speed. Butchers stir it by hand or add salt or vinegar to keep it liquid.
- **Uses:** Blood sausage, blood meal fertilizer, traditional polished "oxblood" clay floors. Maasai herders tap a live cow's neck vein and drink the blood fresh or mixed with milk.

## Pig

- **Color:** Bright red, nearly identical to human. Pig blood chemistry is among the closest to human of any livestock.
- **Consistency:** Medium, a touch richer than cattle blood.
- **Volume:** About 65 to 70 mL per kg. A 110 kg market hog yields roughly 3 to 4 L at slaughter.
- **Clotting:** Fast and firm. Without constant whisking or added vinegar it sets into lumps within minutes of hitting the pan.
- **Cooked:** Sets into a smooth firm curd that turns dark purplish brown to near black (blood tofu, black pudding, morcilla, boudin noir).
- **Smell:** Mild metallic, little gaminess.

## Boar

- **Color:** Darker and deeper red than domestic pig. Wild animals run higher hemoglobin from constant activity.
- **Consistency:** Slightly thicker than farm pig blood. Clots just as fast.
- **Smell:** Earthier and gamier, influenced by a diet of acorns, roots and grubs. A boar that ran hard before dying has stress chemistry that makes both blood and meat stronger and more acidic.
- **Volume:** A 100 to 150 kg boar holds about 7 to 10 L.
- **Wound behavior:** Thick hide, fat and a cartilage "shield" over the shoulders tend to plug wounds, so blood trails are often sparse even on serious hits.

**Reading a game blood trail (applies to boar, deer, any mammal):**
- Lung hit: bright pink-red, frothy with air bubbles
- Heart or artery: bright red, sprayed or splashed in volume
- Liver: dark maroon, thick, heavy drops
- Gut: brownish or greenish, mixed with stomach matter, sour stink
- Muscle only: medium red, trail thins quickly as it clots

## Bird

- **Color:** Bright scarlet. Same hemoglobin color as mammals; arterial blood looks especially vivid.
- **Cells:** Bird red cells are oval and keep their nucleus (mammal red cells lose theirs). They're larger, roughly 12 by 7 µm vs human 7.5 µm round.
- **Consistency:** Similar to mammal blood. Chickens run about 30 to 35% hematocrit; strong fliers and high-altitude birds run 40 to 55%, so their blood is thicker.
- **Temperature:** Birds run 40 to 42°C, so fresh blood feels noticeably hot.
- **Volume:** Roughly 6 to 10% of body weight. A 2.5 kg chicken holds about 150 to 200 mL; a songbird holds only a few mL.
- **Clotting:** Birds use thrombocytes instead of platelets. A clean cut can bleed longer than expected; a broken growing feather ("blood feather") bleeds heavily.
- **Uses:** Polish czernina (duck blood soup), Chinese duck blood curd, French pressed duck.

## Bug

Insects don't have blood in the mammal sense. They have **hemolymph**, which sloshes freely through the body cavity and bathes the organs. Most insects get oxygen through air tubes, so the fluid needs no red pigment.

- **Color:**
  - Clear to pale yellow: most insects
  - Green: plant-eaters like caterpillars and grasshoppers, from plant pigments
  - Yellow or amber: many beetles. Ladybugs reflex-bleed a bitter yellow fluid from their leg joints when threatened.
  - Red: rare. Midge larvae ("bloodworms") actually carry hemoglobin. A squashed mosquito's red smear is its last blood meal, not its own fluid.
  - Blue: spiders, scorpions, crabs and horseshoe crabs use copper-based hemocyanin. Colorless inside the body, pale blue on contact with air. Horseshoe crab blood is a milky sky blue.
- **Consistency:** Watery to slightly syrupy. High in sugar (trehalose) and amino acids, so it dries sticky.
- **Clotting and darkening:** Cells clump and an enzyme triggers melanin formation. Wounds and smears go brown to black within minutes, which is why squashed bug marks darken.
- **Volume:** Hemolymph is roughly 20 to 40% of an insect's body volume. A large caterpillar holds under 1 mL.
- **Smell:** Usually none or faintly grassy. Defensive species smell bitter or acrid. Blister beetle hemolymph contains cantharidin, which raises blisters on skin.

## Quick comparison

| Animal | Fresh color | Thickness | Clot speed | Notable trait |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cow | Deep red | Slightly thin | Moderate | Large volume, slow settling |
| Pig | Bright red | Medium | Fast | Closest to human |
| Boar | Dark deep red | Medium-thick | Fast | Gamy smell, sparse trails |
| Bird | Bright scarlet | Medium | Variable | Hot, nucleated cells |
| Bug | Clear / yellow / green / blue | Watery-syrupy | Fast, turns black | Not true blood |
