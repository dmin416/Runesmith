## Part 1: The Biology of Getting Dirty

### What "dirty" actually is

Human "dirt" is mostly produced by the body itself. Environmental grime (soil, soot, mud) is the smaller part for most people.

| Source | What it is | Rate / scale |
|---|---|---|
| Dead skin | Outer layer (stratum corneum) constantly shedding | Roughly 30,000 to 40,000 cells per minute; a full outer layer turnover every 2 to 4 weeks |
| Sebum | Oily mix of triglycerides, wax esters, squalene and fatty acids from sebaceous glands | Highest on scalp, face, chest and back; none on palms and soles |
| Eccrine sweat | Mostly water and salt; cools the body | 2 to 4 million glands; 0.5 to 1 liter per day at rest, 1 to 2+ liters per hour in heavy exertion or heat |
| Apocrine sweat | Thicker sweat with proteins and lipids | Armpits, groin, nipples, around the anus; activates at puberty; the main odor source |
| Skin microbiome | Bacteria, fungi and mites living on skin | Roughly 1,000 bacterial species; about a million bacteria per square centimeter in some areas |
| Environmental | Dust, soil, smoke, food, animal matter | Varies with occupation and climate |

### Why people smell

Fresh sweat is nearly odorless. Smell comes from bacteria digesting it.

- **Armpit odor:** Corynebacterium and some Staphylococcus species (especially *S. hominis*) break apocrine secretions into volatile fatty acids and sulfur compounds. Key molecules include 3-methyl-2-hexenoic acid and 3-methyl-3-sulfanylhexan-1-ol (an onion-like thioalcohol detectable at extremely low concentrations).
- **Foot odor:** *Staphylococcus epidermidis* and Brevibacterium break down leucine in sweat into isovaleric acid (cheesy, sour smell). Brevibacterium is related to bacteria used to ripen some cheeses.
- **Scalp odor:** Sebum oxidizes and the yeast Malassezia feeds on it. Produces a rancid, waxy smell after several days.
- **Groin odor:** Apocrine glands plus trapped moisture plus warmth create a high-bacteria zone.
- **"Old person smell":** 2-nonenal, produced by oxidation of skin lipids, increases after about age 40.
- **Breath:** Bacteria on the tongue and between teeth produce volatile sulfur compounds (hydrogen sulfide, methyl mercaptan).

### Genetic variation in odor

- The **ABCC11** gene controls apocrine secretion. A variant common in East Asia (found in a large majority of Koreans and many Chinese and Japanese people) produces dry earwax and very little underarm odor.
- People with this variant can go much longer before smelling noticeable to others.
- Men on average have more apocrine activity and larger sweat output than women.
- Diet affects odor: garlic, onions, curry spices, heavy red meat, alcohol and some fish can alter sweat and breath smell.

### Where dirt builds first (ranked)

1. Armpits
2. Groin and buttocks crease
3. Feet (especially in closed shoes)
4. Scalp and hair
5. Under breasts and belly folds
6. Behind ears and back of neck
7. Hands (from contact, not self-production)
8. Face (oil, especially nose and forehead)

## Part 2: When People Notice

### Olfactory adaptation

- The nose stops registering a constant smell within minutes to hours. This is why people cannot smell their own home, clothes or body accurately.
- Stepping away (going outside, sleeping) partly resets the nose. People often notice their own smell after returning from fresh air or when taking off clothing.
- Other people smell you before you smell yourself, usually by a margin of days.

### Timeline of no washing at all (average adult, temperate climate, normal activity)

| Time | Hair and scalp | Skin | Odor (to others) | Clothes | How it feels |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Normal to slightly flat | Normal | None to faint after exercise | Fine | Normal |
| Day 2 | Roots look oily on fine or straight hair | Slight film on face | Faint armpit odor up close | Underwear and socks smell | Slightly sticky |
| Day 3 | Visibly greasy; may clump | Face shiny; pores look larger | Noticeable at conversation distance in warm weather | Shirt collar and armpits smell | Itchy scalp begins |
| Days 4 to 7 | Heavy grease, flakes, itch | Dull, gray-ish skin in creases; acne flares in prone people | Clearly noticeable in a room | Clothing odor strong | Constant itch, discomfort in folds |
| Week 2 | Matted; scalp crust in some | Darkened patches on neck, ankles, creases | Strong; others avoid close contact | Clothes stiffen from oil and sweat | Rashes possible in folds |
| Weeks 3 to 4 | Hair may feel "self-regulated" for some (oil spreads down shaft) | Brown, scaly buildup (dermatitis neglecta) begins | Strong but stabilizes (microbiome adjusts) | Clothes deteriorate; seams harbor lice if present | Body adapts to some degree |
| Months | Dreadlocking in textured hair; dense oil coat | Thick crusted plaques in areas not rubbed by clothing | Persistent | Rotting fabric | Varies widely |
| Years | Fully matted | Heavy crusting; skin cracks and infections common | Severe | Clothing often discarded and replaced | Social isolation usually the main effect |

### Hair type matters

- **Fine, straight hair:** looks oily fastest (day 1 to 2) because oil travels down the shaft easily.
- **Thick, wavy hair:** 2 to 4 days.
- **Coily or tightly curled hair:** oil travels poorly down the shaft. Many people wash only weekly or less and the scalp often feels dry rather than greasy. Over-washing causes breakage.

### Activity multipliers

| Condition | Effect on dirt and odor speed |
|---|---|
| Hard physical labor | 2x to 4x faster |
| Hot, humid climate | 2x to 3x faster |
| Cold, dry climate | Much slower; people in polar conditions report going weeks with mild odor |
| Tight or synthetic clothing | Faster (traps moisture; polyester holds odor more than cotton or wool) |
| Wool clothing | Slower odor buildup; wool resists bacteria and odor |
| Linen clothing | Absorbs sweat well; historically used as the main "cleaning" layer |
| Sitting indoors, cool | Slowest |

### What people notice on themselves

- Itching (scalp first, then folds)
- Sticky or tacky skin
- Greasy hair texture
- Smell when removing clothing, especially socks and shirts
- Taste and feel of an unbrushed mouth (within hours)
- Flakes on dark clothing

## Part 3: How Long Can a Person Go Without Cleaning?

### Medically

There is no fixed limit. A healthy person with intact skin and a good immune system can go months or years without a full bath and survive. The body's skin barrier, natural shedding and microbiome provide baseline protection.

What matters medically is **targeted hygiene**:
- Hands (before food, after toilet)
- Mouth and teeth
- Wounds
- Feet (when wet or in boots)
- Genitals and anus (to some degree)
- Clothing (for lice)

### Documented cases and experiments

| Person / group | What they did | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Amou Haji (Iran) | Reportedly avoided washing for over 60 years; lived alone in a rural area | Reported healthy into his 90s; died in 2022 at a reported age of 94, months after villagers persuaded him to bathe |
| James Hamblin (journalist and physician) | Stopped using soap and shampoo for several years; rinsed with water only | Reported that odor and oil stabilized after the first weeks as his skin adjusted |
| David Whitlock (chemical engineer, AOBiome) | Stopped showering for over a decade; used a spray of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria | Reported no significant odor |
| Astronauts (ISS) | No showers; use wet wipes, rinseless shampoo and towels | Months-long missions without traditional bathing with no health problems |
| WWI trench soldiers | Often 2 to 6 weeks without proper bathing | Near-universal body lice; trench fever, trench foot and skin infections common |
| WWII submariners | Weeks on patrol with minimal fresh water | Strong odor; skin rashes; few serious health effects |
| Polar and mountain expeditions | Weeks to months with little washing | Mainly discomfort; cold slows odor buildup |
| Medieval and early modern peasants | Infrequent full baths; daily hand and face washing | Lice universal; disease from water and sanitation far more than from body dirt |

### General thresholds

- **Hands:** unwashed hands are a disease risk the same day.
- **Teeth:** plaque forms within 24 hours; hardens into tartar in 1 to 2 weeks; gum inflammation in 2 to 3 weeks.
- **Full body:** cosmetic and social issue for the first several weeks for most healthy people.
- **Months:** skin conditions (dermatitis neglecta, fungal infection) become likely.
- **Clothing:** unwashed clothes worn continuously enable body lice within weeks if exposed.

## Part 4: When It Becomes a Health Risk

### Highest-risk failures (by historical death toll)

1. **Hand hygiene and sanitation:** fecal-oral disease (cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis A, rotavirus, norovirus, intestinal worms). Handwashing with soap is commonly estimated to reduce diarrheal illness by about 30% and respiratory infections by about 20%.
2. **Body lice in clothing:** vector for epidemic typhus, trench fever and relapsing fever.
3. **Wound contamination:** tetanus, gangrene, sepsis.
4. **Oral neglect:** tooth abscesses, which before antibiotics could spread to the jaw, neck and brain and kill.

### Skin conditions from poor hygiene

| Condition | Cause | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dermatitis neglecta (terra firma-forme dermatosis) | Buildup of dead skin, oil and dirt | Neck, ankles, back, areas hard to reach | Brown, scaly; rubs off with alcohol or scrubbing; harmless |
| Intertrigo | Moisture and friction in folds | Under breasts, groin, belly, armpits | Red, raw, can get infected with yeast or bacteria |
| Tinea (ringworm, jock itch, athlete's foot) | Dermatophyte fungi | Feet, groin, body, scalp | Thrives in warm, damp, unwashed skin |
| Candidiasis | Candida yeast | Folds, groin, mouth | Red rash with satellite spots |
| Erythrasma | *Corynebacterium minutissimum* | Groin, armpits, between toes | Brown patches; glows coral-red under UV light |
| Folliculitis | Bacteria or fungus in hair follicles | Buttocks, thighs, back, scalp | Small pus bumps |
| Impetigo | Staph or strep bacteria | Face, hands; common in children | Honey-colored crust; contagious |
| Cellulitis | Bacteria entering through broken skin | Legs most often | Spreading red, hot swelling; can become life-threatening |
| Pitted keratolysis | Bacteria on sweaty feet | Soles | Crater-like pits; strong foot odor |
| Seborrheic dermatitis | Malassezia yeast plus oil | Scalp, face, chest | Worsens with infrequent washing |
| Acne flare | Oil and bacteria in pores | Face, back, chest | Unwashed skin worsens it in prone people |
| Myiasis | Fly larvae in wounds or neglected skin | Wounds, ulcers | Rare; seen in severe neglect |

### Parasites

| Parasite | Lives | Disease spread | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head louse (*Pediculus humanus capitis*) | Scalp hair | None significant | Spreads by head contact; clean hair does not prevent it |
| Body louse (*Pediculus humanus humanus*) | Clothing seams; feeds on skin | Epidemic typhus, trench fever, relapsing fever | Requires unwashed clothing worn continuously; the most dangerous hygiene parasite in history |
| Crab louse (*Pthirus pubis*) | Pubic hair | None significant | Spread by close contact |
| Scabies mite (*Sarcoptes scabiei*) | Burrows in skin | Secondary bacterial infection | Intense itch; crowding spreads it more than dirt |
| Fleas | Clothing, bedding, animals | Plague (via rat fleas), murine typhus | Medieval and early modern homes were full of them |
| Bed bugs | Bedding, walls | No proven disease | Common through history |
| Demodex mites | Hair follicles on the face | Usually none | Nearly all adults have them; overgrowth linked to rosacea |

### Lice-borne disease death tolls

- **Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign:** typhus and other diseases killed a large share of the Grande Armée. Lice DNA and *Bartonella quintana* have been found in soldiers' remains in Vilnius mass graves.
- **Serbia, 1915:** typhus epidemic killed an estimated 150,000 or more people including soldiers, civilians and prisoners.
- **Russia, 1918 to 1922:** typhus killed an estimated 2.5 to 3 million people during the Civil War era.
- **WWI Western Front:** trench fever affected an estimated hundreds of thousands to over a million soldiers. Rarely fatal but disabling for weeks.
- **WWII concentration camps:** typhus killed large numbers of prisoners, including Anne Frank and her sister Margot at Bergen-Belsen in 1945.

### Other hygiene-linked conditions

- **Trench foot (immersion foot):** feet kept wet and cold for 12+ hours to days. Nerve and blood vessel damage, gangrene, amputation. Roughly 20,000 British cases in the winter of 1914 to 1915. Prevented with dry socks, foot inspections and whale oil rubbed onto feet.
- **Trachoma:** chlamydia eye infection spread by dirty hands, cloths and flies. Leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide. Face washing is a core prevention measure.
- **Periodontitis:** gum disease leading to tooth loss; linked to heart disease and diabetes complications.
- **Urinary tract infections:** poor genital hygiene raises risk, particularly in women.
- **Balanitis:** inflammation of the head of the penis, more common in uncircumcised men with poor hygiene.
- **Intestinal worms:** pinworm, roundworm and others spread by dirty hands and fingernails.

### Over-washing risks (the other direction)

- Frequent hot showers and harsh soap strip skin oils and damage the skin barrier.
- Leads to dryness, cracking, eczema flares and itching.
- Cracked skin is an entry point for bacteria, ironically increasing infection risk.
- Overuse of antibacterial products may disrupt the skin microbiome.
- Healthcare workers washing dozens of times a day commonly develop hand dermatitis.

### Risk summary table

| Neglect | Time to problem | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| No handwashing around food or toilet | Same day | High (potentially fatal disease) |
| No tooth care | Weeks to months | Moderate to high over years |
| Dirty wounds | Hours to days | High |
| Wet feet in boots | Hours to days | High (trench foot) |
| Same clothes worn constantly | Weeks | High if body lice present |
| No full-body bath | Weeks to months | Low to moderate (skin issues) |
| No hair washing | Weeks | Low (itch, flakes, head lice unaffected) |

## Part 5: Superhumans

Whether a superhuman needs to bathe depends on which systems their powers modify. The core point: **odor and most dirt come from bacteria and secretions, not from weakness.** A superhuman who still sweats, sheds skin and hosts a microbiome will still get dirty and smell.

### Power-by-power breakdown

| Power | Disease risk from dirt | Odor | Skin buildup | Parasites | Net result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regenerative healing factor | Near zero; infections clear | Normal or worse (faster cell turnover = more shedding) | Normal | Lice and fleas still bite; bites heal instantly | Can go indefinitely without bathing medically; still smells |
| Perfect immune system | Near zero | Normal (commensal bacteria not attacked) | Normal | Lice live but carry no disease | Same as above |
| Invulnerable or armored skin | Near zero through skin | Normal if they sweat | Possibly less shedding | Scabies cannot burrow; lice may not be able to bite | Hygiene mostly cosmetic |
| Super strength / high metabolism | Normal unless paired with healing | Faster; more sweat | Faster | Normal | Gets dirty faster than humans |
| Super speed | Normal | Much faster sweat and heat output | Friction may scrub some areas | Normal | High hygiene demand |
| Temperature resistance / no sweating | Low | Much lower | Slower | Normal | Could go long periods with little odor |
| Self-cleaning / controlled microbiome | None | None | None | None | Never needs to bathe |
| Energy-based body or self-repair at a cellular level | None | Depends on design | Possibly none | Unlikely to support parasites | Effectively immune to dirt |
| Altered biology (non-human chemistry) | Possibly immune to Earth pathogens | Could smell very different or not at all | Unknown | Earth parasites may reject them | Author's choice |

### Questions that determine a superhuman's hygiene needs

1. Do they sweat? How much?
2. Do they shed skin? At what rate?
3. Do they produce sebum (skin oil)?
4. Do normal bacteria live on their skin?
5. Can pathogens infect them at all?
6. Can insects pierce their skin?
7. Do they heal from infection automatically?
8. Does their body expel foreign material (dirt, splinters, toxins)?
9. Do their clothes survive their activity, or do they rot and tear?
10. Do they eat and excrete normally?

### Things that still apply even to invulnerable superhumans

- **Clothing:** sweat and oil rot fabric. Clothing still needs washing or replacing.
- **Social perception:** smell and appearance affect how others treat them.
- **Teeth:** unless enhanced, plaque still forms. With healing, decay may reverse but staining and tartar may remain.
- **Hair:** still matts and gathers grease and debris.
- **External grime:** blood, mud, soot and gore stay on the surface until removed.
- **Carrying disease to others:** an immune superhuman can be an asymptomatic carrier, spreading pathogens on hands and clothes to normal people. A plague-proof hero who never washes could be a walking disease vector.
- **Cold-resistant superhumans** in winter conditions smell less. Fire or heat users may sweat far more unless their powers prevent it.

### Fantasy and LitRPG equivalents

- Cleaning cantrips or utility spells (removes surface dirt, often not internal causes like microbiome).
- Constitution, Vitality or Endurance stats granting disease resistance.
- Body tempering or cultivation systems that expel impurities through the skin as black sludge or sweat (a common trope in cultivation fiction), which creates an extra need to bathe at breakthroughs.
- Enchanted clothing that self-cleans.
- Alchemical soaps, purification potions or holy water.
- Racial traits (elves who do not sweat, dwarves with heavy body oil, beastkin with fur and stronger scent).

## Part 6: Medieval Europe (roughly 500 to 1500 CE)

### The myth

The popular image of medieval people never washing is largely a later invention. Much of it comes from the 1500s to 1700s, when bathing actually declined, and from Victorian writers contrasting themselves with the "Dark Ages."

### Daily hygiene

- **Hands and face:** washed every morning in a basin of water. Etiquette books stressed washing hands before meals.
- **At meals:** in wealthy households, servants brought a basin and ewer (water jug) and a towel to diners before and after eating. People shared dishes and ate with their hands, so clean hands were a social expectation.
- **Mouth:** rinsed with water or wine; teeth rubbed with linen cloth, herbs, salt or powdered materials; twigs used as chew sticks.
- **Hair:** combed daily. Combs were common possessions and found widely in archaeology. Fine-toothed combs were used against lice.
- **Full body:** less frequent. Ranged from weekly to occasional depending on wealth, location, season and access to heated water.

### Bathing methods

| Method | Who | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Wooden tub at home | Wealthier households | Lined with linen cloth to avoid splinters; often tented with fabric to hold heat; herbs added to water; shared by family members in sequence |
| Public bathhouses (stews) | Townspeople | Steam baths and hot tubs; common in towns across Europe by the 1200s and 1300s |
| Rivers, lakes, streams | Rural people, summer | Seasonal; common in warm months |
| Sponge or cloth washing | Everyone | Basin washing of the body without immersion |
| Monastic baths | Monks | Regulated by rule; some orders bathed only at major feasts like Christmas and Easter; the sick were allowed more frequent baths |
| Medicinal baths | The ill, the wealthy | Hot springs (such as at Bath, England, or spa towns in Germany and Italy) |

### Public bathhouses

- Paris in the late 1200s had around 30 public bathhouses according to records of the city's trades.
- Bathhouse keepers announced in the streets each morning that the water was hot.
- Bathhouses offered steam rooms, hot tubs, food, drink and sometimes prostitution.
- London's Southwark stews were tied to prostitution and regulated under the Bishop of Winchester.
- German towns had especially strong bathhouse cultures; bathing was a social event.
- **Decline:** from the late 1400s onward, bathhouses closed due to fear of plague, the spread of syphilis (after the 1490s), firewood shortages, rising fuel costs and moral campaigns by religious reformers.

### Laundry

- **Linen undergarments** (shirts, chemises, braies) were the layer closest to skin and the most frequently washed.
- **Wool outer garments** were rarely washed with water since it caused shrinking and felting. They were brushed, aired, shaken and spot cleaned.
- **Laundry process:** soaking, beating with paddles on rocks or boards, scrubbing with lye (made from wood ash and water), rinsing, then bleaching in the sun on grass.
- **Stale urine:** used as a source of ammonia for cleaning and in fulling (processing) wool cloth.
- **Laundresses** were an established working-class occupation.

### Soap

- Soap was made from animal fat (tallow) or plant oil boiled with lye (from wood ash).
- **Hard tallow soap:** coarse; used for laundry and sometimes the body.
- **Castile soap:** made from olive oil in Spain; prized for its gentleness.
- **Aleppo soap:** made from olive oil and laurel oil in Syria; traded into Europe.
- Soap-making centers included Castile, Marseille, Savona, Venice and Bristol.
- Soap was taxed and regulated in some places. Ordinary people often used lye water, herbal washes, sand or ash instead.

### Toilets and waste

- **Chamber pots:** used at night; emptied in the morning.
- **Garderobes:** castle toilets; a chute down the wall or into a pit or moat. Clothing was sometimes stored near them since ammonia fumes were believed to kill moths and fleas.
- **Privies and cesspits:** in towns, emptied by workers called gong farmers or gongfermors, who worked at night.
- **Wiping:** moss, hay, straw, leaves, rags, wool, sticks or the hand; the wealthy used cloth.
- **Street waste:** towns passed laws against dumping waste in streets with mixed success.

### Odor management

- Strewing herbs on floors: lavender, rosemary, meadowsweet, rushes, tansy.
- Pomanders: balls of perfume, ambergris or spices carried on the person.
- Perfumes and scented waters (rosewater, lavender water).
- Herbal sachets in clothing chests.

### Pests

- Lice were near-universal across classes.
- Fleas infested beds, rushes and clothing.
- Bed bugs were common.
- Picking lice from family members was a normal, even affectionate activity.

### Dental health

- Low sugar diets meant fewer cavities than modern populations before sugar became widespread.
- Coarse stone-ground flour wore teeth down significantly.
- Barber-surgeons and tooth-drawers pulled bad teeth.
- Remedies for toothache included herbs, prayers and pulling.

## Part 7: Early Modern Europe (roughly 1500 to 1800)

### The dry wash era

- Physicians in the 1500s and 1600s believed hot water opened pores and let disease (miasma, bad air) into the body.
- Full immersion bathing was widely avoided as dangerous.
- **Clean linen became the substitute for washing.** Changing the white linen shirt absorbed sweat and oil and was considered cleansing.
- Visible white linen at the collar and cuffs signaled personal cleanliness and status.
- Rubbing the body with cloth and using perfume replaced bathing for many.

### Reported royal habits (often exaggerated or apocryphal)

- **Elizabeth I** is said to have bathed once a month "whether she needed it or not." The quote is likely apocryphal.
- **Louis XIV** is often claimed to have bathed only a handful of times in his life. In fact Versailles had bathing apartments, though he relied heavily on rubbing down with alcohol and linen changes.
- **Versailles** is often described as filthy, with courtiers relieving themselves in corridors. Contemporary complaints suggest sanitation problems were real.

### Return of bathing

- The 1700s saw renewed interest in cold bathing for health and in sea bathing.
- Spa towns like Bath, Spa (Belgium) and Baden-Baden became fashionable.
- Bidets appeared in France in the early 1700s.

## Part 8: Ancient Civilizations

### Mesopotamia

- Earliest known soap-like recipes on Babylonian clay tablets from around 2800 BCE: fats boiled with ashes.
- Used mainly for cleaning wool and textiles rather than bodies.

### Indus Valley Civilization

- Mohenjo-daro (around 2500 BCE) had the "Great Bath," a large waterproofed public pool likely used for ritual bathing.
- Homes had private bathing platforms and connected drains.
- Covered street drainage systems; among the most advanced sanitation of the ancient world.

### Ancient Egypt

- Bathed daily in rivers or with basin water.
- Used natron (a natural mix of sodium carbonate and salt) as a cleanser.
- The Ebers Papyrus (around 1550 BCE) describes mixing oils with alkaline salts for skin treatment.
- Priests shaved their entire bodies for purity and washed several times a day.
- Many Egyptians shaved their heads and wore wigs, reducing lice.
- Used scented oils, kohl eye makeup (which may have had antibacterial properties) and early toothpastes made from ingredients such as ground ox hooves, myrrh, eggshells and pumice.

### Ancient Greece

- Washing linked to athletics; gymnasia had bathing areas.
- Cleaned by rubbing olive oil into skin, then scraping it off with a curved bronze tool called a strygil (strigil), removing sweat, dirt and oil.
- Public baths (balaneia) existed from the 400s BCE onward.
- Homer describes guests being bathed as a sign of hospitality.

### Ancient Rome

- Public baths (thermae and smaller balneae) were central to social life.
- By the 4th century CE, Rome reportedly had over 800 bathhouses of various sizes.
- **Baths of Caracalla:** capacity of around 1,600 bathers.
- **Baths of Diocletian:** capacity of around 3,000.
- **Bathing sequence:**
  1. Apodyterium (changing room)
  2. Palaestra (exercise yard)
  3. Tepidarium (warm room)
  4. Caldarium (hot room and hot pools)
  5. Frigidarium (cold plunge)
- Heated by hypocausts: furnaces sending hot air under raised floors and through wall flues.
- Oil and strigil used for cleaning; soap was known but used more for hair or medicine.
- Admission was cheap or free; slaves and the poor could attend.
- **Hidden problems:** water was changed infrequently; dirt, oil and germs floated on the surface; sick people bathed alongside healthy ones. Archaeological studies of Roman sites found intestinal parasites (whipworm, roundworm) did not decrease despite baths, toilets and sewers.
- **Public latrines:** multi-seat communal toilets; shared sponge on a stick (tersorium) possibly used for wiping.
- **Urine:** collected for fulling and laundry; Emperor Vespasian taxed it, origin of the phrase *pecunia non olet* ("money does not stink").

### Ancient China

- Han dynasty officials received a day off every five days for bathing and hair washing (*xiumu*).
- Bathing frequency varied widely by period and class.
- Soap beans (from the honey locust tree) and other saponin-rich plants used as cleansers.
- Early toothbrushes made from bristles fixed to bone or bamboo handles appear in China by the Tang or Song era (the bristle toothbrush is often credited to China).
- Chinese medicine linked bathing to balance of heat and cold in the body.

### India

- Daily bathing tied to Hindu religious practice; bathing before prayer and in sacred rivers.
- Neem and babul twigs chewed as toothbrushes (still used today).
- Ayurvedic oil massage (abhyanga) followed by bathing.
- The English word "shampoo" comes from the Hindi *champo*, meaning to press or knead (massage).
- Herbal hair cleansers: soapnut (reetha), shikakai and amla.

## Part 9: Other Cultures and Traditions

### Islamic world

- **Wudu:** ritual washing of hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head and feet before each of the five daily prayers.
- **Ghusl:** full-body ritual washing after sex, menstruation, childbirth and before Friday prayer.
- **Hammams:** public steam baths inherited from Roman and Byzantine baths and expanded throughout the Islamic world. Cities such as Baghdad, Cairo and Damascus had large numbers of them.
- **Hammam sequence:** warm room, hot steam room, scrubbing with a rough mitt (kese), soaping, rinsing, resting.
- **Miswak:** chewing stick from the *Salvadora persica* tree used for teeth, encouraged in hadith.
- Soap-making with olive oil was advanced and exported to Europe.

### Ottoman Empire

- Hammams served as social hubs, especially for women.
- Bathing was part of wedding rituals and religious observance.
- Famous architect Mimar Sinan designed several grand hammams in the 1500s.

### Japan

- Public bathing (sento) began with Buddhist temple baths, especially from the Nara period (700s).
- Sento spread among commoners in the Edo period (1603 to 1868).
- Mixed-gender bathing was common until the Meiji government restricted it in the late 1800s under Western pressure.
- **Ofuro:** deep home soaking tub; people wash and rinse thoroughly outside the tub first, then soak in clean hot water shared by the family.
- **Onsen:** natural hot spring baths.
- Shinto concepts of ritual purity (misogi, harae) shaped attitudes toward cleanliness.
- Daily bathing remains the cultural norm, usually in the evening.

### Korea

- Public bathhouses (mogyoktang) and modern jjimjilbang (bathhouse and sauna complexes).
- Body scrubbing with exfoliating cloths (Italy towels) to remove dead skin.

### Finland and Scandinavia

- **Sauna:** Finnish steam bathing tradition over 1,000 years old.
- Smoke saunas (savusauna) were the original form.
- Saunas were used for bathing, childbirth, healing and preparing the dead.
- **Vikings:** archaeological finds include combs, tweezers, razors and ear spoons. An English chronicler (attributed to John of Wallingford) complained that Danes in England combed their hair daily, bathed on Saturdays and changed clothes often, attracting English women. The Old Norse word for Saturday, *laugardagr*, means "washing day."

### Russia and Eastern Europe

- **Banya:** wood-fired steam bath with beating by bundles of birch or oak twigs (venik).
- The *Primary Chronicle* includes a tale of the Apostle Andrew observing Slavs whipping themselves in steam baths and pouring cold water on themselves.
- Weekly banya was traditional, especially on Saturdays.

### Mesoamerica (Aztecs, Maya)

- Aztecs bathed daily in rivers and lakes.
- **Temazcal:** sweat lodge-style steam baths used for cleansing, healing, childbirth recovery and ritual purity.
- Spanish chroniclers such as Bernal Díaz described Moctezuma bathing daily and changing clothes frequently.
- Soap substitutes: fruit of the soapberry tree (copalxocotl) and roots with saponins.
- Spanish conquerors were often described as smelling bad by Indigenous observers.

### Indigenous North America

- Many peoples bathed in rivers daily, even in winter.
- **Sweat lodges:** steam purification for physical and spiritual cleansing.
- Yucca root used as soap by many Southwest peoples.
- Bear grease and animal fats used on hair and skin for protection.

### Sub-Saharan Africa

- Very diverse practices across hundreds of cultures.
- **African black soap:** made in West Africa from ash of plantain skins, cocoa pods or palm leaves combined with palm or shea oils.
- **Shea butter:** used on skin and hair.
- Chewing sticks from various trees for teeth.
- River and stream bathing common.
- Himba people of Namibia use otjize (ochre and butterfat paste) and smoke baths with aromatic herbs instead of water due to water scarcity.

### Polynesia and the Pacific

- Frequent sea and freshwater bathing.
- Coconut oil (often scented with flowers) used on skin and hair.
- European sailors were often described as dirty and foul-smelling by Pacific Islanders.

### Ancient Hebrew tradition

- Ritual purity laws in the Torah include washing after contact with bodily fluids, disease and the dead.
- **Mikveh:** ritual immersion bath, still used today.
- Handwashing before meals (netilat yadayim) is a ritual practice.
- Some historians suggest these practices may have reduced disease spread among Jewish communities in medieval Europe.

## Part 10: Modern Hygiene History (1800 to Present)

### Germ theory revolution

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1847 | Ignaz Semmelweis introduces handwashing with chlorinated lime at a Vienna maternity clinic; maternal deaths from childbed fever drop sharply (from around 10% or higher in some periods to around 1 to 2%). He is ridiculed and dies in an asylum in 1865 |
| 1854 | John Snow traces a London cholera outbreak to the Broad Street water pump |
| 1858 | London's "Great Stink": raw sewage in the Thames causes Parliament to fund a modern sewer system |
| 1860s | Louis Pasteur's work establishes germ theory |
| 1867 | Joseph Lister publishes on antiseptic surgery using carbolic acid |
| 1876 to 1882 | Robert Koch identifies the bacteria causing anthrax, tuberculosis and later cholera |
| 1909 | Charles Nicolle identifies body lice as the vector of epidemic typhus (Nobel Prize 1928) |

### Public baths movement

- **Britain:** Baths and Washhouses Act of 1846 allowed councils to build public baths and laundries for the poor.
- **United States:** cities such as New York built public baths in the late 1800s and early 1900s; many tenements lacked bathing facilities.
- **Germany:** "a bath for every German every week" was a slogan of the public bath movement.

### Household plumbing in the United States

- In 1940, close to half of US homes lacked complete plumbing (hot and cold running water, a bathtub or shower and a flush toilet).
- Home plumbing spread rapidly after World War II.
- Daily showering became the American norm during the 1950s to 1970s.

### Commercial products timeline

| Year | Product |
|---|---|
| 1888 | Mum, the first commercial deodorant (US) |
| 1890s | Toothpaste sold in collapsible tubes (Colgate around 1896) |
| 1903 | Everdry, an early antiperspirant |

### Advertising and social anxiety

Much of the modern fear of body odor and dirt was shaped by early 20th century advertising. Companies created and amplified anxieties about smell, bad breath and dandruff to sell products.

### World War hygiene

**World War I:**
- Body lice infested the vast majority of frontline soldiers.
- Soldiers burned lice out of seams with candles; picking lice was called "chatting" in British slang.
- Delousing stations used steam, hot air and chemicals on clothing.
- Brewery vats and large tubs used for mass bathing parades behind the lines.
- Keating's Powder (an insecticide powder) was widely used with limited success.
- Trench fever (*Bartonella quintana*) was spread by lice feces scratched into bite wounds.
- Trench foot prevented by dry socks (soldiers were told to carry several pairs), foot inspections and whale oil.

**World War II:**
- DDT was used to stop a typhus epidemic in Naples in 1943 to 1944 by dusting civilians and soldiers.
- Better sanitation and vaccines reduced disease deaths compared to earlier wars.
- Submariners and soldiers in remote areas still went weeks without bathing.

### Modern global bathing frequency (survey averages, varies by source)

| Country / region | General pattern |
|---|---|
| Brazil | Among the highest; often more than once per day in hot climate |
| Japan | Nightly bath or shower is standard |
| United States | Most adults shower daily or nearly daily |
| United Kingdom | Many shower daily; a significant minority less often |
| France | Historically lower than US; now near daily for many |
| China | Varies; surveys have shown lower average frequency than the US, especially in northern and rural areas with cold, dry climates |
| Scandinavia | Frequent showering plus sauna culture |

### Modern dermatologist consensus

- Daily full-body soaping is not medically necessary for most people.
- Washing armpits, groin, feet and face daily (or after sweating) covers most hygiene needs.
- Full showers 2 to 3 times per week are considered adequate for many people with low activity levels.
- Daily showers are fine if soap use is moderate and skin is moisturized.
- Hair: anywhere from daily to weekly or less depending on hair type.
- Hands: frequently, especially before eating and after toilet use.

### Minimum hygiene for survival in a harsh setting (soldiers, expeditions, wilderness)

1. Wash hands before eating and after toilet use (water and ash or sand if no soap).
2. Keep feet dry; change socks daily if possible; air feet out.
3. Clean all wounds immediately.
4. Clean teeth daily with any method available.
5. Wash or rotate underwear and socks.
6. Clean groin and armpits every few days with a damp cloth.
7. Inspect clothing seams for lice; heat or boil clothes when possible.
8. Avoid drinking unclean water.
9. Keep toilets away from water sources and food prep.

## Part 13: Worldbuilding Notes

### Making hygiene realistic in fiction

- **Smell is the first sign.** Characters notice others before themselves.
- **Clothing tells the story.** Rich characters change linen often; poor characters wear the same clothes for weeks.
- **Lice are everywhere** in premodern settings and do not mark someone as unusually dirty.
- **Hands and food** are where disease actually spreads. A plague scene works best through water, food and lice rather than "unwashed bodies."
- **Soldiers and adventurers** on campaign go weeks unwashed; trench foot, lice and rashes are realistic consequences.
- **Bathing as luxury:** hot water required fuel and labor; a hot bath was a sign of wealth or hospitality.
- **Inns and bathhouses** can serve as social hubs, places for gossip, deals, romance or ambushes.
- **Religion shapes hygiene:** ritual washing laws, purity concepts and prohibitions create cultural differences between groups.
- **Cultural clash:** one group finding another smelly or dirty is a historically consistent pattern (Aztecs and Spanish, Pacific Islanders and European sailors, English chroniclers and Danes).

### Superhuman and magical character design questions

- Can they carry disease to normal people?
- Do they still need clean clothes?
- Do companions complain about their smell?
- Does their power make them smell different (sulfur, ozone, smoke, flowers, metal)?
- Do enhanced senses make them more sensitive to others' odors? A superhuman with a sharp sense of smell would find premodern or crowded settings overwhelming.
- Does advancing in power (leveling, cultivation, tempering) expel impurities that require bathing?
- Does magic replace bathing entirely for the wealthy or powerful, creating a class marker?
