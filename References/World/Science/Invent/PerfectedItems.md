# Universally Widespread Items That Reached Perfection

Earth manufacturing notes for everyday tools whose form is settled. Century invent bands: `CraftsmanByCentury.md`. Shop precision: `MechanicalPrecision.md`. Industrial order: `MedievalIndustrialization.md`. Writing tools: `WritingTools.md`. Matches seed: `Printing.md`.

## 1. Sewing Needle

Over 40,000 years old. A pointed shaft with an eye has never been surpassed.

**Materials:** High-carbon steel wire. Nickel, gold or platinum plating.

**Process:**

1. **Wire drawing:** Steel rod is pulled through a series of progressively smaller tungsten carbide or diamond dies until it reaches the needle's final diameter. The wire is then straightened and cleaned.
2. **Cutting:** The wire is cut into lengths equal to two needles. Bundles of these blanks are rolled between steel plates under pressure to make each one perfectly straight.
3. **Pointing:** Both ends of each double-length blank are ground on rotating abrasive wheels while the blank spins, producing a cone-shaped point at each end.
4. **Stamping:** The center of the blank is struck between dies that flatten it slightly and imprint two eye shapes plus the long grooves that guide thread into each eye.
5. **Punching the eyes:** A press punches both eyes through in a single stroke.
6. **Splitting:** Blanks are threaded onto two parallel wires running through the eyes. The strip is flexed until it breaks apart in the middle, leaving two rows of single needles.
7. **Head filing:** The rough ends left by the split are ground smooth and rounded.
8. **Hardening:** Needles are heated to about 800°C and quenched in oil. This makes the steel very hard but brittle.
9. **Tempering:** Needles are reheated to around 300°C to restore toughness so they flex slightly instead of snapping.
10. **Scouring and polishing:** Needles are tumbled for hours or days in bags with abrasive paste, soap and oil. This removes scale and leaves a smooth surface. The inside of the eye is polished separately with fine abrasive threads drawn through it.
11. **Plating:** Needles are electroplated with nickel for corrosion resistance. Premium needles get gold-plated eyes or platinum coating to reduce drag through fabric.
12. **Inspection and packing:** Machines and inspectors check straightness, point sharpness and eye smoothness before packing.

## 2. Button and Buttonhole

In use since medieval Europe and found on clothing worldwide.

**Materials:** Polyester resin, nylon, brass, zinc alloy, shell, horn, wood or corozo nut. Polyester or cotton thread.

**Polyester buttons (most common):**

1. **Mixing:** Liquid polyester resin is mixed with a catalyst and pigments.
2. **Centrifugal casting:** The mixture is poured into a spinning cylindrical drum. Centrifugal force spreads it into a flat sheet against the drum wall. The sheet is removed while still soft and rubbery.
3. **Blanking:** A punching machine cuts round blanks from the sheet.
4. **Curing:** Blanks are fully hardened in a warm water bath or oven.
5. **Turning:** Automatic lathes cut the front profile and back shape, including rims, domes or dished centers.
6. **Drilling:** Drills bore two or four thread holes. Shank buttons get a molded or attached loop on the back instead.
7. **Polishing:** Buttons are tumbled with abrasive media and polishing compounds. Some are dyed or lacquered at this stage.

**Metal buttons:**

1. Brass or steel sheet is stamped into a front cap and a back plate.
2. Designs are embossed during stamping.
3. The two halves are crimped together around a wire shank loop.
4. Finished buttons are plated or enameled.

**Buttonhole:**

1. The garment panel is placed under a dedicated buttonhole sewing machine.
2. The machine sews a narrow, dense zigzag (satin stitch) along both sides of where the slit will be. Heavier bar tacks are sewn across each end to stop tearing.
3. A blade cuts the slit between the stitched rows. Shirts use cut-after buttonholes. Coats and jeans often use cut-first keyhole buttonholes.
4. Keyhole buttonholes have a round eyelet punched at one end for the button's shank to rest in. Many are corded with a filler thread under the stitches for added strength.
5. **Button attaching:** A button sewing machine holds the button in position and stitches through its holes in a set pattern before knotting off the thread.

## 3. Spoon

Prehistoric origin and identical in function across every culture.

**Materials:** Stainless steel, most commonly 18/10 (18% chromium and 10% nickel) or 18/0.

**Process:**

1. **Blanking:** Stainless steel sheet is fed into a press that stamps flat blanks in the rough outline of a spoon.
2. **Cross rolling:** Blanks pass through rollers lengthwise and crosswise. This thins the bowl area and tapers the handle to their correct thicknesses while stretching the metal larger.
3. **Annealing:** Rolling hardens the steel, so blanks are heated in a furnace to soften them for further forming.
4. **Trimming:** A second press cuts the rolled blank to the exact final outline.
5. **Forming:** The blank is struck between hardened steel dies under heavy pressure. This curves the bowl, shapes the handle and imprints any decorative pattern in one blow.
6. **Edge grinding:** Edges are ground smooth to remove burrs left from cutting.
7. **Polishing:** Spoons pass through several stages of progressively finer abrasive belts and cloth buffing wheels with polishing compounds, producing a mirror or satin finish.
8. **Cleaning and inspection:** Polishing residue is removed in ultrasonic baths. Spoons are inspected and packed.

**Wooden spoons:** A blank is sawn from hardwood such as beech, cherry or maple. The bowl is hollowed with a hook knife or a router. The handle is carved or shaped on a band saw and spindle sander. The spoon is sanded smooth and finished with food-safe oil.

## 4. Scissors

The pivoted cross-blade design dates to ancient Rome.

**Materials:** Stainless or high-carbon steel. Plastic or steel handles. Steel pivot screw or rivet.

**Process:**

1. **Blade blanks:** Premium scissors are hot forged between dies. Common scissors are stamped or laser cut from steel sheet.
2. **Pivot hole:** The pivot hole is drilled or punched at the joint.
3. **Hardening:** Blades are heated to roughly 1,000°C and quenched, then tempered to balance hardness with toughness.
4. **Grinding:** Each blade's inner face is ground slightly concave (hollow ground) so only the cutting edges touch. The outer bevel of each cutting edge is ground at a set angle.
5. **Setting:** Each blade is given a slight bow toward the other. This is the critical step. It makes the blades meet at only one moving point as they close, pressing the edges together exactly where the cut happens.
6. **Handles:** Common scissors get plastic handles injection molded directly over the steel tangs. Forged scissors have steel handles formed during forging, often coated or wrapped.
7. **Assembly:** The two halves are joined with a screw or rivet at the pivot. Tension is adjusted so the blades close smoothly with no gap.
8. **Sharpening and honing:** Edges are finished on fine abrasive wheels and honed.
9. **Testing:** Each pair is test cut through cloth or paper along the full length of the blade.

## 5. Hammer

The claw hammer has kept its basic form since Roman times.

**Materials:** High-carbon steel head. Hickory, ash, fiberglass or steel handle. Steel or wooden wedges.

**Process:**

1. **Cutting billets:** Steel bar is cut into billets sized for one head.
2. **Heating:** Billets are heated to about 1,200°C in an induction furnace.
3. **Drop forging:** The glowing billet is placed in a lower die and a power hammer or press drives the upper die down repeatedly. Several die stations shape it progressively into the face, cheeks, neck and claw.
4. **Trimming:** Excess metal squeezed out between the dies (flash) is trimmed off in a press. The eye for the handle is punched through.
5. **Claw splitting:** The V-shaped notch of the claw is forged or cut.
6. **Heat treatment:** The head is hardened and tempered. The striking face and claw tips are often hardened further by induction heating and quenching only those areas. The middle stays tougher so the head resists cracking.
7. **Grinding:** The face is ground flat with a slight bevel around its edge to prevent chipping. Framing hammers get a milled waffle pattern cut into the face.
8. **Finishing:** Heads are shot blasted, then painted, powder coated or polished.
9. **Handle making:** Wood handles are turned on a lathe, sanded and lacquered. Fiberglass handles are made by pultrusion and fitted with a rubber grip. One-piece steel hammers are forged with the handle and head together.
10. **Hanging the head:** A wood handle is driven into the eye. Its top end is split and a wooden or steel wedge is hammered in to expand it and lock the head tight. Fiberglass handles are bonded into the eye with epoxy.

## 6. Nail

Thousands of years old. Only the manufacturing method has changed.

**Materials:** Low-carbon steel wire. Zinc, vinyl or cement coatings.

**Process:**

1. **Wire drawing:** Steel rod is pulled through dies down to the gauge required for the nail.
2. **Feeding:** Wire from a coil passes through straightening rollers into the nail machine.
3. **Gripping:** Gripping dies clamp the wire, leaving a short length sticking out.
4. **Heading:** A punch strikes the protruding end, flattening it into the head.
5. **Advancing:** The wire is pushed forward by the length of one nail.
6. **Pointing and cutting:** Cutters close from the sides, forming the four-sided point and severing the nail from the wire in the same motion.
7. **Ejection:** A kicker pushes the finished nail out. The cycle repeats at hundreds of nails per minute.
8. **Shank shaping:** Ring shank and spiral shank nails have their ridges rolled on at this stage.
9. **Tumbling:** Nails are tumbled with sawdust to remove oil and burrs.
10. **Finishing:** Nails may be hot-dip galvanized for outdoor use, electro-galvanized, vinyl coated or cement coated for extra grip.
11. **Collation:** Nails for nail guns are joined in strips or coils with plastic, paper or welded wire.

## 7. Brick

Fired clay bricks date back roughly 5,000 years with the same proportions still in use.

**Materials:** Clay, shale, water. Sand or pigments for surface texture and color.

**Process:**

1. **Mining:** Clay and shale are dug from open pits.
2. **Preparation:** Raw material is crushed, ground fine in pan mills and screened to remove large particles. Material from different pit layers is blended for consistent color.
3. **Tempering:** Water is mixed in with a pug mill to form a stiff, workable mud.
4. **De-airing and extrusion:** The mud passes through a vacuum chamber that removes trapped air to prevent cracking. It is then forced through a die that forms a continuous column with the brick's width and height. Pins in the die form the holes (cores).
5. **Texturing:** Rollers, brushes or sand sprayers can texture or color the surface of the column.
6. **Cutting:** A frame of taut wires slices the column into individual bricks.
7. **Drying:** Bricks are stacked on kiln cars and dried in chambers at about 40 to 150°C for one to two days. Firing wet bricks would make them crack or burst.
8. **Firing:** Kiln cars roll slowly through a tunnel kiln, often over 100 meters long, over two to five days. Temperatures reach roughly 900 to 1,200°C. Clay particles partially melt and fuse together (vitrification), which gives the brick its strength. Final color depends on iron content and kiln atmosphere.
9. **Cooling:** Bricks cool gradually in the last zone of the kiln to prevent thermal cracking.
10. **Packaging:** Bricks are sorted, stacked into cubes and banded for shipping.

**Molded bricks:** Soft mud is pressed into sanded wooden or steel molds instead of being extruded, giving a handmade look.

## 8. Rubber Band

Patented by Stephen Perry in 1845 and unchanged since.

**Materials:** Natural rubber, sulfur, zinc oxide, accelerators, pigments, talc.

**Process:**

1. **Raw rubber:** Latex is tapped from rubber trees, coagulated with acid, dried and pressed into bales.
2. **Mixing:** Bales are broken down in a heavy internal mixer and blended with sulfur, zinc oxide, curing accelerators, fillers and pigments. Batches are cooled to prevent premature curing.
3. **Milling:** The compound is rolled into sheets on twin-roll mills and cut into strips.
4. **Extrusion:** Strips are fed into an extruder that forces the rubber through a die around a central pin, forming a long hollow tube. The tube is slid onto aluminum poles.
5. **Vulcanization:** The poles are loaded into large steam-heated vessels. Heat makes the sulfur form crosslinks between rubber molecules. This turns soft sticky rubber into a stable elastic material.
6. **Removal and washing:** Tubes are pulled off the poles and washed.
7. **Slicing:** A cutting machine slices the tubes into rings. Slice spacing sets the band's width. Tube diameter sets the band's length.
8. **Finishing:** Bands are tumbled with talc so they do not stick together, then weighed and bagged.

## 9. Binder Clip

Louis Baltzley, 1910. Folding steel clip with two wire handles.

**Materials:** Spring steel strip. Steel wire. Paint, powder coat or plating.

**Process:**

1. **Blanking:** Spring steel strip feeds into a press that cuts flat blanks with slots punched along the edges for the handle tabs.
2. **Forming:** Progressive dies bend each blank into the triangular clamp shape. The top edges are rolled into small channels that will hold the handles.
3. **Heat treatment:** Clamps are hardened and tempered to give the spring tension that snaps them shut.
4. **Deburring:** Clamps are tumbled to remove sharp edges.
5. **Finishing:** Clamps are painted black, powder coated in colors or plated with zinc or nickel.
6. **Handle wires:** A wire-forming machine cuts and bends steel wire into the handle shape with inward-turned ends. Handles are nickel plated.
7. **Assembly:** A machine squeezes each handle's legs together, inserts the ends into the slots in the rolled edge and releases them. The legs spring apart and lock in place.
8. **Testing:** Samples are tested for clamping force and handle retention.

## 10. Crown Bottle Cap

William Painter, 1892. Still sealing glass bottles globally.

**Materials:** Thin tinplate or chrome-coated steel sheet. Lacquer, printing inks. Polymer liner compound (originally cork).

**Process:**

1. **Coating:** Steel sheets are lacquered on the inner side to prevent corrosion and keep metal away from the drink.
2. **Printing:** The outer side is printed with logos and designs by offset lithography. Sheets are baked in ovens to cure the inks.
3. **Stamping:** A high-speed press punches circular discs and forms them in one stroke into the cap shell with 21 pleated teeth around the skirt.
4. **Lining:** A pellet of molten polymer is dropped into each shell and pressed by a cooled punch into a flat liner with a raised sealing ring. Some liners contain oxygen scavengers to protect beer flavor.
5. **Inspection:** Cameras check print alignment and liner placement. Defective caps are rejected.
6. **Packing:** Caps are counted into cartons for bottlers.
7. **Capping:** At the bottling plant, a capping head pushes the cap onto the bottle and crimps the teeth under the glass lip. The compressed liner forms a gas-tight seal that holds carbonation.

## 11. Pin Tumbler Lock

Linus Yale Jr., 1860s. Still the basis for most door locks and keys.

**Materials:** Brass, zinc alloy or steel housing. Brass plug and pins. Stainless steel springs. Brass or nickel silver keys.

**Process:**

1. **Plug:** Brass bar stock is turned on a CNC lathe into the cylindrical plug. The keyway profile is broached through it with a toothed cutting tool so only matching key blanks fit.
2. **Housing:** The housing is die cast or machined with a bore that holds the plug.
3. **Pin holes:** A row of five or six holes is drilled through the housing and into the plug in perfect alignment.
4. **Pins:** Key pins are machined from brass rod in several lengths that correspond to the key cut depths. Driver pins are a uniform length. Security locks use spool or serrated driver pins to resist picking.
5. **Springs:** Small coil springs are wound from stainless steel wire.
6. **Pinning:** For each position a key pin matching that lock's code is dropped into the plug hole. A driver pin and spring go into the housing hole above it. A cap or strip seals the top.
7. **Assembly:** The plug is inserted into the housing and held by a retaining clip at the rear. A cam or tailpiece is attached to operate the latch or bolt.
8. **Key making:** Key blanks are stamped with the matching keyway profile. A key cutting machine cuts notches into the blade at depths that match the key pins. With the correct key inserted, the top of every key pin sits exactly at the shear line between plug and housing. The plug can then rotate.
9. **Finishing and testing:** Housings are plated in brass, nickel or chrome and each lock is tested with its key.

## 12. Push Pin

Edwin Moore, 1900. Found on bulletin boards everywhere.

**Materials:** Steel wire. Polystyrene or ABS plastic.

**Process:**

1. **Pin forming:** Steel wire is fed into a heading machine that cuts it to length, forms a small head or barbs at one end and grinds a sharp point at the other.
2. **Plating:** Pins are nickel or zinc plated against rust.
3. **Insert molding:** Pins are loaded point-down into an injection mold. Molten plastic is injected around the blunt end to form the cylindrical grip. The head or barbs lock the pin inside the plastic.
4. **Ejection and inspection:** Finished pins are ejected, inspected for straightness and packed.

**Thumbtacks:** A flat steel disc is stamped from sheet. The point is either stamped out of the disc itself and bent down or made from a separate pin riveted through the center.

## 13. Safety Match

Developed in Sweden in the 1840s and still produced the same way.

**Materials:** Aspen or poplar wood. Ammonium phosphate. Paraffin wax. Head compound of potassium chlorate, sulfur, powdered glass, fillers, glue and pigment. Striker of red phosphorus, powdered glass and binder. Paperboard box.

**Process:**

1. **Splints:** Logs are debarked, softened with steam and peeled on a rotary lathe into thin sheets of veneer. Chopping machines cut the veneer into square splints.
2. **Impregnation:** Splints are soaked in ammonium phosphate solution so they stop glowing once blown out.
3. **Drying and polishing:** Splints are dried and tumbled to polish them. Broken or crooked splints are screened out.
4. **Loading:** Splints are pushed into perforated plates on a long continuous chain that carries them through every following step.
5. **Paraffin dip:** The ends are dipped in molten paraffin wax. This helps the flame spread from the head to the wood.
6. **Head dip:** The ends are dipped into the head compound and coated evenly.
7. **Drying:** The chain carries the matches through a drying zone for about an hour.
8. **Boxing:** Matches are punched out of the plates into inner box trays. The trays slide into outer sleeves.
9. **Striker coating:** The sides of the box are coated with the red phosphorus striker mixture.
10. **How it works:** Striking rubs a trace of red phosphorus from the box onto the head. Friction heat ignites it and sets off the potassium chlorate and sulfur. The phosphorus stays on the box and not the head, so the match will not light by accident.

## 14. Corrugated Cardboard Box

Robert Gair's precut box design from the 1890s now ships most of the world's goods.

**Materials:** Kraft linerboard, corrugating medium, starch glue, inks.

**Process:**

1. **Papermaking:** Wood pulp, often mixed with recycled fiber, is formed on paper machines into heavy linerboard for the flat faces and lighter medium for the wavy middle. Both are wound into huge reels.
2. **Corrugating:** The medium is softened with steam and fed between heated fluted rollers that press it into waves. Flute sizes are labeled A, B, C, E and F from coarse to fine.
3. **Single facer:** Starch glue is applied to the flute tips on one side and a liner sheet is pressed on, creating single-faced board.
4. **Double backer:** Glue is applied to the other side's flute tips and a second liner is added. The board travels over heated plates that set the glue. Double-wall board adds another layer of medium and liner.
5. **Slitting and scoring:** The continuous board is slit to width and scored along future fold lines.
6. **Sheeting:** A rotary knife cuts the board into sheets.
7. **Printing:** Flexographic presses print logos and labels onto the sheets.
8. **Converting:** A flexo folder-gluer slots the flaps, cuts hand holes, folds the sheet and glues the side seam to make a flat, collapsed box. Custom shapes are cut on rotary or flatbed die cutters.
9. **Bundling:** Flat boxes are counted, stacked and strapped for shipping. The end user unfolds them, folds the flaps and tapes the bottom.

## 15. Cable Tie (Zip Tie)

Thomas & Betts, 1958. One-way ratchet strap used in every industry.

**Materials:** Nylon 6/6. Carbon black for UV-resistant versions.

**Process:**

1. **Drying:** Nylon pellets are dried because absorbed moisture causes molding defects.
2. **Injection molding:** Pellets are melted at about 270 to 300°C and injected into a multi-cavity steel mold. The strap, teeth, head and pawl all form in one shot.
3. **Cooling and ejection:** The mold is cooled, the ties are ejected and the runners are trimmed off.
4. **Conditioning:** Ties are exposed to humidity so the nylon absorbs a small amount of water. Dry nylon is brittle and conditioned nylon is flexible.
5. **Testing:** Samples are loop-tested for tensile strength.
6. **Packing:** Ties are counted into sealed bags that keep their moisture content stable.
7. **How it works:** The head contains a small flexible pawl angled so the teeth slide past it going in and catch on it going out.

## 16. Duct Tape

Developed during World War II and used everywhere since.

**Materials:** Cotton or polyester scrim. Polyethylene film with aluminum pigment. Rubber-based adhesive.

**Process:**

1. **Scrim:** Cotton or polyester threads are woven or laid into an open mesh fabric. This gives strength in both directions and lets the tape tear cleanly by hand.
2. **Film:** Polyethylene is extruded into a thin film, usually colored silver with aluminum powder.
3. **Lamination:** The scrim and film are bonded with heat and pressure. Some lines extrude molten polyethylene directly onto the scrim.
4. **Adhesive compounding:** Natural or synthetic rubber is mixed with tackifying resins and fillers in a heavy mixer.
5. **Coating:** A calender or set of coating rollers spreads a thick, even layer of adhesive onto the scrim side.
6. **No release liner needed:** The polyethylene face naturally releases from the rubber adhesive, so the tape can be wound directly on itself.
7. **Winding:** The wide coated sheet is wound into large jumbo rolls.
8. **Slitting:** Jumbo rolls are unwound, slit into standard widths such as 48 mm and rewound onto cardboard cores. Some plants saw whole wound logs into individual rolls instead.
9. **Packing:** Rolls are labeled, shrink wrapped and boxed.

## 17. Bucket

Ancient origin and universal across all cultures.

**Plastic buckets:**

1. **Material:** HDPE or polypropylene pellets are mixed with colorant.
2. **Injection molding:** The plastic is melted and injected into a steel mold. The sides are tapered so the bucket releases from the mold and stacks inside other buckets.
3. **Cooling and ejection:** Water channels in the mold cool the part before it is ejected.
4. **Handle:** Steel wire is cut and bent with hooked ends. A plastic grip is slid on and the ends are snapped into molded ears on the bucket.
5. **Lids:** Lids are molded separately with a snap ring that locks over the rim.

**Metal buckets:**

1. **Blanking:** Galvanized steel sheet is cut into circular or rectangular blanks.
2. **Forming:** A press deep draws a circular blank into a seamless tapered bucket in one or more stages. Alternatively a flat blank is rolled into a cone, the side seam is locked or welded and a separate bottom is double seamed on.
3. **Wiring the rim:** The top edge is rolled over a steel wire for stiffness and safety.
4. **Ears:** Handle ears are riveted or spot welded to the sides.
5. **Bail handle:** A wire handle is hooked into the ears.
6. **Galvanizing:** Buckets made from bare steel are hot-dip galvanized after forming.

## 18. Ladder

Depicted in cave paintings about 10,000 years old.

**Aluminum ladders:**

1. **Extrusion:** Heated aluminum billets are forced through dies to form channel-shaped rails and ribbed rungs.
2. **Cutting and aging:** Profiles are cut to length and heat treated to reach full strength.
3. **Punching:** Rung holes are punched along each rail.
4. **Rung attachment:** Rungs are pushed through the rails. A swaging tool flares the rung ends against the rails, locking them permanently.
5. **Hardware:** Extension ladders get guide brackets, rung locks, a pulley and rope. Step ladders get hinged spreader braces and a molded top cap.
6. **Feet and caps:** Rubber or plastic feet and end caps are fitted.
7. **Testing:** Ladders are load tested to safety standards and labeled with their duty rating.

**Fiberglass ladders:**

1. **Pultrusion:** Glass fiber strands and mat are pulled through a resin bath and then through a heated die where the resin cures into a solid rail profile.
2. **Assembly:** Rails are cut, drilled and fitted with aluminum rungs and hardware. Fiberglass does not conduct electricity, which makes these the standard for electrical work.

**Wooden ladders:**

1. Rails are sawn from straight-grained spruce or hardwood.
2. Rungs are shaped and fitted into mortises cut in the rails.
3. Joints are glued and reinforced with steel rods running under the rungs.
4. The ladder is sanded and sealed.

## 19. Wheelbarrow

Used in China roughly 2,000 years ago with the same single wheel and two handles.

**Materials:** Steel sheet or polyethylene tray. Steel tube frame or hardwood handles. Steel wheel rim, pneumatic or solid tire, bearings. Bolts.

**Process:**

1. **Tray:** Steel sheet is deep drawn in a large hydraulic press into the tray shape and the edges are rolled for stiffness. Poly trays are made by injection or rotational molding.
2. **Frame:** Steel tube is cut and bent into the handle and frame shape on a tube bender. Braces are cut and welded in place. Some models use hardwood handles instead.
3. **Legs:** Legs are bent from steel bar or tube.
4. **Finishing:** Steel parts are degreased, phosphate treated, powder coated and baked in a curing oven.
5. **Wheel:** A steel rim is pressed or rolled and fitted with a hub and bearings. A pneumatic tire with an inner tube or a solid flat-free tire is mounted.
6. **Assembly:** The tray is bolted to the frame. The legs are bolted under the rear. The wheel axle is mounted in brackets at the front. Plastic or rubber grips are pushed onto the handles.

## 20. Rope

Twisted fiber cordage dates to prehistoric times. Only the materials have changed.

**Natural fiber rope (manila, sisal, hemp, cotton):**

1. **Fiber extraction:** Fibers are stripped from the plant leaves or stalks and dried.
2. **Hackling:** Fibers are combed through rows of steel pins to align them and remove short pieces. They are drawn into a continuous loose ribbon called a sliver.
3. **Spinning:** The sliver is twisted into yarn, usually in a right-hand direction.
4. **Forming strands:** Several yarns are twisted together in the opposite direction to form a strand.
5. **Laying:** Three strands are twisted together in the original direction. The opposing twists pull against each other and lock the rope together so it does not unravel. This was traditionally done on rope walks, long buildings that could exceed 300 meters.
6. **Finishing:** Ends are whipped with twine or dipped to prevent fraying.

**Synthetic rope (nylon, polyester, polypropylene, UHMWPE):**

1. **Filament spinning:** Polymer is melted and pushed through spinnerets with many tiny holes, forming continuous filaments.
2. **Drawing:** Filaments are stretched to several times their length, aligning the molecules and greatly increasing strength.
3. **Yarn making:** Filaments are twisted together into yarns.
4. **Braiding or laying:** Braiding machines run carriers in crossing serpentine paths to weave yarns around each other. Double-braid rope has a braided core inside a braided cover. Climbing rope (kernmantle) has a core of twisted or parallel strands inside a tightly woven sheath. Three-strand synthetic rope is laid the same way as natural rope.
5. **Heat setting:** The rope is heated under tension to stabilize its length and structure.
6. **Cutting:** Rope is cut to length with a hot knife that melts and seals the ends.
