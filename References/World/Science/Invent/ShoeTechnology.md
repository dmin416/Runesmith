# Best Shoe Technology Throughout History

**Footwear itself is the greatest shoe technology in history.** Toe bone evidence shows humans were wearing protective foot coverings by about 40,000 years ago, which reduced injury and let people cross ice, rock and thorny ground into every climate on Earth.

## Major Milestones

- **Woven plant sandals (about 10,000 years ago):** The Fort Rock sandals from Oregon, made of sagebrush bark, are among the oldest surviving shoes.
- **Leather tanning (prehistoric to ancient):** Treating hides with bark tannins made leather rot resistant. It stayed the dominant shoe material for over 5,000 years.
- **One-piece leather moccasins (about 5,500 years ago):** The Areni-1 shoe from Armenia was a single cowhide piece laced with leather cord.
- **Insulated boots (about 5,300 years ago):** Ötzi the Iceman wore bearskin soles and deerskin uppers stuffed with grass for warmth.
- **Waterproof sealskin boots (ancient Arctic):** Inuit kamiks used tightly stitched sealskin that kept feet dry in freezing water.
- **Hobnailed soles (Roman era):** Iron nails on caligae added grip and durability for armies marching thousands of miles.
- **Heels (about 10th century):** Persian cavalry used heels to lock feet into stirrups for mounted archery.
- **Raised wooden soles (ancient to medieval):** Japanese geta and European pattens lifted feet above mud and filth.
- **Turnshoe and welted construction (medieval):** Sewing the sole to the upper evolved into the resoleable welt.
- **Left and right lasts (early 1800s):** Shoes began to be shaped for each foot instead of made straight.
- **Vulcanized rubber (1839):** Charles Goodyear's process made rubber durable and flexible. It is the base of nearly every modern sole.
- **Goodyear welt machine (1869):** Charles Goodyear Jr.'s machine stitched uppers to soles quickly for shoes that could be resoled for decades.
- **Lasting machine (1883):** Jan Matzeliger's invention automated the hardest hand step and cut shoe prices dramatically.
- **Steel toe caps (1930s):** These cut workplace foot injuries sharply.
- **Vibram lug sole (1937):** Vitale Bramani's rubber lug pattern transformed hiking, mountaineering and military boots.
- **EVA foam midsoles (1970s):** Lightweight cushioning foam that made modern running shoes possible.
- **Gore-Tex membranes (late 1970s to 1980s):** Waterproof yet breathable boots.
- **Air cushioning (1979):** Nike's encapsulated gas units popularized engineered cushioning.
- **Carbon plate with PEBA "super foam" (2017):** The Nike Vaporfly pairing improved running economy by about 4% and rewrote marathon records.

## Shoes by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

**Construction and Trade**

- **Turnshoe construction:** The dominant method for most of the period. The shoe was sewn inside out on a last with a thin flexible sole and then turned right side out, which hid and protected the seam. Soles wore through quickly and were often patched or replaced with clump soles stitched over the bottom.
- **Cordwainers and cobblers:** Cordwainers made new shoes, often from fine goatskin called cordwain after Córdoba in Spain. Cobblers only repaired old shoes. Guilds strictly separated the two trades in many cities.
- **Welted construction (late 1400s):** A strip of leather stitched between upper and sole allowed thicker durable soles. This method spread through the Renaissance and became the standard for quality shoes.

**Common Styles**

- **Ankle shoes and low shoes:** Simple leather shoes fastened with toggles, laces, buckles or latchets (straps). Most people wore some version of these.
- **Ankle boots and calf boots:** Worn for travel, work and cold weather. Many fastened at the side or front with laces or toggles.
- **Poulaines and crakows (14th and 15th centuries):** Shoes with extremely long pointed toes, sometimes stuffed with moss or wool to hold their shape. Some English sumptuary laws limited toe length by social rank.
- **Pattens:** Wooden or later cork platforms strapped under shoes to raise them above mud, filth and wet streets. They protected expensive thin-soled shoes.
- **Wooden clogs:** Carved wooden shoes such as French sabots and Dutch klompen were cheap and durable footwear for peasants and laborers.
- **Sabatons:** Articulated steel foot armor worn by knights. Some late medieval sabatons copied the pointed poulaine fashion.
- **Monastic sandals and soft slippers:** Many religious orders wore simple sandals while wealthy households used soft indoor shoes of cloth or fine leather.

**Who Wore What**

- **Peasants:** Rough leather shoes, wrapped rawhide, clogs or bare feet in warm months.
- **Townspeople and merchants:** Turnshoes and ankle boots from local cordwainers.
- **Nobility:** Fine dyed leather, silk or velvet shoes, exaggerated poulaines and decorated pattens.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

**Construction and Trade**

- **Welted soles became widespread:** Thicker stitched soles made shoes more durable and easier to repair.
- **Heels arrived in Europe (late 1500s):** Persian riding heels reached Europe through diplomatic contact. Heels first appeared on riding boots and were adopted by fashionable men and women around 1600.
- **Left and right shoes were rare:** Most shoes were made on straight lasts so either shoe fit either foot.

**Common Styles**

- **Duckbill shoes (early 1500s):** Also called bear's paw or cow mouth shoes. These broad square toed shoes replaced the pointed poulaine. The toes of some reached about six inches wide during the reign of Henry VIII.
- **Slashed shoes:** Uppers were cut with decorative slits that revealed colorful silk or lining underneath. This matched the slashed doublets and sleeves of the period.
- **Chopines (Venice and Spain):** Platform shoes made of cork or wood covered with fabric or leather. Some surviving Venetian examples stand around 20 inches tall. Wearers often needed servants to help them walk. Height signaled wealth because more fabric was needed for the long skirts covering them.
- **Pantofles:** Slip-on mules with thick cork soles worn over regular shoes or indoors. They protected fine footwear much like pattens.
- **Latchet shoes:** Shoes with straps over the instep tied with ribbon. These were common in the late 1500s and later decorated with large ribbon rosettes.
- **Tall riding boots:** Soft leather boots that reached the knee or thigh. These were essential for horsemen and soldiers.
- **Peasant footwear:** Clogs, simple leather shoes and rawhide wraps continued largely unchanged from the medieval period.

**Who Wore What**

- **Laborers:** Sturdy plain leather shoes and wooden clogs.
- **Middle classes:** Welted leather shoes with modest decoration.
- **Nobility and royalty:** Velvet, silk and slashed leather shoes, chopines, embroidered pantofles and later heeled shoes.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

**Construction and Trade**

- **Industrial mass production:** Shoemaking moved from workshops to factories during this era.
- **Blake-McKay sole sewing machine (1858):** Lyman Blake's machine stitched soles directly to uppers. Gordon McKay improved and leased it widely. It helped supply Union boots during the American Civil War.
- **Goodyear welt machine (1869):** Mechanized the welted construction method.
- **Lasting machine (1883):** Jan Matzeliger's machine replaced the hardest hand step and cut costs dramatically.
- **Standard sizing and left/right shoes:** Shaped left and right shoes became normal and standardized sizing spread with factory production.
- **Vulcanized rubber:** Rubber soles, galoshes and rubber boots became affordable after Goodyear's 1839 process.

**Men's Styles**

- **Wellington boots:** Popularized by the Duke of Wellington before the Victorian era. Rubber Wellingtons appeared after the 1850s.
- **Balmoral boots:** Closed-lacing ankle boots associated with Prince Albert and Balmoral Castle. Their design influenced the modern Oxford shoe.
- **Oxford shoes:** Low-cut closed-lacing shoes that became standard formal footwear.
- **Derby and Blucher shoes:** Open-lacing shoes named after the Earl of Derby and Prussian Field Marshal Blücher. These offered an easier fit than Oxfords.
- **Brogues:** Originally rugged Irish and Scottish outdoor shoes with holes that let water drain. Their decorative perforations became fashionable in the late 1800s.
- **Spats:** Cloth coverings over the shoe and ankle that protected footwear from mud and splashes.
- **Hobnailed work boots:** Heavy boots with iron nails in the soles for laborers, miners and soldiers.

**Women's Styles**

- **Elastic-sided boots:** J. Sparkes-Hall introduced boots with elastic gussets around 1837 and presented them to Queen Victoria. They are the ancestor of the Chelsea boot.
- **Button boots:** Tall boots fastened with many small buttons using a button hook. These were extremely common in the late 1800s.
- **Side-lacing boots:** Ankle boots that laced along the inner side for a smooth front appearance.
- **Satin and kid leather slippers:** Flat delicate shoes worn indoors and at balls, especially in the early Victorian years.
- **Louis heels:** Curved heels inspired by 18th century French fashion returned in the late 1800s.

**Athletic and Leisure Shoes**

- **Plimsolls (sand shoes):** Canvas uppers with rubber soles used at the beach and for sport. The name likely came from the Plimsoll line on ships because the sole band resembled it.
- **Croquet sandals (1860s):** Canvas and rubber shoes worn for lawn games.
- **Spiked running shoes (1890s):** Joseph William Foster made spiked shoes for runners. His company later became Reebok.

**Who Wore What**

- **Working classes:** Hobnailed boots, clogs in northern English mill towns and cheap factory-made shoes.
- **Middle classes:** Factory-made Oxfords, Balmorals, button boots and elastic-sided boots.
- **Upper classes:** Custom bespoke shoes, fine kid leather, satin evening slippers and patent leather dress shoes.
