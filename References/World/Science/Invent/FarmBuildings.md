A complete reference to the buildings found on a working farm, what each one does and how each one is built. Covers traditional methods (timber frame, stone, log) and modern methods (post-frame, steel, concrete).

---

## Part 1: Farmstead Planning

### Layout Principles

- **Prevailing wind:** Place the house upwind of livestock barns, manure storage and the slaughter shed. Odor, dust and flies travel downwind.
- **Drainage:** Build on the highest practical ground. Water should flow away from every foundation at a minimum slope of 5% for the first 10 ft.
- **Water protection:** Keep wells at least 100 ft from manure storage, privies and septic fields. Keep wells uphill from these where possible.
- **Fire separation:** Space hay barns at least 75-100 ft from the house and other major buildings. Hay fires are common and spread fast.
- **Traffic flow:** Lay out a service yard where trucks, tractors and trailers can turn without backing. A loaded semi needs a turning circle of roughly 100-120 ft diameter. Farm wagons and tractors need 50-60 ft.
- **Sun orientation:** Run open-front sheds and greenhouses with the open or glazed face to the south (northern hemisphere). Long barns usually run east-west so the broad side catches winter sun.
- **Future growth:** Leave room on at least one end of every barn for an extension.
- **Work triangle:** Keep the house, the main barn and the shop close enough to walk between in a few minutes. Daily chores repeat hundreds of times a year.

### Typical Zones

1. **Residential zone:** Farmhouse, summer kitchen, garden, root cellar, privy, woodshed.
2. **Service zone:** Shop, machine shed, fuel storage, well house.
3. **Crop storage zone:** Granary, corn crib, grain bins, silos, hay barn.
4. **Livestock zone:** Barns, coops, sties, folds, loafing sheds, corrals.
5. **Waste zone:** Manure storage, compost, dead animal disposal. Farthest downwind and downhill.

## Part 2: General Construction Methods

### Foundations

| Type | Use | Construction |
|---|---|---|
| **Pier** | Corn cribs, granaries, small sheds | Concrete, stone or treated wood piers set below frost line. Building sits on beams across the piers. |
| **Embedded post** | Pole barns, sheds | Posts set 4-5 ft into the ground on concrete pads or collars. |
| **Spread footing + stem wall** | Barns, houses, shops | Wide concrete footing below frost line with a concrete or block wall rising above grade. |
| **Slab-on-grade** | Shops, poultry houses, bins | 4-6 in reinforced concrete slab on compacted gravel, thickened at the edges. |
| **Rubble stone** | Traditional barns and houses | Dry-laid or mortared fieldstone in a trench below frost line. |

**Frost line:** Footings must sit below the depth the ground freezes in winter or frost heave will crack and lift them. Depth ranges from 12 in in mild climates to 48-60 in or more in northern regions. Local building offices publish the figure.

**Basic footing procedure:**
1. Stake the corners and check square by measuring diagonals (they must be equal).
2. Set batter boards 3-4 ft outside the corners and run string lines.
3. Excavate to undisturbed soil below frost line.
4. Compact the trench bottom and add 4-6 in of crushed gravel.
5. Build forms, set rebar (typically two runs of #4 bar on chairs) and pour.
6. Embed anchor bolts or straps in the stem wall at 4-6 ft spacing for the sill plate.

### Framing Systems

**Timber frame (traditional):** Heavy timbers (8x8 to 12x12 in) joined with mortise and tenon joints locked by wooden pegs called trunnels. The frame is assembled flat on the ground in cross-sections called bents. Each bent is raised upright with pike poles, ropes or a gin pole, then the bents are connected with girts, plates and braces. Strong, long-lasting (barns stand 200+ years) and open inside. Labor-intensive.

**Post-frame (pole barn):** The modern standard for farm buildings. Treated posts (6x6 or three-ply laminated 2x6 or 2x8) set in the ground every 8-12 ft carry the roof trusses. Horizontal girts (2x4 on the flat, 24 in on center) hold the wall steel. Horizontal purlins (2x4 on edge, 24 in on center) carry the roof steel. Cheap, fast and capable of very wide clear spans (up to 80 ft or more with engineered trusses).

**Stick frame (light frame):** Standard 2x4 or 2x6 studs at 16 in on center on a sill plate over a foundation. Used for houses, small shops and coops.

**Masonry:** Stone, brick or concrete block. Fireproof, rodent-proof and thermally stable. Used for springhouses, smokehouses, root cellars, milk houses and old bank barn foundations.

**Steel frame:** Rigid steel I-beam frames bolted to a concrete foundation. Clear spans over 100 ft. Used for large equipment buildings and riding arenas.

**Log:** Notched logs stacked horizontally (saddle, V, dovetail or square notches). Common in frontier and forest regions for cabins, cribs and small barns.

### Roofing

- **Pitch:** Expressed as rise over 12 in of run. Steel roofs run 3/12 to 4/12 minimum. Shingle roofs need 4/12 or steeper. Traditional barns run 6/12 to 12/12 to shed snow.
- **Gambrel roof:** Two slopes on each side (steep lower, shallow upper). Maximizes loft volume for hay. The classic "red barn" shape.
- **Gable roof:** Simple two-slope roof. Easiest to build.
- **Shed (lean-to) roof:** Single slope. Used for additions and open-front sheds.
- **Monitor roof:** A raised center section with its own short walls for light and ventilation. Common on horse barns and dairy barns.
- **Materials:** 29 or 26 gauge ribbed steel panels (most common today), cedar shakes, slate, asphalt shingles, thatch (historic).
- **Overhangs:** 12-24 in overhangs keep rain off walls and foundations.
- **Ridge vent:** A continuous opening at the peak, covered by a vent cap, lets hot moist air out. Essential in any livestock or hay building.

### Siding

- Steel panels screwed to girts.
- Board and batten: vertical boards with narrow strips covering the gaps.
- Lap siding: overlapping horizontal boards.
- Vertical board siding with gaps: used on hay barns and corn cribs for airflow.
- Stone or brick.

### Ventilation

Livestock produce enormous amounts of moisture and ammonia. Poor ventilation causes respiratory disease, rot and condensation. Basic rules:
- Air enters low along the eaves (soffit vents or sidewall openings).
- Air exits high at the ridge.
- Cold-climate barns need roughly 1 sq in of ridge opening per sq ft of floor space as a starting point. Each 10 ft of building width wants about 2-3 in of continuous ridge opening.
- Modern barns use curtain sidewalls (rolled fabric panels that open in summer) and exhaust fans.

## Part 3: The Buildings

### 1. Farmhouse

**Purpose:** Living quarters for the farm family. Also serves as the farm office, food preservation kitchen and the point from which the whole farmstead is watched.

**Siting:** Upwind and uphill of livestock. Front faces the road. Kitchen windows ideally face the barnyard so activity can be watched during meals.

**Typical features:**
- Mudroom at the back door with a utility sink, boot storage and coat hooks. Workers come in dirty.
- Large kitchen for canning, butchering prep and feeding hired hands.
- Cellar for food storage and the furnace.
- Wide porch for shade and outdoor work.

**Construction (stick-frame):**
1. Pour footings and a foundation wall (basement, crawlspace or slab).
2. Bolt treated 2x6 sill plates to the foundation over a foam sill gasket.
3. Frame the floor: rim joists, then floor joists (2x10 or 2x12) at 16 in on center. Sheath with 3/4 in tongue-and-groove plywood glued and screwed.
4. Frame walls flat on the deck: bottom plate, studs at 16 in on center, double top plate. Frame headers over every window and door. Raise each wall, plumb it and brace it.
5. Set ceiling joists or roof trusses at 24 in on center.
6. Sheath walls with 7/16 in OSB or plywood. Sheath the roof.
7. Wrap the walls in house wrap. Install windows and doors with flashing tape.
8. Install roofing underlayment, drip edge and roofing.
9. Rough in plumbing, wiring and HVAC.
10. Insulate (R-13 to R-21 walls, R-38 to R-60 attic depending on climate).
11. Drywall, trim, flooring, cabinets and fixtures.

### 2. General-Purpose Barn

**Purpose:** The center of the farm. Houses livestock on the ground floor and stores hay and straw in the loft above. Also holds feed, tack, tools and sometimes the threshing floor.

**Traditional types:**
- **English barn:** Three bays. Center bay is a threshing floor with large doors on both long sides so wagons can drive through. Side bays hold grain sheaves and livestock.
- **Dutch barn:** Broad gable roof, doors on the gable ends, central nave for threshing with side aisles for animals.
- **Bank barn (Pennsylvania barn):** Built into a hillside. The upper level opens at grade on the uphill side so wagons drive straight in to unload hay and grain. The lower level opens on the downhill side for livestock. Often has a forebay: the upper floor cantilevers 6-10 ft past the lower wall on the downhill side to shelter the stable doors.
- **Round or polygonal barn:** Livestock arranged in a circle around a central silo or feed area. Efficient feeding. Harder to build.

**Typical dimensions:** 30-60 ft wide, 40-120 ft long. Sidewall height 12-16 ft. Loft floor 8-10 ft above the ground floor.

**Construction (traditional bank barn):**
1. Choose a south-facing slope with a grade of roughly one story over the barn's width.
2. Excavate into the hill to create a level lower floor.
3. Build the uphill wall and the two end walls of mortared stone, 18-24 in thick, as retaining walls. Backfill with gravel and install a drain at the base of the uphill wall.
4. Leave the downhill side open for stable doors or build it of stone with door openings.
5. Lay heavy sill timbers on top of the stone walls.
6. Cut and assemble timber-frame bents on the ground. Each bent contains two or more posts, a tie beam and diagonal braces.
7. Raise the bents one by one and set them on the sills. Connect them with plates along the top and girts along the sides.
8. Install the forebay beams cantilevered over the downhill wall.
9. Lay the threshing floor of 2-3 in thick plank over heavy joists.
10. Set rafters (or common purlin roof framing) and sheath with boards.
11. Roof with shakes, slate or steel.
12. Side the upper level with vertical boards. Leave narrow gaps or install louvered vents for hay ventilation.
13. Build the earth ramp (the "bank") up to the upper doors. Retain its sides with stone. Some builders bridge the last few feet to keep water away from the wall.
14. Hang large sliding doors on overhead track at the upper level.
15. Fit out the lower level with stalls, pens and feed alleys.

**Construction (modern post-frame barn):**
1. Mark post locations at 8 ft on center (or as engineered).
2. Auger holes 18-24 in diameter to 4-5 ft deep or below frost line.
3. Pour or set a concrete footing pad (precast or 8 in thick poured) in each hole.
4. Set treated posts, plumb them with braces and pour concrete collars or backfill with compacted gravel.
5. Snap a level line across all posts at the truss height. Cut posts to that line.
6. Install the treated splash board (skirt board) at the base of the posts. It sits partly in the ground and should be rated for ground contact.
7. Fasten truss carrier beams (doubled 2x10 or 2x12) to the post tops if trusses fall between posts.
8. Lift trusses with a crane, telehandler or gin pole. Set and nail them at 8 ft spacing (or 4 ft for heavy snow loads).
9. Install permanent truss bracing per the truss engineering sheet: lateral braces and diagonal X-braces.
10. Fasten purlins across the truss top chords at 24 in on center.
11. Fasten wall girts across the posts at 24 in on center.
12. Screw down steel roofing starting from the end away from the prevailing wind so laps shed wind-driven rain. Use gasketed screws in the flat of the panel at each purlin.
13. Install ridge cap with foam closures.
14. Install wall steel, corner trim and base trim.
15. Frame door openings with headers. Hang sliding doors on track or install overhead doors.
16. Pour concrete floors in work areas and alleys. Leave dirt or gravel in stall areas if preferred.

### 3. Dairy Barn and Milking Parlor

**Purpose:** Houses milk cows, provides a clean place to milk and stores milk under sanitary cold conditions.

**Types:**
- **Tie-stall barn:** Cows stand in individual stalls, tied with a neck chain. Milked in place with portable units or a pipeline. Common on small farms.
- **Free-stall barn:** Cows move freely between feed lanes and rows of individual bedded resting stalls. They walk to a separate milking parlor.
- **Milking parlor:** A dedicated room where cows enter in groups and the milker works from a pit 30-36 in below cow level. Layouts include herringbone, parallel and rotary.
- **Milk house:** Attached room holding the bulk milk cooling tank, wash vats and pipeline equipment. Kept separate from the animal area by a door for sanitation.

**Key dimensions (Holstein):**
- Tie stall: 4-5 ft wide x 5.5-6 ft long.
- Free stall: 45-48 in wide x 8-9 ft long (head-to-head rows), 9-10 ft (facing a wall).
- Feed alley: 12-16 ft wide (wide enough for a feed mixer wagon).
- Manure alley: 10-14 ft wide.
- Feed bunk space: 24-30 in per cow.

**Construction:**
1. Pour a reinforced concrete floor throughout, minimum 5 in thick. Groove the surface in alleys (diamond or linear grooves 1/2 in deep) so cows don't slip.
2. Slope floors toward gutters or scrape alleys at 1-2%.
3. In tie-stall barns, form a gutter behind the stalls, 16-24 in wide and 12-16 in deep, for manure. Install a gutter cleaner chain or form it for hand cleaning.
4. Build stall platforms raised 6-8 in above the alley. Install curbs at the front.
5. Set stall dividers (steel pipe loops) on embedded anchors.
6. Install stall mats or mattresses on the platforms. Bed with sand, sawdust or straw.
7. Build the parlor pit: excavate, form and pour walls and floor with a center drain. Line walls with tile or fiberglass panel for washing.
8. Install the milk pipeline sloped continuously toward the receiver jar (1-1.5 in per 10 ft).
9. Build the milk house on a sloped concrete floor with a drain. Line walls and ceiling with washable panel. Provide hot water, a two-compartment wash vat and the bulk tank on a pad.
10. Ventilate heavily: curtain sidewalls, open ridge, and circulation fans over stalls for heat stress.

### 4. Horse Barn (Stable)

**Purpose:** Shelters horses, stores tack and feed and provides a grooming and care area.

**Layout:** Most horse barns use a center aisle with stalls on both sides. Tack room, feed room and wash stall sit at one end.

**Key dimensions:**
- Box stall: 12x12 ft standard, 12x14 or 14x14 ft for large breeds, 14x16 ft or larger for foaling.
- Aisle: 10-14 ft wide.
- Ceiling: minimum 10 ft, 12 ft preferred. A horse rearing in a stall needs the clearance.
- Stall doors: 4 ft wide x 8 ft tall minimum.
- Wash stall: 10x12 ft with a floor drain.

**Construction:**
1. Build a post-frame or stick-frame shell with a monitor or high gable roof for light and airflow.
2. Excavate stall areas 12 in deep. Fill with 8 in of compacted crushed stone then 4 in of stone dust or clay for drainage. Top with rubber stall mats (3/4 in thick).
3. Pour concrete or lay paving brick in the aisle. Broom-finish concrete for grip.
4. Build stall walls 8 ft high. The lower 4-5 ft is solid 2 in hardwood or treated tongue-and-groove planking (horses kick). The upper 3-4 ft is steel bars or grille at no more than 3 in spacing so hooves can't pass through.
5. Hang sliding stall doors on track (safer than swinging doors in an aisle). Install a yoke or grille upper section so horses can look out.
6. Install corner feeders and water buckets or automatic waterers.
7. Insulate and seal the tack room against dust, mice and humidity. Install saddle racks and bridle hooks.
8. Build the feed room with rodent-proof bins and a latch a horse cannot open.
9. Wire all lighting with fixtures in protective cages and run wire in conduit (horses chew wire and rodents chew insulation).
10. Install a wash stall with hot and cold water and a sloped floor to a trapped drain.

### 5. Hay Barn / Hay Shed

**Purpose:** Keeps baled hay and straw dry. Rained-on hay loses feed value and molds.

**Types:**
- Fully enclosed barn.
- Open-sided pole shed: roof on posts with no walls or partial walls. Cheapest. Good airflow.
- Loft over a livestock barn.

**Sizing:**
- Small square bales: about 200-250 cu ft per ton.
- Large round bales (5x6 ft): about 1 ton each depending on moisture and density. Plan floor space by bale footprint.
- Large square bales: about 0.8-1 ton each.

**Critical fact:** Hay stored above roughly 20% moisture heats as microbes work. Above 150°F it can spontaneously combust days or weeks after baling. Hay barns need airflow and should stand apart from other buildings.

**Construction:**
1. Build a post-frame structure with wide post spacing (12-16 ft) for loader access.
2. Use a sidewall height of 16-20 ft to allow stacking.
3. Pour a concrete floor or lay 6 in of crushed rock over geotextile fabric to keep bottom bales off wet ground. Use pallets under bales on dirt floors.
4. Keep the windward side and ends closed. Leave the leeward side open or with large doors.
5. Install a ridge vent and gable vents.
6. Extend roof overhangs 3-4 ft on open sides to keep blowing rain off.
7. Include a probe access point so stack temperature can be checked with a hay thermometer.

### 6. Granary

**Purpose:** Stores threshed small grain (wheat, oats, barley, rye) dry and safe from rodents, birds and insects.

**Construction (traditional wooden granary):**
1. Set the building on stone or concrete piers 18-24 in high so air moves underneath and rats can't nest beneath.
2. Cap each pier with a flat metal plate or an inverted metal pan larger than the pier. Rats cannot climb around it.
3. Frame a heavy floor with joists at 12 in on center. Grain is extremely heavy (wheat weighs about 48 lb per cu ft).
4. Lay tight tongue-and-groove flooring.
5. Frame the walls with studs at 12-16 in on center. Sheath the inside with tight tongue-and-groove boards to hold grain. Grain pushes outward hard, so bins must be tied across with tension rods or heavy cross-ties.
6. Divide the interior into bins with removable boards slotted into grooved posts so bins can be emptied from the bottom.
7. Install a door with a removable board stack (stacked boards in slots) behind the main door so grain doesn't spill out when the door opens.
8. Roof tightly with generous overhangs.
9. Screen every vent with 1/4 in hardware cloth.

### 7. Corn Crib

**Purpose:** Stores ear corn (corn still on the cob) and lets it air-dry. Ear corn harvested at high moisture dries naturally in a crib over winter.

**Key design rule:** Width determines whether the corn dries or rots. Air must pass all the way through.
- Dry climates: up to 8 ft wide.
- Moderate climates: 6 ft wide.
- Humid climates: 4-5 ft wide.

**Construction:**
1. Set on piers with rat guards (same as a granary).
2. Frame a sloped floor or a floor with slatted sections.
3. Build the walls of horizontal slats 1-2 in apart or vertical slats with gaps, or use 1/2 in hardware cloth stretched over framing. The walls often slope outward toward the top so rain runs off.
4. Build double cribs with a drive-through alley between them. Wagons pull through and corn is scooped or elevated into the cribs on each side. The space above the alley often holds a small granary.
5. Install the roof with a wide overhang and a loading hatch at the peak for an elevator spout.

### 8. Grain Bins

**Purpose:** Modern bulk storage for shelled corn, soybeans, wheat and other grain. Equipped with aeration fans to control moisture and temperature.

**Typical sizes:** 18-60 ft diameter or more. A 30 ft diameter bin with 8 rings holds roughly 20,000 bushels.

**Construction:**
1. Excavate and compact the site. Remove all organic topsoil.
2. Form a ring footing at the perimeter, typically 24 in wide and 30-48 in deep, plus a reinforced slab 6-8 in thick. Large bins require an engineered foundation because loads are enormous.
3. Set anchor bolts in the footing ring per the bin manufacturer's pattern.
4. Pour the slab with a sump in the center for the unload auger and a trench for the unload tube.
5. Install the aeration floor: perforated steel planks on supports 12-24 in above the slab. Air from the fan flows under the floor and up through the grain.
6. Build the bin from the top down using bin jacks: assemble the roof and top ring on the ground, jack it up, bolt the next ring underneath and repeat. Each corrugated sheet is bolted with sealing washers.
7. Install the stiffeners (vertical channels on the outside) on large bins.
8. Bolt the bottom ring to the anchor bolts and caulk the base.
9. Install the aeration fan and transition, roof vents, the center fill spout and the unload auger.
10. Install the outside ladder with safety cage, roof ladder and inside ladder.

**Safety fact:** Flowing grain can engulf a person in seconds. Bins have lockout points on augers and the access door.

### 9. Silos

**Purpose:** Store silage: chopped green corn, grass or legumes packed airtight so it ferments and preserves. Silage feeds dairy and beef cattle through winter.

**Types:**
- **Tower (upright) silo:** Concrete stave, poured concrete, brick, tile or glass-lined steel (the blue Harvestore type). Height usually 2.5-3.5 times diameter. Common sizes 16-30 ft diameter and 50-80 ft tall.
- **Bunker silo:** Concrete walls on three sides with a concrete floor. Silage is driven in, packed with a tractor and covered with plastic weighted with tires.
- **Silage pile (drive-over pile):** Like a bunker with no walls.
- **Silage bag:** Long plastic tubes filled with a bagging machine.

**Construction (concrete stave tower silo):**
1. Excavate to firm soil and pour a heavy reinforced ring footing and floor. Footing loads are very high.
2. Stack interlocking precast concrete staves (about 10 in wide x 30 in tall x 2.5-3 in thick) in a staggered brick pattern.
3. Wrap steel hoops around the outside every course or two. Tighten the hoops with lugs. Hoops are spaced closer near the bottom where pressure is highest.
4. Leave a continuous column of door openings up one side. Fit small removable doors in each.
5. Build the chute enclosure over the doors.
6. Coat the interior with a cement plaster wash to seal it.
7. Install a dome roof and an unloader that sits on top of the silage and throws feed out the doors.

**Construction (bunker silo):**
1. Pour a 6-8 in reinforced concrete floor sloped 1-2% toward the open end for drainage.
2. Build reinforced concrete walls 8-12 ft high, often with a slight outward lean (batter) so packing tractors don't catch the wall.
3. Set precast concrete panels as an alternative to poured walls.
4. Collect runoff (silage leachate) in a drain and holding tank. Leachate is highly polluting.
5. Width should be at least twice the width of the packing tractor so it can pack properly.

### 10. Machine Shed (Equipment Storage)

**Purpose:** Protects tractors, combines, planters and implements from weather, extending their life and reducing repair costs.

**Key dimensions:**
- Door height: 14-16 ft for tractors with cabs, 18-20 ft for combines and sprayers.
- Door width: 24-40 ft or more. Large sliding or hydraulic bifold doors.
- Clear span: 40-80 ft so equipment can be parked without posts in the way.

**Construction:**
1. Build a post-frame or steel-frame building.
2. Engineer the header over the large door. Hydraulic doors and bifold doors put heavy point loads on the end posts.
3. Lay a gravel or concrete floor. Concrete allows clean sweeping and easier maintenance work.
4. Run ventilation at the ridge and eaves to prevent condensation (moisture drips on equipment and rusts it). Install a condensation blanket under roof steel.
5. Set a gravel or concrete apron outside each door.
6. Install bird netting or close the eaves with closure strips. Birds roosting on equipment are a constant problem.

### 11. Farm Shop (Workshop)

**Purpose:** Repair and fabrication: welding, engine work, tire changes, building gates and feeders. On a traditional farm this was the blacksmith shop.

**Features:**
- Heated and insulated for winter repairs.
- Concrete floor, 6 in thick and reinforced, to carry jacks and heavy equipment.
- Overhead door large enough to bring in the largest machine.
- 240V service for a welder, air compressor and heater.
- Overhead hoist or chain fall on a beam or trolley.
- Workbenches, vise, drill press and tool storage.
- Floor drain with a sediment trap and oil separator.

**Construction:**
1. Pour a thickened-edge slab with in-floor heat tubing (PEX) if radiant heating is wanted. Lay 2 in rigid foam under the slab first.
2. Frame with post-frame or stick-frame walls.
3. Insulate walls (R-19 to R-21) and ceiling (R-38 or more). Install a vapor barrier on the warm side.
4. Line the walls with steel liner panel or plywood to resist damage and allow tool mounting.
5. Install bright LED lighting (at least 50 foot-candles at the bench).
6. Wire outlets every 4-6 ft along benches. Place a 240V outlet near the door for welding outside.
7. Install a heavy beam rated for the hoist.
8. Separate the welding area from flammable storage.

**Traditional blacksmith shop:** Stone or brick walls for fire resistance. Dirt or stone floor. A forge with a brick or stone hearth and chimney, a bellows or blower, an anvil set on a heavy stump at knuckle height and a slack tub of water for quenching.

### 12. Chicken Coop (Poultry House)

**Purpose:** Shelters laying hens or meat birds, provides nesting boxes for eggs and roosts for sleeping and protects birds from predators.

**Key dimensions:**
- Interior floor: 3-4 sq ft per standard bird. 2 sq ft per bantam.
- Outdoor run: 8-10 sq ft per bird.
- Roost bar: 8-10 in of length per bird. Set 2-4 ft high and 12-18 in from the wall. Use 2x4 lumber with the wide side up so birds can cover their toes in cold weather.
- Nest boxes: 1 per 4-5 hens. 12x12x12 in each. Mount 18-24 in above the floor and lower than the roosts (birds sleep in the highest spot available).
- Pop door: 12 in wide x 14 in tall.
- Ventilation: about 1 sq ft of vent opening per 10 birds, placed high above the roosts.

**Construction:**
1. Set the coop on concrete blocks or a slab, or build it on skids so it can be moved.
2. Frame the floor with treated 2x6 joists. Sheath with 3/4 in plywood. Cover with vinyl or sealed paint for easy cleaning.
3. Frame walls with 2x4 studs. Sheath with plywood or OSB and side with weatherproof siding.
4. Frame a shed or gable roof. Leave the area under the eaves open and covered with hardware cloth for ventilation.
5. Cover every opening (windows, vents, run) with 1/2 in galvanized hardware cloth fastened with screws and washers. Chicken wire keeps chickens in but does not keep raccoons, foxes or weasels out.
6. Bury a hardware cloth apron 12 in deep or lay it flat 18-24 in outward from the run perimeter to stop digging predators.
7. Install the roost bars and a droppings board beneath them for easy cleanout.
8. Build the nest boxes as a row on the wall. Hinge an exterior lid for egg collection from outside.
9. Install a large door for cleaning. Install the pop door with a latching sliding cover or an automatic door.
10. Bed with pine shavings 4-6 in deep or use the deep litter method.
11. Install a waterer and feeder off the floor at back height of the birds.

**Commercial poultry houses:** Long single-story buildings (40-60 ft wide x 400-600 ft long) with tunnel ventilation fans at one end, evaporative cooling pads at the other, automatic feed and water lines and insulated walls and ceilings.

### 13. Hog Barn (Pig House / Sty)

**Purpose:** Shelters pigs for breeding, farrowing (giving birth), nursery growth and finishing to market weight.

**Space needs:**
- Nursery pig (to 50 lb): 3-4 sq ft each.
- Finishing pig (to 280 lb): 7-8 sq ft each on slats.
- Sow in gestation stall: 2 ft wide x 7 ft long.
- Farrowing crate and pen: 5x7 ft. The crate keeps the sow from lying on her piglets. A heated creep area at the side keeps piglets warm.

**Traditional sty:** A small shed with a muddy outdoor yard. Pigs can't sweat and need shade and wallows in summer.

**Construction (small farm hog house):**
1. Build a three-sided or enclosed shed with the opening facing south or southeast.
2. Pour a concrete floor sloped 1/2 in per ft toward an outside gutter for cleaning.
3. Build pen walls of 2 in plank, concrete block or steel panels at least 36-42 in high. Pigs root and push, so all fasteners and boards must be stout.
4. Install a farrowing guard rail along the pen walls 8-10 in off the floor and 8-10 in from the wall so piglets can escape being crushed.
5. Provide a heat lamp or heat mat in the creep area.
6. Install nipple waterers at shoulder height for the size of pig.

**Construction (confinement barn):**
1. Excavate for a deep manure pit beneath the building (6-8 ft deep).
2. Pour reinforced concrete pit walls and floor. Waterproof and engineer them for liquid and soil pressure.
3. Set precast concrete slats across the pit. Gaps of 3/4 to 1 in let manure fall through.
4. Frame the superstructure above with insulated walls and ceiling.
5. Install curtain sidewalls or a mechanical ventilation system with fans and inlets.
6. Install feeders, waterers and pen dividers.

---

### 14. Sheep Barn, Lambing Shed and Shearing Shed

**Purpose:** Shelters sheep in bad weather, provides a place for lambing and gives a dry covered space for shearing.

**Space needs:**
- Ewe: 12-16 sq ft.
- Ewe with lambs: 16-20 sq ft.
- Lambing jug (private pen for a ewe and new lambs for 1-3 days): 5x5 ft.
- Feeder space: 16-20 in per ewe.

**Construction:**
1. Build a post-frame shed with one side open to the south or east.
2. Use a dirt or gravel floor with deep straw bedding. Sheep do well on packed bedding.
3. Build portable panels (hurdles) of wood or steel, 4-5 ft long and 36-42 in high, to make lambing jugs. Pin them together with rods.
4. Install hay racks with a keyhole design or slanted bars so sheep don't pull hay onto their fleece.
5. For a shearing shed: build a raised slatted wooden floor so wool stays clean, a catching pen, a shearing board (smooth wooden platform) and a wool table with slatted top for skirting fleeces. Provide overhead power for clippers.
6. Insulate one corner or provide heat lamps for weak lambs in cold climates.

---

### 15. Loafing Shed (Run-In Shed)

**Purpose:** An open-front shelter in a pasture or lot where cattle or horses can escape wind, rain and summer sun.

**Space needs:**
- Horse: 100-150 sq ft per horse.
- Beef cow: 25-40 sq ft.
- Dairy heifer: 25-30 sq ft.

**Construction:**
1. Choose high, well-drained ground. Face the open side away from prevailing winter winds (usually south or east).
2. Build a post-frame or skid-mounted shed with a single-slope shed roof. Front height 10-12 ft, back height 8 ft. The roof drains to the back.
3. Line the inside walls with 2 in plank or plywood to 4-5 ft high as a kick wall.
4. Add a gutter on the back edge and direct water away.
5. Lay compacted crushed rock inside and in a 10-12 ft apron outside the opening to control mud.
6. Depth of at least 12 ft (front to back) keeps rain out.

---

### 16. Springhouse

**Purpose:** Before refrigeration, a springhouse sat over a spring or stream so the cold running water (around 50-55°F year-round) kept milk, butter, cheese and eggs cool.

**Construction:**
1. Locate the spring and dig down to where the water emerges cleanly.
2. Build a stone or concrete trough or channel through the floor. Water enters on the uphill side, flows through the channel and exits on the downhill side.
3. Build thick stone walls (18-24 in) to keep the interior cool. Set the floor partially below grade.
4. Crocks and cans of milk stand in the water channel.
5. Build shelves along the walls for butter and cheese.
6. Roof with a deep overhang. Plant shade trees around it.
7. Install small screened vents high on the walls.

---

### 17. Milk House / Dairy (Traditional)

**Purpose:** Separates cream, churns butter and makes cheese. Separate from the barn for cleanliness.

**Construction:** Same masonry approach as a springhouse but without running water through it if no spring is available. Thick walls, north-facing or shaded location, washable floor sloped to a drain, shelves and a large work table.

---

### 18. Smokehouse

**Purpose:** Preserves meat (hams, bacon, sausage, fish) by curing it with salt and then drying and flavoring it in smoke.

**Types:**
- **Cold smoking:** Below 90°F. Preserves and flavors without cooking. Takes days to weeks. Requires a separate firebox.
- **Hot smoking:** 150-225°F. Cooks and flavors.

**Dimensions:** Typical farm smokehouse is 6x6 to 8x10 ft and 7-10 ft tall.

**Construction:**
1. Build on a concrete or stone foundation. Use a dirt or brick floor (some designs keep the fire pit in the center of a dirt floor).
2. Build walls of brick, stone, block or tight wood (vertical boards with battens). Wood smokehouses must be well away from other buildings.
3. For cold smoking, build the firebox 10-20 ft away and 2-4 ft lower than the smokehouse. Connect it with a buried flue pipe or a covered clay tile trench. Smoke cools as it travels uphill.
4. Install crossbeams or rods inside near the top for hanging meat on hooks. Hang meat at least 4-6 ft above the fire in direct-fire designs.
5. Install a small adjustable vent at the top (a gable vent or roof jack) to draw smoke through.
6. Fit a tight door. Use a lock: smoked hams were valuable and often stolen.
7. Screen all openings against flies.

---

### 19. Root Cellar

**Purpose:** Stores potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, cabbage, apples and other produce through winter using the stable cold and humidity of the earth.

**Target conditions:** 32-40°F and 85-95% humidity for most root vegetables.

**Construction (hillside type):**
1. Choose a north-facing slope in well-drained soil.
2. Dig into the hill to create a room about 8x10 ft and 7 ft high.
3. Pour a concrete or stone footing. Build walls of stone, block or poured concrete.
4. Build a roof of reinforced concrete or a stone or block arch. Waterproof the exterior.
5. Cover the roof with 2-3 ft of earth. Plant grass to hold it.
6. Install a drain line from the floor (or use a gravel floor that stays moist).
7. Install two vent pipes (4-6 in diameter): an intake that opens low near the floor and an exhaust that opens near the ceiling and extends up above the earth cover. Cold air enters low and warm moist air leaves high. Cap both outside with screened covers.
8. Build an entry with two doors and an airlock vestibule between them.
9. Build slatted wooden shelves and bins. Keep apples away from potatoes and cabbages (apples release ethylene and cabbage odors transfer).

---

### 20. Ice House

**Purpose:** Before refrigeration, ice cut from ponds and lakes in winter was stored packed in sawdust and lasted into late summer.

**Construction:**
1. Choose a shaded site. Dig a pit 3-6 ft deep or build at grade.
2. Lay a drain of gravel and stone under the floor. Melted water must leave immediately or it melts the ice faster.
3. Build double walls with a 12-18 in gap between them. Fill the gap with sawdust, straw or wood shavings as insulation.
4. Lay a slatted floor over the drainage bed.
5. Roof with a wide overhang and install a ventilated gable or cupola. Heat rising off the stored ice must escape.
6. To fill: cut ice blocks (about 1-2 ft square) from a frozen pond with an ice saw. Haul them by sled. Stack them tightly in the house with no gaps, leaving 12 in of space at the walls. Pack sawdust into every gap and over the top 12 in or more deep.
7. Remove blocks from the top as needed and re-cover with sawdust.

---

### 21. Greenhouse and Hoop House

**Purpose:** Extends the growing season by trapping solar heat. Used for starting seedlings, growing winter greens and protecting tender crops.

**Types:**
- **Glass greenhouse:** Permanent. Metal or wood frame with glass panes.
- **Polycarbonate greenhouse:** Twin-wall plastic panels. Good insulation.
- **Hoop house (high tunnel):** Steel bows covered with greenhouse film. Crops grow in the ground. Cheap and common on market farms.
- **Cold frame:** Low box with a glass or plastic lid laid on the ground.

**Orientation:** Long axis east-west to maximize winter sun. In hot climates or for summer crops, north-south gives more even light.

**Construction (hoop house, 30x96 ft):**
1. Level the site and mark a rectangle. Check square with the diagonals.
2. Drive galvanized steel ground posts 2-3 ft into the ground every 4-6 ft along both sides. Set them precisely in line and at the same height.
3. Assemble the steel bows (1 3/8 or 1 5/8 in galvanized tube) on the ground and slide them over the ground posts. Bolt them.
4. Install a ridge purlin along the peak and side purlins connecting all bows.
5. Install diagonal corner bracing.
6. Frame the end walls with lumber, including doors large enough for a small tractor.
7. Fasten treated baseboards along the bottom on both sides and hip boards along the sides about 4-5 ft up.
8. Install aluminum wiggle-wire channel on the baseboards, hip boards and end bows.
9. Pull greenhouse film (6 mil UV-treated polyethylene) over the structure on a calm day. Secure it in the channels with wiggle wire.
10. For a double layer, install a second sheet and a small inflation blower to keep a cushion of air between them for insulation.
11. Install roll-up sides below the hip boards with a crank so the sides open for ventilation.
12. Install end-wall vents or exhaust fans for summer.

**Construction (cold frame):**
1. Build a rectangular box of rot-resistant boards (cedar or treated lumber) about 3x6 ft.
2. Make the back 18 in tall and the front 12 in tall so the lid slopes toward the sun.
3. Hinge an old window sash or a polycarbonate panel lid on the high side.
4. Set the frame in the ground with the slope facing south. Prop the lid open on warm days.

---

### 22. Well House / Pump House

**Purpose:** Shelters the wellhead, pump, pressure tank and treatment equipment and keeps them from freezing.

**Construction:**
1. Pour a slab around the well casing. The casing must extend at least 12 in above the slab and the slab must slope away from it so surface water can't run down the casing.
2. Frame a small insulated building (6x6 to 8x8 ft).
3. Insulate walls, floor edges and ceiling heavily. Install a small thermostatic heater.
4. Install a removable roof panel or roof hatch directly over the well so the pump and drop pipe can be pulled straight up by a pump truck.
5. Mount the pressure tank, pressure switch, filters and any water softener on the slab.

**Traditional windmill:** A steel or wooden lattice tower over the well with a multi-blade wheel driving a pump rod. Pumps water into a stock tank. Tower must be anchored in concrete footings at each leg.

---

### 23. Outhouse (Privy)

**Purpose:** Toilet before indoor plumbing, still used at remote fields and camps.

**Construction:**
1. Choose a site at least 100 ft downhill from any well and at least 50 ft from the house.
2. Dig a pit 3x3 ft or 4x4 ft and 4-6 ft deep. Stay well above the water table.
3. Line the top 18-24 in of the pit with boards, block or a timber crib so the edge doesn't collapse.
4. Build a floor of treated framing and plywood over the pit, extending well past the pit edges.
5. Frame a small building (about 4x4 ft, 7 ft tall at the front).
6. Build a seat box with one or two holes and hinged lids.
7. Install a vent pipe from the pit up through the roof, painted black so sun heats it and draws odor upward. Screen the top.
8. Hang the door facing away from the house for privacy.
9. Keep a bucket of lime, ash or sawdust to cover waste.
10. When the pit fills to within 18 in of the top, fill it with soil, dig a new pit and move the building.

---

### 24. Summer Kitchen

**Purpose:** A separate small building for cooking, canning, baking and laundry in hot months. Kept the heat, smell and fire risk out of the main house.

**Construction:**
1. Build a small one-room building (12x16 ft or similar) 20-40 ft from the house.
2. Use stone, brick or wood frame construction.
3. Build a large hearth or install a wood cookstove with a masonry chimney.
4. Install many windows on opposite walls for cross-breeze.
5. Lay a stone, brick or concrete floor.
6. Build shelves for canning jars and a large work table.
7. Some include a bake oven: a masonry dome built on a raised base outside or attached to the chimney.

---

### 25. Woodshed

**Purpose:** Stores and seasons firewood. Wood needs to dry for 6-12 months to below 20% moisture before burning well.

**Construction:**
1. Set treated posts on concrete piers or in the ground.
2. Build a shed roof sloping away from the open side.
3. Leave the front open. Side walls should have gaps between boards so air passes through.
4. Build a raised slatted floor or set pallets on gravel. Wood should never sit on bare ground.
5. Face the open side toward the sun and the prevailing breeze (but away from driving rain).
6. Divide into two or three bays: one for this year's wood, one for next year's wood, one for kindling.
7. Size: one full cord (4x4x8 ft = 128 cu ft) per bay as a starting point.

---

### 26. Bunkhouse

**Purpose:** Housing for seasonal workers, hired hands and ranch crews.

**Construction:**
1. Build a single-story stick-frame or post-frame building on a slab or crawlspace.
2. Divide into sleeping rooms, a common room, a washroom and sometimes a kitchen.
3. Insulate and heat it to residential standards.
4. Install bathroom and shower facilities with a septic or sewer connection.
5. Provide a mudroom entry and boot storage.

---

### 27. Slaughter Shed / Butcher House

**Purpose:** On-farm processing of hogs, cattle, sheep and poultry for family use.

**Construction:**
1. Pour a reinforced concrete floor sloped 1/4 in per ft toward a central trapped drain.
2. Build walls of block or frame lined with washable fiberglass panel or stainless sheet.
3. Install a heavy beam or I-beam rail overhead with a hoist and gambrel hook capable of lifting the largest animal (a beef can exceed 1,200 lb live).
4. Provide hot and cold water with a hose bib and a large sink.
5. For hogs, include a scalding vat (heated water at about 145-150°F) for hair removal.
6. Screen all windows and doors.
7. Include or connect to a walk-in cooler for hanging carcasses at 34-38°F.
8. Plan proper disposal for blood, offal and wash water. Keep the shed far from wells.

---

### 28. Manure Storage

**Purpose:** Holds manure until it can be spread on fields at the right time of year. Prevents runoff pollution of streams and wells.

**Types:**
- **Stacking slab:** Concrete pad with walls on three sides for solid manure and bedding.
- **Roofed manure shed:** Stacking slab with a roof to keep rain out.
- **Earthen lagoon or pond:** For liquid manure. Clay-lined or synthetic-lined.
- **Concrete or steel tank:** Above ground or below ground for liquid manure.

**Sizing:** Most systems hold 180 days of production so nothing needs spreading on frozen ground.

**Construction (stacking slab):**
1. Site at least 100 ft from wells and outside floodplains. Downwind of the house.
2. Pour a reinforced concrete floor (6 in minimum) sloped toward the open end or toward a collection drain.
3. Build reinforced concrete or block walls 4-8 ft high on three sides. Push walls must be engineered for loader pressure.
4. Install a curb and drain at the open end leading to a runoff holding area or filter strip.
5. Add a roof to reduce the volume of contaminated water.

---

### 29. Dovecote (Pigeon House)

**Purpose:** Historic. Raised pigeons for meat (squab), eggs and fertilizer. Common on European and colonial estates.

**Construction:**
1. Build a round, square or octagonal tower of stone or brick, or a small house on a tall post.
2. Line the interior walls with rows of nesting holes (about 8x8 in each, 12 in deep) built into the masonry.
3. Install a cupola or small openings near the roof for birds to fly in and out.
4. Many round dovecotes use a potence: a central rotating post with arms and a ladder so the keeper can reach every nest.
5. Keep the door tight against rats and predators.

---

### 30. Apiary and Bee House

**Purpose:** Keeps honeybees for honey, wax and crop pollination.

**Construction:**
1. Choose a site with morning sun, afternoon shade, a windbreak on the north side and a water source within a short distance.
2. Build hive stands 16-18 in high from treated lumber or concrete blocks to keep hives off damp ground and away from skunks.
3. Face hive entrances to the southeast.
4. Space hives 3-4 ft apart, or set them on a long two-rail stand.
5. Build a honey house nearby with a washable floor, screened windows and a door that closes tightly against bees. It holds the extractor, uncapping tank, settling tanks and storage.

---

### 31. Fuel and Chemical Storage

**Purpose:** Safely stores diesel, gasoline, oil, fertilizer and pesticides.

**Construction:**
1. Set fuel tanks on a concrete pad inside a secondary containment berm or use double-walled tanks. Containment should hold at least 110% of the largest tank.
2. Keep fuel tanks at least 40 ft from buildings.
3. Build a small dedicated chemical storage building with a concrete floor and a curb to contain spills. Install ventilation, shelving that keeps containers off the floor and a locked door.
4. Keep fertilizer separate from fuel. Ammonium nitrate and fuel oil together are an explosive mixture.

---

### 32. Corrals, Pens and Handling Facilities

**Purpose:** Hold, sort, load and treat livestock. A cattle working facility saves time and prevents injury.

**Components:**
- **Holding pen:** Collects animals from pasture.
- **Crowding tub (sweep tub):** Circular pen that funnels animals into the chute.
- **Alley (lead-up):** Single-file chute 26-30 in wide for adult cattle with solid sides so animals can't see out.
- **Squeeze chute:** Restrains one animal at a time for vaccinations, tagging and vet work.
- **Loading chute:** Ramp up to trailer or truck height (about 40 in for a pickup trailer, 48-52 in for a semi).

**Construction:**
1. Set posts (treated wood, steel pipe or railroad ties) 3-4 ft in the ground on 8 ft centers. Concrete them in at corners, gates and the crowding tub.
2. Use 2x6 or 2x8 treated planks or steel pipe rails for fences 5-6 ft high for cattle.
3. Use solid sheeting on the crowding tub and alley walls. Cattle move better when they can't see people or distractions outside.
4. Build a curved alley. Cattle move more willingly when they can't see the end.
5. Pour concrete with grooved surfaces in the alley and at the squeeze chute so animals don't slip.
6. Hang gates on heavy hinges with spring-loaded or slam latches.
7. Build the loading ramp with cleats or stair steps (4 in rise, 12 in tread) and solid sides.

## Part 4: Materials and Tools Reference

### Lumber Grades and Treatments

- **Ground contact treated (UC4A/UC4B):** Posts and anything in or touching soil.
- **Above ground treated (UC3B):** Exterior framing not in the ground.
- **Untreated:** Interior framing protected from weather.
- **Laminated posts:** Three or four 2x boards glued or nailed together. Straighter and stronger than solid timber of the same size. Often have treated bottoms and untreated tops.

### Concrete

- Standard strength for farm slabs and footings: 3,500-4,000 psi.
- Manure and silage areas: 4,000-5,000 psi with air entrainment to resist acid and freeze-thaw damage.
- Cure concrete moist for at least 7 days. Full strength develops in 28 days.

### Fasteners

- Ring-shank or hot-dipped galvanized nails for treated lumber.
- Gasketed self-tapping screws for steel panels.
- Structural screws and through-bolts for truss and beam connections.
- Stainless or galvanized hardware in livestock buildings (ammonia corrodes plain steel).

### Essential Tools

- Tractor-mounted post hole auger (12-24 in bits).
- Transit or laser level.
- String line, batter boards, stakes and a long tape.
- Circular saw, chainsaw, reciprocating saw.
- Impact driver and drill.
- Framing nailer and compressor.
- Scaffolding or a telehandler / man lift.
- For timber framing: chisels (1-2 in), slick, boring machine or auger, mallet (beetle), framing square and drawknife.

## Part 5: Typical Construction Sequence for a New Farmstead

1. Survey the property, test soils and locate water.
2. Drill the well and install the well house.
3. Build the access road and service yard with compacted gravel.
4. Bring in electrical service to a central meter pole.
5. Build the farmhouse or living quarters.
6. Build the shop (useful for every project that follows).
7. Build the main barn or livestock housing.
8. Fence pastures and build corrals.
9. Build hay and grain storage.
10. Build the machine shed.
11. Add specialty buildings (coops, smokehouse, greenhouse) as needed.
12. Build manure storage before livestock numbers grow.
