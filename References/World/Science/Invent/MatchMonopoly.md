## Short Answer

A self-igniting match under a real monopoly would be a fortune on the scale of soap, alum or glass rather than salt or spices. Inside one kingdom it could equal a noticeable share of royal income. Across Western Europe, sustained for a few decades, it could build wealth comparable to the great banking houses like the Fuggers.

## Why the Ceiling Exists

Fire was already nearly free. Flint, steel and tinder cost little and lasted years. Households banked the hearth overnight and borrowed coals from neighbors. The product sells **speed and convenience**, not fire itself. That caps per-person spending well below true necessities.

The Song Chinese already had sulfur-tipped pine sticks sold in city markets, yet they made nobody rich because they only caught from an existing ember. The money is entirely in **self-ignition**.

## Who Pays

- **Armies:** Matchlock gunners kept smoldering cord lit for hours. It failed in rain, burned constantly and gave away positions at night. The wheellock (c.1500) was invented to solve this and was very expensive. States buying in bulk would be the single most reliable customer.
- **Churches:** Thousands of candles and lamps lit daily.
- **Towns:** Urban households, servants, inns and workshops where banking a fire is less practical.
- **Travelers and sailors:** Merchants, pilgrims, couriers and ships, where carrying live embers is dangerous or impossible.

## Rough Numbers (England, c.1450)

| Factor | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Population | ~2.5 million |
| Households | ~500,000 |
| Laborer wage | ~4d per day |
| Price | 1d per box of ~20 |
| Buyers | 20% of households, one box weekly |
| Household revenue | ~5.2 million pence, about £21,700/yr |

Military and Church contracts push that higher. Royal ordinary income in this period ran in the tens of thousands of pounds a year, so this business rivals a large slice of the crown's own revenue. Raw materials and labor are cheap, so margins of 50% to 80% are realistic.

Scale that across France, the Empire, Italy and Iberia and gross revenue reaches roughly 15 to 20 times the English figure annually. Jakob Fugger left about 2 million guilders in 1525. A match monopoly held across several kingdoms for a generation lands in that tier.

## Lighter vs. Match

A lighter is a durable good. It sells once, saturates the wealthy market and becomes a status item. A match is a consumable that sells every week forever. The strongest model uses both: the lighter as a luxury piece and the replacement flints, fuel or matches as the controlled consumable.

## Holding the Monopoly

- **Chemical secrecy:** Phosphorus was not isolated until 1669, so the inventor would need a proprietary formula. Secrets like this held for years to centuries historically (Venetian glass, Chinese porcelain until Meissen cracked it in 1710). Alchemists would attack it relentlessly.
- **Legal protection:** Venice passed a patent statute in 1474. Crowns sold letters patent for monopolies so freely that England passed the Statute of Monopolies in 1624 to curb them.
- **Partnering with the state:** Any king will tax or seize something this profitable. The winning move is farming the monopoly to the crown for a share, like the French salt gabelle. Ivar Kreuger did exactly this in the 1920s, lending governments money in exchange for national match monopolies and controlling most of world production.

## Risks

- **Fire:** Timber cities burned regularly. Authorities could ban or tightly license the product.
- **Toxicity:** Historical white phosphorus matches rotted workers' jaws ("phossy jaw"). Labor losses and public horror follow.
- **Witchcraft:** Fire from nothing during the witch-trial era invites accusations. Church endorsement or sales of "blessed" matches neutralizes this and opens the Church as a customer at the same time.
- **Seizure and counterfeits:** Powerful lords take what they want. Rivals sell inferior copies.

## Bottom Line

| Scope | Outcome |
|---|---|
| One city or duchy | Wealthy merchant family |
| One kingdom with crown backing | Fortune rivaling major nobility |
| Multiple kingdoms, decades | Banking-house level dynasty |

The product itself is not the limit. Control of the secret and the political alliance that protects it decide which tier is reached.
