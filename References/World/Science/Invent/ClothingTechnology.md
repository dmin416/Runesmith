# Underwear

**The washable underlayer is the greatest underwear technology in history.** Wool outer garments were hard to clean, so a layer of washable linen next to the skin absorbed sweat and dirt. It protected expensive outer clothes and was the main form of personal hygiene for centuries.

## Major Milestones

- **Loincloths (prehistoric):** Ötzi the Iceman wore a goatskin loincloth about 5,300 years ago.
- **Linen loincloths (ancient Egypt):** Tutankhamun's tomb held around 145 linen loincloths.
- **Roman subligaculum and strophium:** Romans wore wrapped loincloths or shorts along with a breast band. A Roman letter found at Vindolanda in Britain mentions underpants sent to a soldier.
- **Linen braies and chemises (medieval):** Washable linen drawers and shirts became the standard underlayer across Europe.
- **Early bras and briefs (15th century):** Linen garments found at Lengberg Castle in Austria, dated 1440 to 1485, closely resemble modern bras and briefs.
- **Corsets and stays (16th century onward):** Stiffened bodices shaped the torso for over 300 years.
- **Women's drawers (early 1800s):** Drawers for women became common for the first time.
- **Union suit (1868):** A one-piece knit garment covering the whole body. It was patented in Utica, New York.
- **Modern bra (1914):** Mary Phelps Jacob patented a soft bra made from handkerchiefs and ribbon. Cup sizing followed in the 1930s.
- **Elastic waistbands (1920s and 1930s):** Rubber elastic replaced buttons and ties. Jacob Golomb's elastic-waisted boxing trunks led to boxer shorts.
- **Briefs (1935):** Coopers introduced the Jockey brief with a Y-front.
- **Spandex (1958):** Joseph Shivers developed spandex at DuPont. It gave underwear lasting stretch and fit.
- **Moisture-wicking synthetics and merino wool (late 20th century):** These kept skin dry during exertion and cold.

## Underwear by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

- **Braies:** Men wore loose linen drawers tied at the waist with a cord or belt called a braiel. Early braies reached the knee or lower. By the late medieval period they were much shorter and closer to modern shorts.
- **Points to hose:** Separate leg coverings called hose were tied to the braiel or later to the doublet with laces called points.
- **Shirts and chemises:** Both sexes wore a long linen shirt or chemise against the skin. Men's shirts were usually called shirts and women's were called chemises or smocks.
- **Women's lower garments:** Most women wore no drawers. The chemise served as the main underlayer.
- **Breast bands:** Linen bands and fitted bodices supported the bust. The Lengberg finds show cupped bra-like garments existed by the 1400s.
- **Laundering:** Clean white linen signaled wealth and respectability. Many believed changing linen cleaned the body better than bathing.
- **Class differences:** The wealthy wore fine bleached linen. The poor wore coarse linen, hemp or sometimes nothing under their wool garments.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

- **Shirts and smocks:** Still the core underlayer. Collars and cuffs grew decorative and were meant to be seen at the neck and wrists. Blackwork embroidery in black silk was popular.
- **Stays and bodies:** Women's bodices called a pair of bodies were stiffened with reed, whalebone or wood. A rigid busk down the front created a flat cone-shaped torso.
- **Farthingales:** The Spanish farthingale used hoops of cane or whalebone sewn into an underskirt to create a stiff bell shape. The later French wheel farthingale extended skirts straight out from the hips.
- **Drawers for women:** Some Italian noblewomen and courtesans wore linen drawers called calzoni. Most European women still went without.
- **Men's drawers:** Linen drawers continued under trunk hose and breeches.
- **Codpieces:** These began as a gusset covering the gap in joined hose. They grew into a prominent padded fashion item by the mid 1500s.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

- **Women's layers:** A typical outfit included a chemise, drawers, a corset, a corset cover, several petticoats and a crinoline or bustle.
- **Drawers for women:** These became standard by the 1840s. Many were split at the crotch for easier use of the toilet.
- **Corsets:** Metal eyelets introduced in the 1820s allowed tighter lacing. Front-opening split busks let women dress without help. Edwin Izod's steam-molding process from 1868 shaped corsets on heated forms.
- **Cage crinolines (1856):** Lightweight steel hoops replaced heavy layers of petticoats.
- **Bustles (1870s and 1880s):** Frames or pads behind the waist pushed skirts backward.
- **Combinations (1870s):** A single garment joining chemise and drawers.
- **Union suits:** Knit one-piece underwear for men, women and children, especially in cold climates.
- **Wool health underwear (1880s):** Gustav Jaeger promoted wool next to the skin for health. The Jaeger company sold his designs in Britain.
- **Men's underwear:** Long knit drawers and undershirts of cotton, wool or flannel.

## Ranking by Total Impact

1. The washable linen underlayer
2. Elastic waistbands
3. Spandex

# Pants

**Trousers themselves are the greatest pants technology in history.** Horse-riding peoples of Central Asia invented them to protect the legs and allow free movement in the saddle. The oldest surviving pair, found at Yanghai in western China, is about 3,000 years old.

## Major Milestones

- **Woven wool trousers (about 3,000 years ago):** The Yanghai trousers used tailored crotch panels for riding.
- **Steppe and Persian trousers (ancient):** Scythians, Persians and other riders spread trousers across Eurasia.
- **Roman braccae:** Greeks and Romans first viewed trousers as barbarian. Roman soldiers on cold frontiers later adopted them.
- **Germanic trousers (4th century):** The Thorsberg trousers from northern Germany had full legs with attached foot loops.
- **Hose (medieval):** Separate fitted leg coverings dominated European men's wear for centuries.
- **Breeches (16th to 18th centuries):** Knee-length pants worn with stockings.
- **Long trousers (late 1700s to early 1800s):** Long trousers replaced breeches for fashionable men after the French Revolution.
- **Riveted denim (1873):** Levi Strauss and Jacob Davis patented copper rivets on work pants. Jeans became the most worn pants in history.
- **Zipper fly (1930s):** Gideon Sundback's improved zipper from 1913 replaced button flies on many trousers.
- **Belt loops (early 20th century):** Belts replaced suspenders for most men.
- **Cargo pants (1938):** British battle dress added large leg pockets.
- **Permanent press (1950s):** Chemist Ruth Benerito helped develop wrinkle-resistant cotton.
- **Stretch fabrics (late 20th century):** Spandex blends added comfort and flexibility.

## Pants by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

- **Leg wraps:** Early medieval men often wore loose trousers bound with cloth strips wound around the calf.
- **Hose or chausses:** Separate legs of woven wool cut on the bias for some stretch. They often included a foot and were tied to the braies belt with points.
- **Joined hose (15th century):** The two legs were sewn together into a single tight garment like modern tights. A flap or codpiece covered the front opening.
- **Working dress:** Laborers often wore looser hose or rolled them down. Braies were sometimes worn alone during hot field work.
- **Colors:** Wealthy men wore dyed hose in bright colors. Parti-colored hose with each leg a different color was fashionable in the 1300s and 1400s.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

- **Trunk hose:** Short puffed breeches covering the hips and upper thighs. Many were made of panes or strips of fabric with a contrasting lining showing through.
- **Canions:** Fitted tubes of fabric from trunk hose to the knee.
- **Upper stocks and nether stocks:** The upper part became breeches and the lower part became stockings. This split is the origin of separate pants and socks.
- **Codpieces:** Padded and decorated codpieces peaked in the mid 1500s and faded by the 1590s.
- **Venetians:** Knee-length breeches that were full at the hip and narrowed toward the knee. These were popular in the late 1500s.
- **Slops:** Wide loose breeches worn by sailors and some laborers.
- **Peasant wear:** Simple wool or linen breeches and stockings.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

- **Long trousers:** The standard for men of every class.
- **Fall fronts and fly fronts:** Early trousers used a buttoned flap across the front. Fly fronts with hidden buttons became standard by the mid 1800s.
- **Braces:** Suspenders held trousers high on the waist. Belts were uncommon for formal wear.
- **Patterns:** Checks, stripes and plaids were fashionable for day trousers. Formal evening trousers were black with braid along the outer seam.
- **Creases and turn-ups (late 1800s):** Pressed front creases and cuffs appeared late in the era. Edward VII is often credited with popularizing them.
- **Knickerbockers:** Loose knee breeches for sport, shooting, cycling and boys.
- **Workwear:** Corduroy, moleskin, canvas and denim. Levi's riveted work pants appeared in 1873.
- **Women's trousers:** Amelia Bloomer promoted loose Turkish-style trousers in 1851. Cycling bloomers spread in the 1890s.
- **Boys:** Young boys wore dresses until breeching around age four to seven. Short trousers or knickerbockers came next and long trousers marked adulthood.

## Ranking by Total Impact

1. Trousers themselves
2. Riveted denim
3. The zipper fly

# Shirts

**Woven linen fabric is the greatest shirt technology in history.** Linen was strong, washable and cool. The Tarkhan Dress from Egypt, a linen shirt with pleated sleeves, is over 5,000 years old and is the oldest known woven garment.

## Major Milestones

- **Linen tunics (over 5,000 years ago):** The Tarkhan Dress shows tailored sleeves and a fitted bodice.
- **Greek chiton and Roman tunica:** Draped and sewn tunics were the basic upper garment of the classical world.
- **Buttons with buttonholes (13th century):** Functional buttonholes appeared in Germany. They made close-fitting garments possible.
- **Linen shirts as underlayers (medieval):** The shirt became the universal garment next to the skin.
- **Ruffs and starch (16th century):** Starch reached England in the 1560s and allowed huge pleated collars.
- **Lace (16th century):** Needle lace and bobbin lace trimmed collars and cuffs.
- **Cotton gin (1793):** Eli Whitney's machine made cotton fabric cheap and widely available.
- **Detachable collars (1827):** Hannah Montague of Troy, New York created separate collars that could be washed without washing the whole shirt.
- **Full button-front shirts (1871):** Brown, Davis and Company registered the coat-style shirt that opened all the way down the front.
- **T-shirts (around 1913):** The US Navy issued crew-neck cotton undershirts.
- **Polo shirts (1920s):** René Lacoste designed a short-sleeved knit tennis shirt.
- **Wash-and-wear shirts (1950s):** Wrinkle-resistant cotton and polyester blends.
- **Performance fabrics (late 20th century):** Synthetic and merino shirts for sweat and temperature control.

## Shirts by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

- **Linen shirts:** Long pullover shirts worn under all other clothing. They reached the thigh or knee.
- **Tunics or cottes:** Wool tunics worn over the shirt. Length and fullness varied by period and rank.
- **Surcoats:** Sleeveless or loose outer tunics worn over the cotte and sometimes over armor.
- **Cote-hardie (14th century):** A tight-fitting garment with many buttons down the front and along the sleeves. It showed off the new technology of buttons.
- **Necklines:** Shirts were simple with small neck openings and drawstrings.
- **Class differences:** Nobles wore fine linen and silk. Peasants wore coarse linen, hemp or wool.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

- **Visible shirts:** Shirts were deliberately shown through slashed doublets and sleeves.
- **Embroidery:** Collars, cuffs and fronts carried blackwork or colored silk embroidery.
- **Frills to ruffs:** Small frills at the neck grew into the standalone ruff by the 1560s. Some later ruffs were over a foot wide and required wire supports called supportasses.
- **Falling bands:** Flat collars that lay over the shoulders became popular toward the end of the period.
- **Doublets:** Fitted padded jackets worn over the shirt. These acted as the main upper garment for men.
- **Women's smocks:** Long linen smocks with decorated necklines worn under bodices and gowns. Partlets covered the upper chest and neck.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

- **White shirts:** A clean white shirt signaled respectability. The phrase white collar came from this association.
- **Pullover shirts:** Most shirts opened only partway down the front until the coat-style shirt of 1871.
- **Detachable collars and cuffs:** High starched collars and separate cuffs could be changed daily. Celluloid collars from the 1870s could be wiped clean.
- **Studs and cufflinks:** Formal shirts fastened with removable studs.
- **Starched fronts:** Evening shirts had stiff starched bib fronts.
- **Working shirts:** Collarless shirts of flannel, cotton or striped fabric. These are now called grandad shirts.
- **Women's blouses:** Shirtwaists became popular in the 1890s with the Gibson Girl look. They were often paired with a plain skirt for office and shop work.

## Ranking by Total Impact

1. Woven linen fabric
2. The cotton gin
3. Buttons with buttonholes

# Jackets and Coats

**Sewn hide and fur garments are the greatest coat technology in history.** Eyed bone needles from at least 40,000 years ago let people sew fitted hide clothing. That warmth allowed humans to survive ice age Europe and Siberia.

## Major Milestones

- **Sewn fur and hide clothing (at least 40,000 years ago):** Eyed needles made fitted warm garments possible.
- **Ötzi's coat (about 5,300 years ago):** A goat hide coat stitched from strips of different colors. He also had a woven grass cloak.
- **Felt and wool cloaks (ancient):** Felted and woven wool cloaks were common across Eurasia.
- **Roman paenula and sagum:** Hooded travel cloaks and military cloaks.
- **Arctic parkas (ancient Arctic):** Fur parkas kept the wearer warm. Some Arctic peoples made waterproof parkas from seal intestine.
- **Padded gambesons (medieval):** Quilted layers of linen worn as armor or under armor.
- **Fitted tailoring (14th century):** Cut and shaped garments replaced loose tunics and began Western tailoring.
- **Waterproof rubberized cloth (1823):** Charles Macintosh patented fabric bonded with rubber.
- **Gabardine (1879):** Thomas Burberry created a weatherproof tightly woven fabric. It led to the trench coat of World War I.
- **Waxed cotton (late 1800s):** Oiled sailcloth and waxed cotton gave water resistance for sailors and outdoor workers.
- **Leather flight jackets (1930s):** The A-2 jacket kept pilots warm in open cockpits.
- **Quilted down jackets (1936):** Eddie Bauer designed a quilted goose down jacket after nearly dying from hypothermia.
- **Gore-Tex (1976):** A waterproof breathable membrane for rain jackets. Pore vs vapor physics: `../../Materials/PermeationBarriers.md`.
- **Fleece and synthetic insulation (1979 onward):** Polar fleece and Thinsulate gave light warmth that held up when wet.

## Jackets and Coats by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

- **Cloaks and mantles:** The main outer layer for most people. They were fastened with a brooch or clasp at the shoulder or chest.
- **Hooded cloaks:** Common for travelers, peasants and monks.
- **Gambesons:** Padded quilted jackets for soldiers. Some townsmen wore lighter versions as fashion.
- **Pourpoints (14th century):** Fitted padded jackets that buttoned down the front. These were the ancestor of the doublet.
- **Houppelandes (late 14th and 15th centuries):** Long voluminous robes with huge sleeves and high collars. They were often lined with fur.
- **Fur linings:** Squirrel, rabbit, fox, sable and ermine lined cloaks and robes. Sumptuary laws restricted the finest furs to the nobility.
- **Peasant wear:** Coarse wool tunics and cloaks with leather or sheepskin for cold weather.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

- **Doublets:** Fitted padded jackets. Late 1500s doublets had a peascod belly, which was a padded point at the stomach.
- **Jerkins:** Sleeveless or short-sleeved jackets worn over the doublet. Leather jerkins were common.
- **Gowns:** Long loose fur-lined overgowns for older men, scholars and officials. Academic gowns descend from these.
- **Short capes:** Spanish style capes worn over one or both shoulders were fashionable in the late 1500s.
- **Mandilions:** Loose hip-length jackets often worn sideways with sleeves hanging.
- **Women's outer wear:** Loose gowns over fitted dresses and fur-lined cloaks for warmth.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

- **Frock coats:** Knee-length fitted coats with a full skirt. These were the standard formal day coat for much of the era.
- **Morning coats:** Cutaway coats sloping back from the waist. They replaced the frock coat for formal day wear later in the century.
- **Tailcoats:** Formal evening coats cut away at the front.
- **Lounge jackets (1860s onward):** Loose short jackets for casual wear. They became the modern suit jacket.
- **Dinner jackets (1880s):** Tailless evening jackets. Americans called them tuxedos after Tuxedo Park in New York.
- **Norfolk jackets (1860s and 1870s):** Belted tweed jackets for shooting and outdoor sport.
- **Overcoats:** The Chesterfield had a velvet collar. The Inverness had a shoulder cape. The Ulster was a long heavy travel coat with a cape.
- **Pea coats:** Short double-breasted wool coats for sailors.
- **Mackintoshes:** Rubberized raincoats became common.
- **Smoking jackets:** Velvet or silk jackets worn at home while smoking.
- **Working wear:** Rural English workers wore smock frocks. Laborers wore corduroy jackets and heavy wool donkey jackets.
- **Women's outer wear:** Mantles, paletots, shawls and capes were cut to fit over crinolines and bustles. Dolmans had sleeves cut as part of the body for bustle dresses. Paisley shawls were extremely popular in the mid 1800s.

## Ranking by Total Impact

1. Sewn hide and fur garments
2. Waterproof fabrics
3. Synthetic insulation and fleece

# Hats

**Felt is the greatest hat technology in history.** Felting mats animal fibers into a dense fabric without weaving. It produced warm, water-resistant and moldable hats for thousands of years.

## Major Milestones

- **Fur caps (prehistoric):** Ötzi wore a bearskin cap with a chin strap.
- **Felt (ancient):** Steppe peoples made felt thousands of years ago. Felt items from the Pazyryk burials in Siberia are about 2,400 years old.
- **Greek pilos and petasos:** The pilos was a felt cap. The petasos was a wide-brimmed sun hat for travelers.
- **Straw plaiting (ancient onward):** Woven straw hats protected farmers from the sun around the world.
- **Helmets (ancient onward):** Bronze, iron and steel helmets protected soldiers.
- **Beaver felt (16th to 19th centuries):** Beaver fur made superior waterproof felt. Demand drove much of the North American fur trade.
- **Hat blocks:** Wooden forms let hatters shape felt into consistent styles.
- **Silk top hats (early 1800s):** Silk plush replaced beaver as beaver populations collapsed.
- **Bowler hat (1849):** Lock and Company designed a hard felt hat for Edward Coke to protect gamekeepers' heads. Thomas and William Bowler made it.
- **Balaclava (1854):** Knitted head coverings warmed British soldiers during the Crimean War.
- **Baseball caps (1860):** The Brooklyn Excelsiors wore early rounded caps with a short brim.
- **Hard hats (1919):** The Bullard company created the hard boiled hat for miners from steamed canvas and glue.
- **Modern helmets (20th century):** Plastics, foam liners and Kevlar improved protection for work, sport and war.

## Hats by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

- **Coifs:** Close-fitting linen caps tied under the chin. Men of all classes wore them and they often sat under other hats.
- **Hoods:** Separate hoods with a shoulder cape were common. Many had a long tail called a liripipe.
- **Chaperons (15th century):** Hoods were rearranged and worn as turban-like hats.
- **Women's veils and wimples:** Married women covered their hair with veils. Wimples wrapped the neck and chin.
- **Hennins (15th century):** Tall conical headdresses for noblewomen with a veil hanging from the tip.
- **Escoffions:** Horned or padded headdresses for wealthy women.
- **Straw hats:** Wide-brimmed straw hats for field workers.
- **Felt hats:** Brimmed felt hats for travelers and pilgrims.
- **Helmets:** Iron and steel helmets for soldiers and knights. The great helm enclosed the entire head.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

- **Flat caps:** Soft round caps with a low crown and narrow brim. These were the standard hat for English men.
- **Statute of Caps (1571):** Elizabeth I required most men to wear wool caps on Sundays and holidays to support the English wool industry.
- **Bonnets and berets:** Soft velvet caps decorated with feathers and jewels for the wealthy.
- **Capotains (late 1500s):** Tall conical hats with a narrow brim. These later became associated with the Pilgrims.
- **Beaver hats:** Expensive waterproof felt hats for the wealthy.
- **French hoods and gable hoods:** Women's headdresses. The French hood was curved and set back on the head. The gable hood was pointed like a house roof.
- **Plumed hats:** Ostrich feathers decorated fashionable hats.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

- **Top hats:** The standard hat for gentlemen. Silk plush replaced beaver felt by the 1840s.
- **Opera hats:** Collapsible top hats with internal springs that folded flat for the theater.
- **Bowlers (1849):** Hard felt hats that spread from gamekeepers to clerks and the working classes.
- **Deerstalkers:** Tweed caps with front and rear brims and ear flaps for hunting.
- **Boaters (late 1800s):** Stiff flat-topped straw hats for summer and boating.
- **Homburgs (1880s and 1890s):** Stiff felt hats with a dented crown. Edward VII made them fashionable.
- **Fedoras (1880s):** Soft felt hats named after the 1882 play *Fédora*. They were first popular with women.
- **Flat caps:** The standard hat of working men.
- **Balaclavas:** Knitted head coverings from the Crimean War.
- **Women's bonnets:** Bonnets tied under the chin dominated the early and middle years.
- **Women's hats (late 1800s):** Small tilted hats and later huge hats with feathers, flowers and whole stuffed birds. Protest against the feather trade helped found the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds in 1889.
- **Mercury poisoning:** Hatters used mercury to treat fur for felting. The resulting nerve damage led to the phrase mad as a hatter.

## Ranking by Total Impact

1. Felt
2. Straw plaiting
3. Protective helmets

# Socks

**Knitting is the greatest sock technology in history.** Knitted fabric stretches to fit the foot, holds its shape and cushions better than woven cloth. It made close-fitting comfortable socks possible.

## Major Milestones

- **Grass and fur foot linings (prehistoric):** Ötzi stuffed dry grass into his shoes for warmth.
- **Felted socks (ancient Greece):** The Greek poet Hesiod described socks of felted hair called piloi around the 8th century BC.
- **Roman socks:** Romans wore cloth socks called udones. A letter found at Vindolanda in Britain mentions socks sent to a soldier.
- **Nålbinding (ancient to medieval):** A single-needle looping technique that came before knitting. Egyptian split-toe socks made this way date to around 300 AD and were designed for sandals.
- **Viking era socks (10th century):** The Coppergate sock from York was made by nålbinding.
- **True knitting (around 1000 to 1300):** Knitted cotton socks in blue and white patterns survive from Egypt.
- **Knitting reaches Europe (13th century):** Knitted cushion covers from Las Huelgas in Spain date to the 1200s.
- **Knitted stockings (16th century):** Knitted stockings replaced cloth hose.
- **Stocking frame (1589):** William Lee invented a knitting machine. Elizabeth I refused him a patent over fears for hand knitters' jobs.
- **Latch needle (1847):** Matthew Townsend's needle made fast machine knitting practical.
- **Circular knitting machines (19th century):** These produced seamless tubes for socks.
- **Nylon (1939 to 1940):** DuPont's nylon stockings sold millions of pairs in their first year.
- **Elastic tops and spandex (20th century):** Socks stayed up without garters.
- **Performance socks (late 20th century):** Merino wool, synthetic blends, cushioning zones and compression designs.

## Socks by Era

### Medieval Period (about 500 to 1500)

- **Cloth hose:** Most leg and foot coverings were cut from woven wool cloth. Hose served as both pants and socks.
- **Footed hose:** Many hose had sewn-in feet. Others had a stirrup under the foot.
- **Short socks:** Short cloth socks and nålbinding socks were worn by some people, especially in Scandinavia.
- **Foot wraps:** Peasants and soldiers wrapped their feet in strips of cloth.
- **Straw and grass:** Poor people stuffed shoes with straw or grass for warmth.
- **Early knitting:** Knitting reached southern Europe through Islamic Spain. Knitted silk gloves and liturgical items appeared for the Church and nobility.

### Renaissance (about 1400 to 1600)

- **Knitted stockings:** Knitted wool and silk stockings replaced cloth hose. They fit far better and stretched with movement.
- **Silk stockings:** Henry VIII owned Spanish silk stockings. According to tradition Elizabeth I received silk stockings around 1560 and refused cloth stockings afterward.
- **Knitting guilds:** Paris established a guild for hosiery knitters in 1527.
- **Stocking frame (1589):** William Lee's machine began the mechanization of knitting.
- **Garters:** Ribbons or bands tied below or above the knee held stockings up.
- **Clocks:** Embroidered designs decorated the ankles of fine stockings.
- **Common wear:** Hand-knitted wool stockings became a major cottage industry in England and Scotland.

### Victorian Era (1837 to 1901)

- **Machine-knitted socks:** The latch needle and circular knitting machines made socks cheap and plentiful.
- **Men's socks:** Long trousers covered the leg, so men's socks grew shorter and reached the calf. Sock suspenders appeared near the end of the era.
- **Women's stockings:** Black cotton or silk stockings were standard. White stockings were worn with light dresses and in summer.
- **Suspenders:** Late Victorian corsets often included straps that held stockings up.
- **Children's stockings:** Children wore long stockings. Striped stockings were popular in the mid and late 1800s.
- **Wool health socks:** The Jaeger company promoted wool socks for health.
- **Hand knitting:** Knitting remained a common domestic skill. Women knitted socks and balaclavas for soldiers during the Crimean War.

## Ranking by Total Impact

1. Knitting
2. Knitting machines
3. Nylon and elastic fibers
