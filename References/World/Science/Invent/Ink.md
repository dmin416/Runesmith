## Earth research (reference only)

### 1. Carbon inks (from about 3000 BCE)

The oldest ink family is fine black soot suspended in water with a binder that glues the particles to the writing surface once dry. Carbon is chemically inert, so it does not fade. It sits on top of the surface rather than soaking in, which means it can be washed or scraped off smooth materials like parchment.

| Ink | Region / Era | Ingredients | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egyptian black | Egypt, Early Dynastic onward | Soot from burnt oil or wood, gum arabic (acacia sap), water; pressed into dry cakes and rewetted with a reed brush | Papyrus, pottery shards, tomb texts |
| Egyptian red | Egypt | Red ochre (iron oxide) with gum | Headings, dates, corrections. The word "rubric" comes from Latin *rubrica*, red earth |
| Chinese ink stick (*mo*) | China, Han dynasty onward | Pine soot (later also lampblack from tung or sesame oil), animal hide glue, additives such as musk, camphor, cloves, pearl powder or gold leaf | Ground with water on an inkstone for calligraphy, painting and woodblock printing |
| Indian *masi* | India, ancient | Burnt bone, tar, pitch and other carbon sources | Writing on palm leaf and birch bark |
| Roman *atramentum* | Rome | Soot with gum or glue; Pliny also mentions wine lees and cuttlefish ink | General writing on papyrus |
| Western India ink | 1800s onward | Carbon black, shellac dissolved with borax, water | Waterproof once dry; drafting, comics, technical pens |

### 2. Iron gall ink (dominant in Europe roughly 1100s-1800s)

Known in late antiquity, this became the standard European writing ink for about 700 years.

**Ingredients**
- Oak galls: growths caused by gall wasps, rich in tannins. Crushed and soaked or boiled to release tannic and gallic acid
- Iron(II) sulfate, called copperas or green vitriol
- Gum arabic for body and flow
- A liquid base: rainwater, wine, vinegar or beer
- Sometimes cloves or alum as preservatives

**Chemistry:** Iron and gallic acid form a pale complex that oxidizes in air into dark iron(III) gallate. The ink goes on pale grey or bluish and darkens over hours to days.

**Purpose:** It bites into parchment and paper fibers, so it cannot be scraped off cleanly. This made it the ink of choice for legal and official records. Bach's manuscripts and the US Declaration of Independence were written with it. UK registrars still use an iron gall "registrar's ink" for marriage registers.

**Drawback:** Excess acid and free iron cause "ink corrosion." Letters slowly eat through the page and the ink fades to brown.

### 3. Brown inks and washes

| Ink | Ingredients | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Bistre | Soot from burnt beechwood, boiled and filtered | Brown drawing washes, 1300s-1800s (Rembrandt used it) |
| Sepia | Ink sac of the cuttlefish (melanin) | Artists' brown ink, late 1700s-1800s; the name became the color |
| Walnut ink | Green walnut hulls boiled down (tannins, juglone) | Drawing ink and wood stain |

### 4. Colored and precious manuscript inks

Binders were mostly gum arabic or glair (beaten egg white).

| Color | Sources | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Cinnabar/vermilion (mercury sulfide), red lead (minium), kermes and cochineal insects, brazilwood | "Miniature" derives from *minium*. Byzantine emperors reserved a purple-red ink for their signatures |
| Blue | Ultramarine from Afghan lapis lazuli, azurite, indigo, woad | Ultramarine could cost more than gold |
| Green | Verdigris (copper acetate), malachite | Verdigris corrodes parchment much like iron gall |
| Purple | Tyrian purple from murex sea snails, orchil lichen, turnsole (*folium*) | Imperial and luxury use; purple-dyed parchment codices |
| Gold / Silver | Leaf ground with honey or salt, washed, then mixed with gum or glair | Chrysography in Byzantine gospels, Qur'ans and luxury books |

### 5. Early printing inks

- **East Asian woodblock** (China from the 600s-700s; the *Diamond Sutra* of 868 CE): water-based carbon ink brushed onto carved wood. The same ink used for calligraphy.
- **Gutenberg (about 1450):** Water ink beads on metal type, so he used an oil-based ink closer to oil paint: lampblack ground into boiled linseed oil varnish. Analysis of the Gutenberg Bible found unusually high copper and lead content. Applied as a paste with leather ink balls.
- **1450s-1800s:** The recipe barely changed. Printers boiled their own varnish (a notorious fire hazard), sometimes adding rosin.
- **1800s newspapers:** Carbon black in cheap mineral oil. It never truly dried and instead soaked into absorbent newsprint, which is why newspapers rubbed off on hands.

### 6. 19th century chemistry

| Ink | Ingredients | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Blue-black (Henry Stephens, 1830s) | Iron gall plus indigo, later aniline blue | Writing is visible immediately as blue, then darkens permanently. Standard ledger and pen ink |
| Logwood ink | Logwood extract (haematoxylin) with potassium chromate or iron/copper salts | Cheap black for schools and offices |
| Aniline dye inks (after 1856) | Synthetic dyes like methyl violet, nigrosine, eosin | Bright and cheap but prone to fading |
| Copying ink | Dye plus sugar or glycerin | Pressed against damp tissue in a copying press to make mirror copies of letters |
| Hectograph ink | Strong aniline violet dye | Transferred to a gelatin pad to pull dozens of copies |
| Mimeograph ink (1880s) | Oily, thick ink | Forced through a waxed stencil |
| Typewriter ribbon ink | Dye or pigment in non-drying oil | Soaked into fabric ribbons |

### 7. 20th century pen and marker inks

| Ink | Introduced | Ingredients | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fountain pen | 1880s | Water, water-soluble dyes, humectants (glycerin, glycols) to stop nib drying, surfactants for flow, biocide, pH buffer | Low-viscosity flow through a capillary feed |
| Parker Quink | 1931 | Fountain pen ink with a penetrant so it soaks in fast | Quick-drying writing |
| Ballpoint | Bíró patent 1938, mass market 1940s | Solvents (benzyl alcohol, phenoxyethanol, glycols), triarylmethane dyes or pigments, resins, fatty acids as lubricant | Thick paste that won't leak; dries by absorption |
| Rollerball | 1963 | Water-based dye ink | Smooth, wet line from a ball tip |
| Gel | 1984 | Pigment in water thickened with xanthan gum or similar polymers | Shear-thinning: thick at rest, thin while the ball rolls. Allows opaque, white and metallic colors |
| Permanent marker | 1950s-60s | Dyes in alcohols (n-propanol, butanol) with resin | Writing on plastic, metal, glass |
| Highlighter | 1963 | Fluorescent dyes such as pyranine in water | Translucent marking over text |
| Dry erase | 1970s | Pigment, alcohol, oily silicone release agent | Release agent keeps ink from bonding to the board |
| Thermochromic erasable | 2006 | Microcapsules that turn clear above about 60 °C | Friction from an eraser removes writing |

### 8. Industrial printing inks

Basic formula: **pigment** (color) + **vehicle** (resins and oils or solvents) + **additives** (driers, waxes, antioxidants, surfactants).

| Process | Vehicle | Drying Method | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Letterpress | Linseed or alkyd varnish paste | Oxidation and absorption | Books, older newspapers |
| Offset lithography | Rosin-modified phenolic resins, vegetable and mineral oils, cobalt or manganese driers | Oxidation, absorption or heat-set evaporation | Magazines, books, packaging. Must repel water to work with the litho plate |
| Soy ink (1980s) | Soybean oil replacing petroleum oil | Same as offset | Newspapers |
| Flexography | Water, alcohols or ethyl acetate with resins | Evaporation | Boxes, labels, plastic bags |
| Gravure | Toluene or ethyl acetate; very fluid | Evaporation | Long runs of magazines, catalogs, wallpaper |
| Intaglio | Heavy pigmented paste wiped from engraved plates | Oxidation | Banknotes and stamps; leaves a raised, tactile line |
| Screen (plastisol) | PVC particles in plasticizer | Heat fusion around 160 °C | T-shirts, signage |
| UV/EB curable | Acrylate monomers and oligomers with photoinitiators | Instant polymerization under UV light or electron beam | Plastics, labels, fast production |
| Inkjet | Water and glycols with dye or pigment; also solvent and UV versions | Absorption or evaporation | Home, office and wide-format printing |
| Toner (xerography 1959, laser printers 1970s) | Dry powder: styrene-acrylate or polyester resin, carbon black or color pigment, wax, charge-control agents, silica flow agent; magnetic toner adds magnetite | Heat-fused onto paper | Copiers and laser printers |

| Ink | Ingredients | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Invisible (sympathetic) | Lemon or onion juice, milk (revealed by heat scorching the organic matter); cobalt chloride (turns blue when heated); iron sulfate revealed by potassium ferrocyanide as Prussian blue | Espionage: George Washington's Culper Ring, WWI spies |

| MICR (1950s) | Iron oxide in the ink | Magnetically readable numbers on bank checks |

| Tattoo | Ancient: soot in water (Ötzi, about 3300 BCE). Modern: carbon black, iron oxides, titanium dioxide, azo and quinacridone pigments in water, glycerin, alcohol or witch hazel. Historic reds used cinnabar | Permanent skin marking; EU restricted many pigments in 2022 |
| Conductive | Silver flake or nanoparticles, copper, carbon or graphene in a binder | Printed circuits, RFID antennas, sensors |
| Stamp pad | Dye in glycerin or glycols | Non-drying so the pad stays wet |
| Edible | Food dyes in water, ethanol or glycerin | Cake printing, egg and fruit stamping |
| Cephalopod | Melanin and mucus | Animal defense decoy; culinary use in squid ink pasta and black rice |

### 10. Density and viscosity

Viscosity governs how an ink behaves far more than density. Water is the baseline at 1.00 g/cm³ and about 1 mPa·s.

| Ink | Density (g/cm³) | Viscosity (mPa·s) |
|---|---|---|
| Alcohol marker | 0.80-0.90 | 2-10 |
| Gravure (solvent) | 0.85-1.00 | 50-200 |
| Flexographic | 0.90-1.10 | 50-500 |
| Fountain pen dye ink | 1.00-1.03 | 1-3 |
| Iron gall | 1.01-1.05 | 2-5 |
| Liquid carbon / India ink | 1.02-1.10 | 3-15 |
| Inkjet (aqueous) | 1.00-1.10 | 1-5 thermal; 5-20 piezo |
| Gel | 1.00-1.10 (metallics higher) | Thousands at rest, drops sharply under shear |
| Ballpoint paste | 1.05-1.20 | 5,000-25,000 |
| UV curable | 1.05-1.20 | Depends on the press process |
| Tattoo | 1.10-1.40 | Thin liquid to light syrup |
| Plastisol screen ink | 1.20-1.60 | 500-10,000 |
| Offset / letterpress paste | 0.95-1.30 for blacks and process colors; 1.50-2.50 for opaque whites and metallics | 40,000-150,000 |
| Toner | Particles 1.10-1.20; magnetic 1.50-1.90; loose powder about 0.4-0.5 | Dry powder |

**Pigment densities** explain why some historical inks were heavy and settled quickly in the inkwell:

| Pigment | Density (g/cm³) |
|---|---|
| Carbon black / soot | 1.8-2.1 |
| Ultramarine (lapis) | 2.4 |
| Azurite | 3.8 |
| Malachite | 3.6-4.0 |
| Titanium dioxide | 4.2 |
| Ochre / hematite | 3.5-5.3 |
| Magnetite | 5.2 |
| Lead white | 6.6 |
| Vermilion | 8.1 |
| Red lead | 8.9-9.1 |
| Silver | 10.5 |
| Gold | 19.3 |

Mineral-based reds and gold inks sank fast in a thin gum solution. Scribes either stirred constantly or thickened them with extra gum or glair to keep the particles suspended.

### 11. Writing in blood

**Chemistry:** Fresh blood is bright red from oxygenated hemoglobin. Within hours it oxidizes through methemoglobin to hemichrome and dries dark brown. It clots within minutes, so it has to be used immediately, stirred to remove fibrin or mixed with an anticoagulant or another ink. Whole blood has a density of about 1.06 g/cm³ and a viscosity of about 3-4 mPa·s while flowing (plasma alone is about 1.025 g/cm³).

| Practice | Region / Era | Method | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood scriptures (*xiejing*) | China, Japan, Korea; Buddhist tradition from about the 500s CE | Monks drew blood from a finger or the tongue, often mixed with ink, gold or cinnabar | Devotional merit; copying sutras as an act of bodily sacrifice |
| Blood seal (*keppan*) | Japan, samurai era | A cut finger pressed beside a signature on an oath document | Binding loyalty oaths |
| Blood oaths and pacts | Worldwide | Signature or mark in the signer's blood | Sealing alliances, brotherhood oaths; the European "pact with the devil" motif |
| Prisoner and battlefield writing | Many eras | Blood used when no ink was available | Last letters, protest messages, graffiti |
| The "Blood Qur'an" | Iraq, 1997-2000 | Calligrapher Abbas Shakir Joudi wrote a full Qur'an using about 27 liters of Saddam Hussein's blood mixed with preservatives | Political and religious display |
| Ox blood | Europe, North America | Mixed with lime, milk or oil | Barn and floor paints; a binder and red coloring rather than a writing ink |

**Not actually blood:** "Dragon's blood" is a red plant resin from *Dracaena* and *Daemonorops* trees. It was used in red inks, varnishes and violin finishes.

### 14. Writing without ink: impressing, carving and scratching

| Method | Region / Era | Materials and Technique | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cuneiform | Mesopotamia, about 3200 BCE onward | Wedge-tipped reed stylus pressed into wet clay; tablets sun-dried or kiln-fired | Accounts, laws, letters, literature. Fired tablets survive millennia |
| Wax tablets | Greece, Rome, medieval Europe | Wooden boards filled with beeswax (often darkened with soot); pointed stylus to write, flat end to smooth and erase | Drafts, school lessons, notes |
| Oracle bones | China, Shang dynasty (about 1250-1050 BCE) | Characters carved into ox scapulae and turtle shells with a knife; sometimes filled with cinnabar or ink | Divination records; earliest substantial Chinese writing |
| Palm leaf manuscripts | South and Southeast Asia | Letters incised with a metal stylus, then rubbed with lampblack and oil so the cuts turn black | Religious texts, literature |
| Birch bark letters | Novgorod, Russia, 1000s-1400s | Scratched with a bone or metal stylus | Everyday letters and notes |
| Stone inscriptions | Worldwide | Chiseled; Roman letters often painted with red minium | Monuments, laws, tombs (Rosetta Stone, Roman capitals) |
| Runes | Germanic and Norse Europe | Carved into wood, bone, stone; straight strokes avoid splitting along wood grain | Memorials, ownership marks, magic |
| Ogham | Ireland and Britain, about 300s-600s CE | Notches cut along the edge of a standing stone | Names and boundary markers |
| Cast bronze | China, Shang and Zhou dynasties | Characters carved into the mold so text is cast into the vessel | Ritual vessels recording honors and events |
| Lead curse tablets (*defixiones*) | Greek and Roman world | Scratched into thin lead sheets, rolled and nailed or buried | Curses and binding spells |
| Copper plate grants | India | Text engraved into copper sheets, bound with a seal ring | Land grants and royal charters |
| Graffiti | Pompeii and everywhere since | Scratched into plaster with a nail or stylus | Everyday messages, insults, election slogans |
| Quipu | Inca Andes | Knotted, colored cotton or llama-fiber cords | Census, tax and calendar records without script |
| Braille (1824) | France onward | Raised dots punched into paper | Reading by touch |

### 16. Engraving, etching and printmaking

Intaglio prints hold ink in grooves below the surface. The plate is inked, the surface wiped clean and damp paper is forced into the lines under a heavy roller press.

| Technique | Introduced | Method | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engraving | 1430s, Germany | A burin (steel graver) cuts V-shaped grooves into copper | Crisp, swelling lines. Dürer; later steel plates (1820s) for banknotes and stamps because they last longer |
| Etching | About 1500 (Daniel Hopfer, adapted from decorating armor) | Plate coated with an acid-resistant ground (wax, resin, asphaltum); the design is scratched through and the plate bathed in acid | Free, sketch-like lines. Rembrandt, Goya |
| Drypoint | Late 1400s | Needle scratched directly into copper, throwing up a ridge of metal (burr) | Soft, velvety lines that wear down after a few dozen prints |
| Mezzotint | 1640s | Whole plate roughened with a rocker, then scraped and burnished smooth where lights are needed | Rich tonal gradation; reproducing paintings |
| Aquatint | 1760s | Rosin dust melted onto the plate so acid bites around each grain | Tonal areas like watercolor washes. Goya's *Caprichos* |
| Soft-ground etching | 1700s | A tacky ground picks up textures pressed into it | Pencil- or fabric-like texture |
| Photogravure | 1870s-80s | Photographic image transferred to a plate and etched | Fine-art photographic reproduction |

**Etching mordants**

| Mordant | Metal | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nitric acid | Copper, zinc | Fast, bubbles; produces toxic fumes |
| Dutch mordant (hydrochloric acid + potassium chlorate) | Copper | Slow, precise lines |
| Ferric chloride | Copper, brass | No gas; also used to etch circuit boards |
| Copper sulfate | Zinc, aluminum | Low-toxicity modern option |

**Relief and planographic methods**

| Technique | Introduced | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Woodcut | China by the 700s; Europe 1400s | Areas not printed are cut away along the plank grain; ink sits on raised surface |
| Wood engraving | 1790s (Thomas Bewick) | Fine tools on hard end-grain boxwood; book illustration |
| Linocut | Early 1900s | Carved linoleum |
| Lithography | 1796 (Alois Senefelder) | Greasy crayon on limestone; water wets bare stone and greasy ink sticks only to the drawing |
| Screen printing | Ancient stencils; silk screens 1900s | Ink pushed through mesh blocked by a stencil |
| Photoengraving / halftone | 1880s | Photos broken into dots and etched into metal blocks for newspapers |
