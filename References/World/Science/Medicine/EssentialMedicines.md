# Other Essential Medicines

Earth history and production for major non-antibiotic essentials. Antibiotics: `Penicillin.md`, `Antibiotics.md`. Hub: `Index.md`.

## Aspirin

- Willow bark appears in the Ebers Papyrus (about 1550 BCE) and Hippocratic texts for pain and fever.
- 1763: Reverend Edward Stone reported willow bark treating fevers to the Royal Society.
- 1828: Johann Buchner isolated salicin. 1838: Raffaele Piria made salicylic acid from it. Salicylic acid worked but irritated the stomach badly.
- 1853: Charles Gerhardt made crude acetylsalicylic acid.
- 1897: Felix Hoffmann at Bayer made a pure, stable form (Arthur Eichengrün later claimed he directed the work). Bayer trademarked Aspirin on 6 March 1899.
- 1971: John Vane showed aspirin blocks prostaglandin synthesis by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (Nobel Prize 1982). Low-dose aspirin for heart attack and stroke prevention followed.
- **Production:** phenol reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium phenoxide. This is heated with carbon dioxide under high pressure (Kolbe-Schmitt process, about 125°C and 100 atmospheres) to give sodium salicylate. Acidification yields salicylic acid, which is acetylated with acetic anhydride. Aspirin is crystallized, dried and pressed into tablets. Tens of thousands of tonnes are made yearly.

## Insulin

- 1889: Oskar Minkowski and Joseph von Mering showed removing a dog's pancreas caused diabetes.
- 1921: Frederick Banting and Charles Best, in J.J.R. Macleod's lab in Toronto, extracted a pancreatic substance that lowered blood sugar in diabetic dogs. Biochemist James Collip purified it enough for humans.
- January 1922: 14-year-old Leonard Thompson became the first person treated. The first crude injection caused an abscess. Collip's purified extract worked dramatically.
- 1923 Nobel Prize to Banting and Macleod. Banting split his share with Best and Macleod with Collip. The inventors sold the patent to the University of Toronto for $1 each.
- **Animal era:** Eli Lilly began mass production in 1923. George Walden's isoelectric precipitation made large-scale purification possible. Tons of pig and cattle pancreas from slaughterhouses yielded small quantities of insulin. Hans Christian Hagedorn added protamine for longer action (1936), leading to NPH insulin (1946).
- Frederick Sanger sequenced insulin (1955), the first protein sequenced. Dorothy Hodgkin solved its 3D structure (1969).
- **Recombinant era:** in 1978 Genentech scientists produced human insulin A and B chains in E. coli and joined them. Lilly launched Humulin in 1982, the first recombinant drug ever approved.
- **Modern production:**
  - E. coli route: bacteria express proinsulin as insoluble inclusion bodies. Cells are broken open, inclusion bodies dissolved in urea or guanidine, and the protein refolded so the correct disulfide bonds form. Trypsin and carboxypeptidase B cut out the C-peptide.
  - Yeast route (Novo Nordisk): Saccharomyces secretes a folded insulin precursor into the broth, which is enzymatically converted.
  - Both routes purify by multiple chromatography steps including reverse-phase HPLC, then crystallize insulin with zinc.
  - Analogs such as lispro (1996) and glargine (2000) are made by altering the gene sequence.

## Vaccines

- 1796: Edward Jenner inoculated James Phipps with cowpox from milkmaid Sarah Nelmes, then showed Phipps resisted smallpox. Smallpox was declared eradicated on 8 May 1980.
- 1885: Louis Pasteur treated Joseph Meister for rabies with vaccine from dried infected rabbit spinal cord.
- 1890s: diphtheria and tetanus antitoxins. Later, toxoid vaccines made by inactivating the toxins with formaldehyde.
- 1955: Jonas Salk's inactivated polio vaccine, after a 1954 field trial involving about 1.8 million children. Albert Sabin's oral vaccine followed in 1961.
- 2020: mRNA vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna) for COVID-19, built on modified-nucleoside work by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman (Nobel Prize 2023).
- **Production methods:**
  - Egg-based influenza: virus is injected into fertilized chicken eggs incubated 9 to 11 days, allantoic fluid is harvested, virus is purified by high-speed centrifugation, split with detergent and inactivated.
  - Cell-culture vaccines: virus grown in mammalian cell lines in bioreactors.
  - Recombinant protein vaccines: hepatitis B surface antigen made in yeast (1986), HPV virus-like particles.
  - mRNA: a DNA template is transcribed in a cell-free reaction by T7 RNA polymerase using modified nucleosides, the mRNA is capped and purified, then mixed with lipids in microfluidic devices to form lipid nanoparticles.

## Cortisone and corticosteroids

- 1930s: Edward Kendall at the Mayo Clinic and Tadeus Reichstein in Switzerland isolated adrenal steroids.
- 1948: Philip Hench gave cortisone to a bedridden rheumatoid arthritis patient who walked within days. Nobel Prize 1950 for Hench, Kendall and Reichstein.
- Merck's Lewis Sarett made the first supply by a 36-step synthesis from ox bile acid. Enormous amounts of bile produced tiny amounts of drug.
- Russell Marker (1938 to 1943) developed the "Marker degradation," converting diosgenin from wild Mexican yams into progesterone. He co-founded Syntex in Mexico City, which also produced the first oral contraceptive progestin, norethindrone (Carl Djerassi, 1951).
- 1952: Upjohn's Durey Peterson and Herbert Murray found the mold Rhizopus could add an oxygen at carbon 11 of progesterone in one step, replacing many chemical steps. Microbial transformation remains central to steroid manufacturing, including prednisone and dexamethasone.

## Quinine and artemisinin

- Indigenous Andean peoples used cinchona bark. Jesuits brought it to Europe in the 1630s ("Jesuit's bark") for malaria.
- 1820: Pierre-Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Caventou isolated quinine.
- Dutch plantations in Java, grown from seeds smuggled out by Charles Ledger in 1865, dominated supply. Japan's 1942 seizure of Java cut off the Allies, spurring synthetic antimalarials (chloroquine, mepacrine) and Woodward and Doering's 1944 formal synthesis of quinine.
- Quinine is still extracted from cultivated cinchona bark: bark is ground, treated with alkali, extracted with solvent and purified as sulfate salt.
- **Artemisinin:** China's secret Project 523, launched in 1967 to find malaria drugs for North Vietnam, assigned Tu Youyou to screen traditional remedies. A 4th-century text by Ge Hong described soaking sweet wormwood (Artemisia annua) in cold water. In 1971 she switched to low-temperature ether extraction, which preserved the active compound. Nobel Prize 2015.
- Artemisinin is mostly extracted from cultivated wormwood leaves with hexane or other solvents. A semisynthetic route (Jay Keasling, Amyris and Sanofi, 2013) uses engineered yeast to make artemisinic acid, converted chemically. Derivatives such as artesunate and artemether are made semisynthetically and combined with partner drugs (artemisinin combination therapies).

## Morphine

- 1804: Friedrich Sertürner, a German pharmacist's apprentice, isolated morphine from opium, the first alkaloid ever isolated from a plant. Merck began commercial production in 1827.
- The hypodermic syringe (Alexander Wood and Charles Pravaz, 1850s) made injected morphine the battlefield painkiller of the American Civil War and later wars.
- Morphine remains on the WHO Essential Medicines List as the reference drug for severe pain. Licensed growers (Australia's Tasmania supplies a large share, plus Turkey, India, Spain and France) cultivate poppies under International Narcotics Control Board quotas. Licensed manufacturers extract alkaloids from poppy straw.

## Paracetamol (acetaminophen)

- 1877: Harmon Morse synthesized it. Joseph von Mering tested it in 1887 and wrongly concluded it was toxic, favoring phenacetin.
- 1948: Bernard Brodie and Julius Axelrod showed paracetamol was the active metabolite of acetanilide and phenacetin without their blood toxicity.
- Sold as Tylenol (McNeil, 1955) and Panadol (1956).
- **Production:** one route nitrates phenol to p-nitrophenol, reduces it to p-aminophenol, then acetylates with acetic anhydride. A major industrial route hydrogenates nitrobenzene in acid to make p-aminophenol directly. The product is purified, crystallized and pressed into tablets.

## Heparin

- 1916: Jay McLean, a medical student in William Howell's lab at Johns Hopkins, found an anticoagulant in liver extracts. Charles Best and colleagues in Toronto purified it in the 1930s for clinical use.
- **Production:** pig intestinal mucosa from slaughterhouses is digested with enzymes, and heparin is captured on ion-exchange resin, eluted with salt, precipitated with alcohol, bleached and purified. Low-molecular-weight heparins (enoxaparin) are made by controlled chemical or enzymatic breakdown.
- 2008: heparin contaminated with oversulfated chondroitin sulfate from Chinese suppliers was linked to dozens of deaths, leading to new testing standards.

## Statins

- 1973: Akira Endo at Sankyo screened about 6,000 fungal strains and found compactin (mevastatin) in Penicillium citrinum, which blocks HMG-CoA reductase, the key enzyme in cholesterol synthesis.
- Merck found lovastatin in Aspergillus terreus and launched it in 1987, the first approved statin.
- **Production:** lovastatin by fungal fermentation. Simvastatin by chemical modification of lovastatin (now also enzymatically). Atorvastatin (Lipitor, 1997, designed by Bruce Roth) is fully synthetic and became the best-selling drug in history.

## Metformin

- Goat's rue (Galega officinalis), a medieval European remedy for frequent urination, contains galegine, which lowers blood sugar.
- 1922: Emil Werner and James Bell synthesized metformin. Jean Sterne in Paris developed it as a diabetes drug in 1957 under the name Glucophage. US approval came in 1994.
- **Production:** dimethylamine hydrochloride is heated with dicyandiamide (cyanoguanidine), and metformin hydrochloride crystallizes. It is cheap and is the world's first-line drug for type 2 diabetes.
