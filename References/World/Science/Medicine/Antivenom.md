# Antivenom

Earth history and manufacture. Toxin recovery physiology: `../Body/ToxinExposureRecovery.md`. Hub: `Index.md`.

## Early roots

- Ancient accounts describe Mithridates VI of Pontus taking small doses of poisons for immunity. Snake charmers in India, Africa and the Middle East long practiced self-immunization with repeated small bites or venom scratches.
- 1767 to 1781: Felice Fontana in Florence performed thousands of viper venom experiments, laying the groundwork of venom toxicology.
- 1887: Henry Sewall at the University of Michigan injected pigeons with gradually increasing sublethal doses of massasauga rattlesnake venom and showed they survived doses that killed untreated birds.
- 1890: Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato showed that serum from immunized animals transferred protection against diphtheria and tetanus toxins. This "serum therapy" became the template for antivenom.

## Calmette, Phisalix and Bertrand (1891 to 1895)

Albert Calmette, a Pasteur Institute physician, founded the Pasteur Institute in Saigon in 1891. An 1891 flood in Bac Lieu drove monocled cobras into a village where dozens of people were bitten. Calmette began immunizing animals against cobra venom.

In February 1894 Césaire Phisalix and Gabriel Bertrand at the Paris Natural History Museum reported that serum from guinea pigs immunized with heated viper venom protected other animals. Calmette reported similar results the same month. By 1895 Calmette was producing equine anti-cobra serum at the Pasteur Institute of Lille. He believed it neutralized all snake venoms and marketed it that way.

## Vital Brazil and specificity (1901)

Vital Brazil, a Brazilian physician working in rural São Paulo state, found that Calmette's serum failed against Brazilian pit vipers. In 1901 he became director of the new Instituto Butantan in São Paulo. He proved antivenoms are species-specific and produced separate anti-Bothrops (lancehead) and anti-Crotalus (rattlesnake) sera, then polyvalent sera covering several species. He set up a system in which farmers traded live snakes for free antivenom vials and snake-catching tools, supplying the venom the institute needed. Butantan remains one of the world's major antivenom producers.

## Twentieth-century spread and refinement

- **India:** the Haffkine Institute in Bombay and the Central Research Institute in Kasauli developed polyvalent equine antivenom against the "Big Four" (spectacled cobra, common krait, Russell's viper and saw-scaled viper). India still has the world's highest snakebite death toll, estimated at about 58,000 per year.
- **Serum sickness:** whole horse serum caused frequent allergic reactions and serum sickness (described by Clemens von Pirquet and Béla Schick in 1905). In the late 1930s C. G. Pope at Wellcome used pepsin digestion to cut the Fc portion off antibodies, producing smaller, less reactive F(ab')2 fragments. Rodney Porter's 1959 papain work produced Fab fragments and contributed to his 1972 Nobel Prize.
- **Australia:** Commonwealth Serum Laboratories (CSL) released tiger snake antivenom in 1930. Taipan antivenom (1955) came from venom of a snake caught by 20-year-old Kevin Budden, who died from its bite in 1950. Brown snake, death adder, sea snake, stonefish, redback spider and box jellyfish (made in sheep) antivenoms followed. Struan Sutherland's funnel-web spider antivenom (1980, made in rabbits) ended deaths from that spider.
- **United States:** Wyeth's whole-IgG equine Antivenin (Crotalidae) Polyvalent appeared in the 1950s and caused frequent reactions. CroFab (FDA-approved 2000) is an ovine Fab product from sheep immunized with four North American pit viper venoms and is affinity-purified. Anavip (2015), an equine F(ab')2 product made by Instituto Bioclon in Mexico, added longer-lasting coverage. Coral snake antivenom production stopped in 2003 and existing lots have been kept in use through expiration-date extensions.
- **Africa:** Sanofi Pasteur ended production of FAV-Afrique in 2014 and its last lots expired in 2016, worsening shortages across sub-Saharan Africa.

## How antivenom is made, step by step

**1. Snake keeping and venom collection.**
- Serpentaria keep hundreds to thousands of snakes per species, sourced from multiple geographic regions because venom composition varies by locality, age and diet.
- Snakes are "milked" every few weeks. A handler holds the head and the snake bites through a membrane stretched over a glass container while the venom glands are gently massaged. Some facilities use mild electrical stimulation.
- Yields range from a few milligrams for kraits to over a gram for large vipers.
- Venom is pooled per species, centrifuged to remove debris, freeze-dried and stored at -20°C or colder. Each batch is characterized for protein content, toxicity (LD50 in mice) and composition (proteomic "venomics").

**2. Immunogen preparation.** Venom is usually used in native form to preserve the antibody targets. Some programs partially detoxify it with formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde or irradiation. It is emulsified with an adjuvant: complete Freund's adjuvant for the first dose, incomplete Freund's or alum, bentonite or alginate for boosters.

**3. Animals.** Horses dominate because they are large, docile and yield huge plasma volumes. Sheep are used for Fab products, plus donkeys, mules, camels and rabbits for specific products. Llamas and alpacas are used experimentally for nanobodies. Chickens (antibodies harvested from egg yolk) are experimental.

**4. Immunization schedule.** Small initial doses (micrograms to low milligrams) are injected subcutaneously at multiple sites near lymph nodes. Doses increase every one to two weeks over roughly 2 to 4 months. Antibody titers are tracked by ELISA and mouse neutralization tests. Once high titers are reached the animal receives periodic boosters and can produce plasma for several years under veterinary care.

**5. Plasma collection.** Blood is drawn from the jugular vein into bags with citrate anticoagulant, roughly 6 to 8 liters per session in a large horse. Red cells settle out or are centrifuged, plasma is removed and red cells are returned to the animal (plasmapheresis), allowing frequent collection.

**6. Fractionation and purification.**
- **Caprylic acid precipitation:** adding octanoic acid at slightly acidic pH precipitates albumin and most non-IgG proteins while IgG stays in solution. This method, refined at Costa Rica's Clodomiro Picado Institute, is simple and cheap. Ammonium sulfate precipitation is the older alternative.
- **F(ab')2 products:** IgG is digested with pepsin at about pH 3 to 3.5. The Fc portion breaks down and is removed by precipitation or chromatography.
- **Fab products:** IgG (often ovine) is digested with papain and the Fc fragments are removed by ion-exchange chromatography.
- **Affinity purification:** passing antibodies over immobilized venom keeps only venom-binding antibodies, which may be a small fraction of the animal's total antibodies.
- **Viral safety:** low pH, caprylic acid and nanofiltration inactivate or remove viruses.
- **Final steps:** ultrafiltration and diafiltration concentrate the product and remove reagents. A preservative such as phenol or cresol may be added. The product is sterile-filtered at 0.22 µm, filled into vials and either kept liquid at 2 to 8°C or freeze-dried. Freeze-dried vials last years without refrigeration, critical in rural tropics.

**7. Quality control.**
- **Potency:** measured in mice as the ED50, the dose that protects half the animals from a set multiple of the venom's lethal dose, following WHO guidelines. Potency is expressed as milligrams of venom neutralized per mL. Indian polyvalent antivenom, for example, is specified to neutralize 0.6 mg of cobra venom, 0.45 mg of krait venom, 0.6 mg of Russell's viper venom and 0.45 mg of saw-scaled viper venom per mL.
- **Other tests:** sterility, endotoxin, protein content, aggregate levels (aggregates drive allergic reactions), anticomplementary activity, preservative content and moisture.

**8. Use.** Antivenom is given intravenously, dosed by vials according to severity, with adrenaline on hand for anaphylaxis.

## The snakebite burden and next-generation antivenoms

WHO estimates 1.8 to 2.7 million envenomings and roughly 81,000 to 138,000 deaths per year, plus several hundred thousand permanent disabilities. WHO restored snakebite to its neglected tropical disease list in 2017 and in 2019 set a goal to halve deaths and disability by 2030.

Newer approaches:
- **Recombinant human monoclonal antibody cocktails:** consistent, non-animal, low-allergy products, developed notably at the Technical University of Denmark.
- **Broadly neutralizing antibodies:** Tim Friede, an American who immunized himself with hundreds of venom injections and bites over nearly two decades, provided blood from which researchers (Centivax and Columbia University) isolated antibodies. A 2025 Cell paper showed two of them plus varespladib protected mice against a broad panel of deadly elapid venoms.
- **Nanobodies:** a 2025 Nature study used a cocktail of small antibodies from immunized llamas and alpacas to neutralize venoms of most medically important African elapids.
- **AI-designed proteins:** David Baker's lab published de novo designed proteins in 2025 that neutralize three-finger toxins.
- **Small-molecule inhibitors:** varespladib (phospholipase A2 inhibitor), marimastat and unithiol (metalloproteinase inhibitors) are in trials as cheap oral drugs to give in the field before hospital arrival.
