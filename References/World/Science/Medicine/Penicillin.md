# Penicillin

Earth history and industrial process. Sibling antibiotic catalog: `Antibiotics.md`. Hub: `Index.md`.

## Before Fleming

- Moldy bread and moldy poultices on wounds appear in folk medicine across many cultures, with no understanding of why they sometimes worked.
- 1870 to 1875: John Burdon Sanderson, Joseph Lister and John Tyndall each noticed that Penicillium molds suppressed bacterial growth. Lister even treated a wound with a mold preparation in 1884 without publishing it.
- 1893: Bartolomeo Gosio isolated mycophenolic acid from a Penicillium, the first antibacterial compound purified from a mold. It was forgotten.
- 1897: Ernest Duchesne, a French army medical student, showed in his thesis that Penicillium glaucum protected guinea pigs from lethal typhoid infection. The Pasteur Institute ignored it and Duchesne died of tuberculosis in 1912.

## Fleming's discovery (1928 to 1929)

Alexander Fleming was a bacteriologist in Almroth Wright's Inoculation Department at St Mary's Hospital, Paddington, London. Before his August holiday in 1928 he left stacks of Staphylococcus culture plates on his bench. On 3 September 1928 he returned and saw that one plate held a mold colony surrounded by a clear ring where staph colonies had dissolved.

The spore most likely drifted up from C. J. La Touche's mycology lab one floor below. A cool spell followed by warm weather let the mold establish itself before the bacteria grew, a timing that rarely reproduces. The mold was later classified as Penicillium notatum and reclassified in 2011 as Penicillium rubens.

Fleming grew the mold in broth and found the filtrate ("mold juice") killed staphylococci, streptococci, pneumococci, gonococci and diphtheria bacilli. It did nothing to Gram-negative gut bacteria such as E. coli or typhoid. It was nontoxic to rabbits and mice and did not damage white blood cells. He named it penicillin on 7 March 1929 and published in the British Journal of Experimental Pathology that year.

His assistants Stuart Craddock and Frederick Ridley tried to concentrate it. The substance kept falling apart. Fleming used it mostly as a lab tool to isolate Haemophilus influenzae from mixed cultures. Harold Raistrick at the London School of Hygiene got penicillin into ether in 1932 and lost the activity during evaporation.

1930 to 1931: Cecil George Paine, a former Fleming student in Sheffield, used crude filtrate to cure gonococcal eye infections in newborns and a pneumococcal eye infection in a colliery manager. He never published.

## Oxford: turning a curiosity into a drug (1938 to 1941)

Howard Florey ran the Sir William Dunn School of Pathology at Oxford. Ernst Chain, a German-Jewish biochemist who fled the Nazis, joined in 1935. In 1938 they surveyed microbial antagonism papers and chose penicillin for study. The Dunn School already held a Fleming culture from George Dreyer's collection. The Rockefeller Foundation funded the work.

Norman Heatley made the project possible:
- **Assay:** a cylinder-plate method measuring zones of bacterial inhibition, defining the "Oxford unit" so batches could be compared.
- **Extraction:** penicillin is stable near neutral pH and breaks down fast in acid. Heatley's trick was to chill the broth, acidify it, immediately pull penicillin into an organic solvent (ether, then amyl acetate) and then shift it back into neutral water. This back-extraction ran in a countercurrent apparatus he built from glass tubing, bottles and scavenged lab parts.
- **Culture vessels:** first bedpans, biscuit tins and bottles. Heatley then designed stackable rectangular ceramic vessels made by James Macintyre & Co. in Burslem. A team of young women ("the penicillin girls") was hired to inoculate and harvest them.

**The mouse experiment (25 May 1940):** 8 mice received lethal doses of Streptococcus pyogenes. 4 got penicillin. By morning the 4 untreated mice were dead while the treated mice lived. The Lancet paper "Penicillin as a Chemotherapeutic Agent" followed on 24 August 1940. Facing possible German invasion, team members rubbed mold spores into their coat linings so the strain could survive if the lab were destroyed.

**First human uses:**
- 27 January 1941: Elva Akers, a terminal cancer patient, received an injection to test toxicity. She developed fever from impurities, which the team then removed.
- 12 February 1941: Albert Alexander, a 43-year-old Oxford policeman, had a spreading staph and strep infection of the face, eye and lungs. He improved dramatically within five days. Supply ran out despite the team recovering penicillin from his urine and reprocessing it. He relapsed and died on 15 March 1941.
- Later cases, mostly children who needed smaller doses, were cured. The Lancet reported them in August 1941.

## American scale-up (1941 to 1945)

Britain lacked industrial capacity in wartime. Florey and Heatley flew to the United States in June 1941. USDA mycologist Charles Thom sent them to the Northern Regional Research Laboratory (NRRL) in Peoria, Illinois, where Robert Coghill ran fermentation research.

Key breakthroughs:
- **Corn steep liquor:** Andrew Moyer added this waste product of corn wet-milling to the medium and swapped lactose for sucrose. Yields rose roughly tenfold. Corn steep liquor also contained phenylacetic acid, the side-chain precursor of penicillin G, which is why G became the standard penicillin.
- **Submerged deep-tank fermentation:** Fleming's mold grew only as a surface mat. Moving to giant aerated stirred tanks required a strain that grew submerged.
- **The cantaloupe:** in 1943 lab aide Mary Hunt ("Moldy Mary") brought in a moldy cantaloupe from a Peoria market. Its Penicillium chrysogenum strain, NRRL 1951, outproduced everything else. The Army Transport Command had also gathered soil and mold samples worldwide for screening.
- **Mutation breeding:** X-ray mutagenesis at the Carnegie Institution, Cold Spring Harbor (Milislav Demerec) produced strain X-1612. UV mutagenesis at the University of Wisconsin produced Q-176, the ancestor of nearly every industrial penicillin strain since. Fleming's original strain made about 2 units per mL. Q-176 made several hundred times that.

**Industry:** the Office of Scientific Research and Development and the War Production Board coordinated Merck, Squibb, Pfizer, Lederle, Abbott and others (21 firms by the end). Pfizer's Jasper Kane championed deep-tank fermentation. Pfizer bought a disused ice plant on Marcy Avenue in Brooklyn and opened it on 1 March 1944 with fourteen 7,500-gallon tanks. Pfizer became the largest producer and supplied much of the penicillin that went ashore at Normandy.

**Milestone patients and numbers:**
- 14 March 1942: Anne Miller, dying of streptococcal sepsis after a miscarriage in New Haven, received about half the total US supply. She recovered and lived to 90.
- November 1942: Merck rushed penicillin to Boston for burn victims of the Cocoanut Grove nightclub fire.
- US output went from about 21 billion units in 1943 to about 1.66 trillion in 1944 to over 6.8 trillion in 1945.
- 2.3 million doses were prepared for D-Day.
- The price per dose fell from about $20 in 1943 to around $0.55 by 1946.
- US civilian sale began 15 March 1945.

**Nobel Prize 1945:** Fleming, Florey and Chain. Heatley was left out and received an Oxford honorary Doctorate of Medicine in 1990, the first awarded to a non-physician in Oxford's history.

## Structure, synthesis and semisynthetic penicillins

- Wartime Anglo-American chemists (about a thousand across 39 labs) tried to synthesize penicillin and failed. The structure itself was disputed.
- 1945: Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin and Barbara Low used X-ray crystallography to prove the four-membered beta-lactam ring fused to a thiazolidine ring. Hodgkin later won the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
- Mechanism: the strained beta-lactam ring acylates penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases) and blocks cross-linking of the peptidoglycan cell wall. Bacteria then burst. Human cells have no cell wall, so toxicity is low. Allergy is the main hazard.
- 1951: Ernst Brandl and Hans Margreiter at Biochemie in Kundl, Austria developed penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin), acid-stable enough to take by mouth.
- 1957: John Sheehan at MIT achieved total synthesis of penicillin V using a carbodiimide reagent to close the strained ring. Fermentation remained far cheaper.
- 1957 to 1959: Beecham scientists Batchelor, Doyle, Nayler and Rolinson isolated **6-aminopenicillanic acid (6-APA)**, the penicillin core without its side chain. Chemists could now bolt on any side chain:
  - Methicillin (1959): resists the penicillinase enzyme.
  - Ampicillin (1961): first broad-spectrum penicillin, active against some Gram-negatives.
  - Carbenicillin (1967): active against Pseudomonas.
  - Amoxicillin (1972): better oral absorption, now among the most prescribed drugs worldwide.
  - Clavulanic acid (Beecham, mid-1970s, from Streptomyces clavuligerus): a beta-lactamase inhibitor. Combined with amoxicillin as Augmentin in 1981.

## Resistance

- 1940: Abraham and Chain described penicillinase in bacteria before penicillin was even in clinical use.
- 1945: Fleming's Nobel lecture predicted that underdosing would breed resistant bacteria.
- By the 1950s most hospital Staphylococcus aureus strains were resistant. Today over 90% of S. aureus produce penicillinase.
- 1961: MRSA appeared in Britain within about two years of methicillin's introduction.
- Penicillin remains first-line for syphilis (benzathine penicillin G), strep throat and rheumatic fever prevention. Benzathine penicillin G has suffered repeated shortages, including a major one beginning in 2023.

## How penicillin is made today, step by step

**1. Strain.** Industrial Penicillium chrysogenum strains descend from Q-176 after decades of mutation and selection. Their genomes carry multiple tandem copies of the penicillin gene cluster plus mutations that redirect metabolism toward penicillin. The best strains yield roughly 40 to 50 grams per liter, thousands of times Fleming's strain. Master cell banks are stored as freeze-dried or frozen spores.

**2. Inoculum train.** Spores are revived on solid medium (agar or sterilized rice grains), then grown in shake flasks, then in one or two seed fermenters. Each stage transfers into a vessel about 10 times larger.

**3. Production fermentation.**
- Stainless steel fermenters of roughly 40,000 to over 200,000 liters, steam-sterilized at 121°C.
- Medium: a carbon source (glucose or sucrose syrup), nitrogen (corn steep liquor historically, now often ammonia and ammonium sulfate), sulfate (sulfur for cysteine), phosphate, trace metals and antifoam (vegetable oil or silicone).
- Side-chain precursor fed continuously: phenylacetic acid for penicillin G or phenoxyacetic acid for penicillin V. It is toxic to the mold in excess, so feeding is metered.
- Temperature 25 to 27°C, pH held near 6.5 with ammonia or acid, intense agitation and sterile air sparging to keep dissolved oxygen high. Cooling coils remove heavy metabolic heat.
- **Fed-batch mode:** an initial growth phase of about 40 hours builds mycelium. Then glucose is fed slowly because high glucose represses penicillin genes (catabolite repression). The mold stays in a slow-growing state that favors penicillin output.
- A run lasts about 6 to 8 days.

**4. Biosynthesis inside the mold.**
- ACV synthetase joins L-α-aminoadipic acid, L-cysteine and L-valine into a tripeptide (ACV).
- Isopenicillin N synthase closes both rings in a single oxygen-dependent reaction, forming isopenicillin N.
- Acyltransferase, working in peroxisomes, swaps the aminoadipyl side chain for phenylacetyl, producing penicillin G. The product is secreted into the broth.

**5. Harvest and filtration.** The broth is cooled to about 0 to 5°C. Rotary vacuum drum filters strip out the mycelium, which is washed to recover trapped penicillin.

**6. Solvent extraction.** The clear filtrate is acidified to pH 2.0 to 2.5 with sulfuric or phosphoric acid. Penicillin degrades within minutes at that pH, so it is pulled immediately into butyl acetate in Podbielniak centrifugal countercurrent extractors with contact times of seconds. The solvent is then mixed with a neutral aqueous potassium buffer, which pulls penicillin back into water. Repeating the cycle concentrates it about 80 to 100 times. Activated charcoal removes pigments and impurities.

**7. Crystallization and drying.** Potassium acetate in butanol is added (or water is removed by azeotropic distillation with butanol), and potassium penicillin G crystallizes. Crystals are filtered, washed with dry solvent and vacuum dried to over 99% purity. Solvents are distilled and recycled.

**8. Conversion to 6-APA and semisynthetics.**
- Penicillin G is dissolved in water and passed over immobilized penicillin G acylase at pH 7.5 to 8 and about 30 to 37°C. The enzyme clips off phenylacetic acid, leaving 6-APA. Ammonia neutralizes the released acid, and the phenylacetic acid is recovered and recycled back into fermentation.
- 6-APA is crystallized by lowering the pH to its isoelectric point near 4.3.
- This enzymatic route replaced the older chemical cleavage, which needed phosphorus pentachloride, silylation and temperatures near -40°C in chlorinated solvents.
- Amoxicillin is now made enzymatically by coupling 6-APA with D-p-hydroxyphenylglycine methyl ester using the same acylase family, then crystallizing amoxicillin trihydrate.
- For cephalosporins, the penicillin ring can be chemically expanded to 7-ADCA (the cephalexin core). Engineered strains can produce cephalosporin precursors directly.

**9. Formulation and quality control.**
- Injectables: the drug solution is sterile-filtered and crystallized aseptically, then filled as powder into vials. Benzathine penicillin G is a long-acting suspension for deep intramuscular injection.
- Oral forms: penicillin V potassium tablets and amoxicillin capsules, tablets and suspensions.
- Testing covers potency, purity, endotoxin, sterility, moisture and degradation products.
- Beta-lactam manufacturing must run in physically segregated, dedicated facilities because trace cross-contamination can trigger allergic reactions in people taking other drugs.

**10. Global supply.** China produces the large majority of the world's penicillin G and 6-APA. Sandoz's plant at Kundl, Austria, the site where penicillin V was invented, is the last large fully integrated penicillin producer in the Western world. India has funded new domestic fermentation capacity to reduce dependence on imports.
