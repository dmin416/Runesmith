# Other Antibiotics

Earth history and production after penicillin. Penicillin deep file: `Penicillin.md`. Hub: `Index.md`.

## Precursors: synthetic chemotherapy

- **Salvarsan (1909 to 1910):** Paul Ehrlich and Sahachiro Hata screened hundreds of arsenic compounds for a "magic bullet" against syphilis. Compound 606, arsphenamine, worked and Hoechst marketed it in 1910. Neosalvarsan followed in 1912. It was toxic and hard to administer. It established the idea of chemicals that selectively kill microbes.
- **Prontosil and sulfa drugs (1932 to 1935):** at Bayer (IG Farben), Gerhard Domagk tested dyes synthesized by Josef Klarer and Fritz Mietzsch. A red azo dye, Prontosil, protected mice from streptococcal infection in 1932. Domagk used it on his own daughter's severe strep infection. Published in 1935.
  - Late 1935: Jacques and Thérèse Tréfouël, Federico Nitti and Daniel Bovet at the Pasteur Institute showed the body splits Prontosil into sulfanilamide, an unpatentable compound first made in 1908. Dozens of manufacturers began producing it.
  - Leonard Colebrook cut deaths from childbed fever sharply at Queen Charlotte's Hospital, London.
  - 1937: the Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster. S.E. Massengill dissolved the drug in diethylene glycol (antifreeze), killing over 100 people. The US Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act of 1938 followed, requiring safety evidence before sale.
  - 1938: sulfapyridine (M&B 693) from May & Baker. It treated Winston Churchill's pneumonia in 1943.
  - 1939: Domagk was awarded the Nobel Prize. The Nazi government forced him to decline it and he received it in 1947.
  - Production: acetanilide is chlorosulfonated to p-acetamidobenzenesulfonyl chloride, reacted with ammonia or an amine and then hydrolyzed. Varying the amine yields different sulfa drugs.
- **Gramicidin (1939):** René Dubos at the Rockefeller Institute deliberately searched soil bacteria for an antibacterial and found it in Bacillus brevis. Too toxic for internal use. It proved soil microbes were a worthwhile hunting ground.

## Streptomycin and the soil-screening method

Selman Waksman, a soil microbiologist at Rutgers, built a systematic program screening actinomycetes (filamentous soil bacteria). He coined the word "antibiotic." His lab found actinomycin (1940) and streptothricin (1942), both too toxic.

In October 1943 his graduate student Albert Schatz isolated streptomycin from Streptomyces griseus. It was the first drug effective against tuberculosis and against many Gram-negative bacteria penicillin could not touch.
- William Feldman and Corwin Hinshaw at the Mayo Clinic proved it worked in guinea pigs. Patricia Thomas became the first human TB patient treated, in November 1944.
- Merck developed industrial production.
- 1948: the British Medical Research Council streptomycin trial, designed by Austin Bradford Hill, became the first modern randomized controlled trial.
- Resistance appeared fast when it was used alone. Combining it with para-aminosalicylic acid (Jörgen Lehmann, 1946) and isoniazid (1952) made TB curable.
- Schatz sued in 1950, won a settlement and was legally recognized as co-discoverer. The 1952 Nobel Prize went to Waksman alone.

Streptomycin production: S. griseus is fermented aerobically at about 28°C on soybean meal and glucose for roughly a week. Streptomycin is water-soluble and strongly basic, so it is captured on cation-exchange resin or activated carbon, eluted with acid, purified and precipitated as streptomycin sulfate.

## How other antibiotics are produced

The fermentation front end resembles penicillin: banked strain, seed train, large aerated fed-batch tanks (bacterial actinomycetes usually at 28 to 30°C on starch, glucose and soy meal). Recovery depends on the molecule's chemistry:
- **Aminoglycosides** (streptomycin, gentamicin, neomycin): basic and water-soluble, so ion-exchange capture and precipitation as sulfate salts.
- **Macrolides** (erythromycin): extracted into solvent at alkaline pH and crystallized. Azithromycin is made from erythromycin by an oxime and Beckmann rearrangement that expands the ring and inserts a nitrogen. Clarithromycin is made by methylation.
- **Tetracyclines:** fermentation chloride levels decide whether the organism makes chlortetracycline or tetracycline. Recovery uses pH-driven precipitation or metal complexes. Doxycycline (1967) is made semisynthetically from oxytetracycline.
- **Cephalosporins:** Acremonium chrysogenum ferments cephalosporin C. Two enzymes (D-amino acid oxidase and glutaryl acylase) convert it to 7-ACA, the core for dozens of injectable cephalosporins, replacing older chemical routes.
- **Glycopeptides** (vancomycin): resin adsorption, then multiple chromatography steps. Early batches were so impure clinicians called it "Mississippi mud," which caused much of its early kidney toxicity.
- **Fully synthetic classes:** sulfonamides, fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim, linezolid and chloramphenicol are made entirely by organic synthesis.

## The discovery gap and resistance crisis

Most antibiotic classes in use today were discovered before 1970. Easy soil finds ran out, and antibiotics earn little because they are taken briefly and new ones are held in reserve. A 2022 Lancet study attributed 1.27 million deaths in 2019 directly to antimicrobial resistance. A 2024 Lancet analysis projected about 39 million cumulative resistance-attributable deaths from 2025 to 2050.

Newer approaches:
- Teixobactin (2015): found with the iChip, a device for culturing previously unculturable soil bacteria.
- Halicin (2020) and abaucin (2023): identified with machine learning at MIT and McMaster.
- Zosurabalpin (Roche, published 2024): a new class targeting carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter, in clinical trials.
