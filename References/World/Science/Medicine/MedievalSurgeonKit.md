## Magic and Its Surgical Uses

### Barrier magic

| Use | How |
|---|---|
| Sterile field | Bubble around the open wound excluding air, dust, flies and droplets from onlookers |
| Atraumatic clamp | Plane compressing a vessel (Pringle, aortic control, limb tourniquet) without crushing it |
| Hemostasis | Steady pressure on oozing surfaces; patch over a hole in a large vein |
| Retraction | Walls holding liver, bowel, ribs or wound edges apart: replaces 1 to 3 assistants |
| Organ protection | Shield over bowel, bladder, nerves or major vessels while cutting nearby |
| Containment | Seal around infected tissue, bowel contents, cysts or tumors to prevent spillage |
| Gloves | Thin film over hands; sterile and impermeable (gloves did not exist until 1889) |
| Airway | Holding the jaw forward or the tongue off the throat during anesthesia |
| Splint / cast | Rigid support for fractures until a plaster cast sets |
| Pressure sterilizer | A sealed barrier vessel holding water and instruments while heated: pressure raises the boiling point, acting as an autoclave that kills spores |
| Fire safety | Enclosing candles or lamps away from ether vapor |
| Wound closure assist | Holding edges together while sutures are placed |

### Magic knives

- Never-dull edge gives clean incisions with minimal tissue crush, which heals better and resists infection.
- Instant cleaning means no carrying of skin bacteria into deep tissue.
- If the knife also seals vessels, it works like a modern energy device: liver transection and thyroid surgery become far less bloody.
- If the knife can cut bone cleanly, amputations, craniotomies and rib resections become fast.

### Suction

- Clear field: surgeon sees the bleeding point instead of working in a pool of blood.
- Suction dissection of liver, brain and fatty tissue.
- Aspiration of pus, cyst fluid, pleural fluid, blood from the abdomen.
- Airway clearance: removal of vomit, blood and secretions from the mouth and trachea during anesthesia. This alone prevents a major cause of anesthetic death (aspiration).
- **Autotransfusion (cell salvage)**: blood sucked from a clean surgical field into a vessel containing citrate anticoagulant, strained through boiled linen, then returned into a vein. Blood lost into the abdomen from a ruptured spleen, liver or ectopic pregnancy goes back into the patient. Not used for blood contaminated by bowel contents or cancer.
- Chest drainage: steady suction on a chest tube re-expands the lung.

### Alcohol

- **Skin and hand antisepsis**: 60 to 80% ethanol is more effective than pure alcohol (some water is needed for the alcohol to denature bacterial proteins). Dilute strong spirit to about 70%.
- **Instrument wiping** (supplementary to boiling, not a replacement: alcohol does not kill spores).
- **Feedstock for ether**: diethyl ether is made by heating ethanol with sulfuric acid and distilling. Valerius Cordus produced it in 1540. Sulfuric acid comes from distilling green vitriol (iron sulfate) or burning sulfur with saltpeter over water.
- **Fuel** for clean flame to heat instruments (away from ether).
- **Drinking alcohol is a poor anesthetic**: the dose needed for surgical insensibility is close to the lethal dose and causes vomiting and aspiration.

## Instruments a Medieval Smith Can Make with Guidance

- Scalpels and bistouries (magic knives may replace).
- Hemostats with ratchet locks (Kelly, Crile, mosquito, right-angle).
- Needle holders with locking jaws.
- Tissue forceps: toothed and smooth.
- Retractors: hand-held (Deaver, Richardson, Army-Navy), self-retaining (Balfour frame).
- Curved surgical needles (fine steel wire, filed and tempered, with eyes) or swaged needles.
- Trocars and cannulas (silver or steel tubes) for draining chest, abdomen, bladder.
- Hollow needles for injection and IV access: drawn silver or steel tubes. A syringe body of glass or metal with a leather or waxed plunger.
- Trephines and burr drills for skull work.
- Bone saws (Gigli wire saw from twisted steel wire), rongeurs, osteotomes, mallets, periosteal elevators.
- Rib spreader.
- Bowel clamps (soft jawed: jaws wrapped in linen).
- Urethral sounds and catheters (silver, as used historically).
- Specula (vaginal, nasal, ear, anal).
- Tonsil snares, mastoid gouges.
- Stethoscope (a simple wooden tube, Laennec 1816) and a sphygmomanometer (mercury column with an arm cuff, buildable from glass, mercury and leather).
- Thermometer (glass and mercury or alcohol).

## Suture Materials

| Material | Source | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Silk | Trade from the East | Strong, handles well, permanent; best all-purpose ligature |
| Linen thread | Flax | Permanent; adequate for ties and fascia |
| Catgut | Sheep or cattle intestine submucosa, twisted and dried | Absorbable in days to weeks; for bowel inner layer, bladder, uterus; must be sterilized carefully (historically a source of tetanus) |
| Horsehair | Tail hair | Fine skin sutures, minimal reaction |
| Tendon fibers | Animal tendons | Absorbable, strong |
| Silver or gold wire | Drawn metal | Permanent, inert; fascia, bone wiring, sternum |

All sutures boiled or pressure-sterilized before use. Silk can be soaked in alcohol or iodine solution after boiling.

## Antisepsis Protocol

1. Operating room cleaned with lime wash or boiled water and soap. Bright daylight from windows or barrier-shielded lamps.
2. Surgeon and assistants: hair covered, clean boiled linen gowns, mouth covered with linen.
3. Hand scrub: soap and running water for several minutes, nails trimmed and scrubbed, then 70% alcohol rub. Barrier gloves if available.
4. Instruments: boiled 20 minutes minimum; pressure sterilization (barrier vessel) whenever possible.
5. Linen drapes, sponges and dressings boiled and dried in a clean place, or baked.
6. Patient skin: washed with soap, shaved at the site just before surgery, painted with 70% alcohol then tincture of iodine.
7. **Iodine**: extracted from seaweed ash with sulfuric acid (Bernard Courtois, 1811). Dissolved in alcohol it becomes tincture of iodine, one of the best skin antiseptics before modern products.
8. Minimal talking over the wound. Barrier bubble over the field if possible.
9. Wounds that are dirty (bites, battle wounds, perforated bowel) are left open and closed a few days later if clean (delayed primary closure).

## Infection Control Without Antibiotics

- **Debridement**: removal of all dead tissue and foreign material. The single most important wound treatment.
- **Drainage**: pus must have a path out.
- **Irrigation**: large volumes of boiled salt water.
- **Honey**: high sugar and natural hydrogen peroxide production; effective wound dressing.
- **Maggot therapy**: sterile blowfly larvae eat only dead tissue; used for chronic wounds and osteomyelitis.
- **Iodine, dilute alcohol, dilute vinegar** for wound washing.
- **Silver** (silver nitrate solution, silver foil): antimicrobial; silver nitrate drops in newborn eyes prevent gonococcal blindness (Credé 1881).
- **Crude penicillin**: *Penicillium* mold grown on bread or broth can produce small amounts of penicillin. Yields and purity are unreliable without industrial methods. Possible as a long project; not a dependable tool. See `Penicillin.md`.
- **Sulfonamide** synthesis requires an industrial organic chemistry base (coal tar dyes). Out of reach early. See `Antibiotics.md`.
- Clean water and handwashing reduce postoperative and childbirth infection more than any single drug (Semmelweis, 1847).

## Anesthesia

| Agent | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Diethyl ether | Ethanol + sulfuric acid, distilled | Best choice. Wide safety margin; supports breathing and blood pressure; slow induction; causes nausea. Extremely flammable: no flame nearby. Dropped onto a cloth mask (open drop method). |
| Chloroform | Bleaching powder (chlorinated lime) + ethanol or acetone | Fast and pleasant but causes sudden cardiac arrest and liver damage. Avoid, especially for liver surgery. |
| Nitrous oxide | Heating ammonium nitrate | Weak; analgesia for short procedures and childbirth only |
| Opium / laudanum | Poppy | Premedication and postoperative pain; respiratory depression in excess |
| Henbane, mandrake, hemlock ("soporific sponge") | Plants | Unpredictable and dangerous; avoid |
| Cold | Snow, ice | Numbs limbs for quick amputation (used historically) |
| Local anesthesia | Cocaine requires New World coca; procaine and lidocaine require advanced chemistry | Generally unavailable |

Anesthesia safety:

- Patient fasts before surgery (no food 6 hours, clear fluids up to 2 hours) to prevent vomiting and aspiration.
- An assistant dedicated to anesthesia watches breathing, pulse, color and pupils.
- Suction ready at the head end.
- Jaw thrust or barrier airway support.
- Endotracheal intubation: a curved metal or rigid tube passed into the trachea with a hand-forged laryngoscope blade and a light source (mirror reflecting a lamp). Allows ether delivered through a tube and protection from aspiration. Requires a skilled hand; essential for chest surgery (positive pressure ventilation with bellows through the tube keeps the lung inflated when the chest is open).

## Fluids and Blood

**Intravenous saline**

- 9 grams of clean salt per liter of boiled water gives normal saline (0.9%).
- Boiled in sealed glass flasks, cooled, given through a sterile hollow needle in a vein or a cutdown cannula (a vein exposed through a small incision and a tube tied in).
- Air in the line must be prevented. Barrier magic can serve as a one-way valve.
- Ringer's solution (adding small amounts of potassium and calcium salts) is better but harder to make pure.

**Oral rehydration** (for anyone who can swallow)

- 1 liter clean water, 6 level teaspoons sugar, half a teaspoon salt. Saves more lives than most surgery (cholera, dysentery, postoperative dehydration).

**Rectal fluids**: slow instillation of saline through a rectal tube absorbs well (proctoclysis, used historically).

**Blood transfusion**

1. **Crossmatch** before every transfusion: let a sample of recipient blood clot; take the clear serum. Mix a drop with a drop of donor blood (washed red cells in saline are better) on a clean glass. Wait several minutes, rock gently. Visible clumping means incompatible.
2. Over time, collected sera that clump certain cells but not others allow sorting the population into the four ABO groups. Group O donors become universal red cell donors (with care).
3. **Anticoagulant**: sodium citrate. Citric acid from lemon or lime juice (concentrated and crystallized) neutralized with washing soda (sodium carbonate from plant ash). About 0.2 to 0.4% citrate in the collected blood prevents clotting (Lewisohn 1915).
4. Collect donor blood by gravity into a boiled glass vessel containing citrate. Give within hours. Without refrigeration storage is short; cool cellar or snow helps.
5. Give slowly at first; stop at any fever, back pain, flushing or dark urine.

**Autotransfusion** with suction (see Magic / Suction above) is the safest blood source: the patient's own blood needs no crossmatch.

## Pain and Recovery

- Opium for postoperative pain.
- Willow bark tea (salicin) for mild pain and fever after the first days. Avoid it before surgery (bleeding).
- Early walking prevents clots and pneumonia.
- Deep breathing exercises and coughing (hug a pillow against the wound).
- Good nutrition: broth, eggs, meat, honey.
- Leg elevation, movement.

## Diagnostic Substitutes for Imaging and Labs

- Detailed history and physical exam (palpation, percussion, auscultation with a wooden stethoscope).
- Transillumination (light through scrotum, skull of infants, sinuses).
- Needle aspiration to sample fluid (pus, blood, bile, cyst fluid).
- Urine examination: color, smell, taste of sugar (diabetes), boiling with vinegar for protein (kidney disease).
- A simple microscope (Leeuwenhoek-style single ball lens) for pus, blood cells, parasites, hydatid hooklets.
- Pulse, temperature, blood pressure charting.
- Fecal blood test with guaiac resin (New World) or simple observation.
