# Blunt Tissue Resection (Blunt Dissection)

Design loot. Setting assumptions: `MedievalSurgery.md`. Liver application: `LiverSurgery.md`. Hub: `Index.md`.

## Definition

Tissue separation falls into two families:

| Type | Instrument | Action | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sharp dissection | Scalpel, scissors cutting | Divides everything in the path equally | Clean edges; cuts vessels, nerves and ducts with no discrimination |
| Blunt dissection | Fingers, closed instrument tips, sponge sticks, peanut swabs, elevators, suction tips | Pushes, spreads, sweeps or crushes | Tissue tears along its weakest planes; strong tubular structures survive and are exposed |

Blunt tissue resection means removing tissue (or opening a path through it) mainly by tearing, crushing and spreading rather than cutting. The surgeon lets the tissue's own mechanical weaknesses choose the line of separation.

## Why It Works: Tissue Mechanics

Different tissues fail at very different forces:

- **Loose areolar connective tissue** (the "fluff" between organs, muscles and fascial layers) has sparse, randomly arranged collagen fibers with lots of ground substance. It tears with very little force. This is what fills natural surgical planes.
- **Arteries** have thick muscular walls and collagen-rich adventitia. They resist tearing and slide away from a pushing instrument.
- **Veins** are thinner and more fragile than arteries. They resist areolar-level force but tear under rough handling. Most blunt dissection disasters are torn veins.
- **Nerves** are wrapped in epineurium and resist tearing well. They tolerate pushing but not prolonged stretching (stretch injury causes palsy even with no visible damage).
- **Ducts** (bile ducts, ureters) have fibrous walls and survive blunt force well.
- **Parenchyma** of solid organs (liver, spleen, kidney cortex) varies. Liver and spleen are extremely friable: the cells are packed with almost no supporting stroma, so they crumble under a clamp.

The principle: **apply a force strong enough to break weak tissue but too weak to break strong tubular structures.** What remains intact after the force is applied is exactly what needs to be tied.

## Instruments and Movements

| Instrument | Medieval build | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Fingers | Available | The best blunt dissector. Tactile feedback identifies pulsing arteries, firm ducts and tumor edges. |
| Hemostat (Kelly or Crile clamp) | Forged steel with a ratchet lock; a competent smith can make these | Push the closed tip into tissue then open it ("push and spread"); crush tissue between jaws |
| Scissors (Metzenbaum style) | Forged and ground steel | Closed tips pushed in then spread open, separating planes without cutting |
| Kittner / peanut | A small tight ball of linen held in a clamp | Gentle sweeping of fine planes around vessels |
| Sponge stick | Folded boiled linen in a ring clamp | Broad sweeping and pressure |
| Scalpel handle | Back end of any knife | Push dissection in firm planes |
| Periosteal elevator | Flat steel blade, dull edge | Strips periosteum and muscle off bone |
| Suction tip | Rigid metal tube | Pushes tissue while removing blood; "suction dissection" in liver and brain |

Core movements:

1. **Push-spread**: insert closed tips parallel to the expected vessels, open the tips. Tissue parts along fiber direction.
2. **Sweep**: finger or swab moved along a plane, peeling one layer off another.
3. **Pinch / fracture**: tissue squeezed between thumb and finger (or clamp jaws) until parenchyma crumbles.
4. **Traction-countertraction**: an assistant (or barrier) pulls one side while the surgeon pulls the other. Tension makes the plane visible as a white web of areolar strands.

Rules:

- Always dissect parallel to the expected course of vessels and nerves, never across them.
- Spread in small bites (a few millimeters at a time) near important structures.
- Any band that does not tear easily is a vessel, duct or nerve until proven otherwise. Tie it or identify it before dividing it.

## Advantages and Risks

Advantages:

- Preserves vessels and nerves by default.
- Follows embryological planes that are naturally bloodless.
- Fast in good planes.
- Needs only simple instruments.

Risks:

- Avulsion of small branches off a larger vessel (the branch tears out of the parent vessel, leaving a hole in the main trunk).
- Tearing thin veins.
- Ragged raw surfaces that ooze.
- Entering the wrong plane in scarred or inflamed tissue, where blunt force can tear bowel or bladder.
