## Anatomy Relevant to Resection

**Basic facts**

- Weight about 1.2 to 1.6 kg in an adult.
- Blood flow about 1.5 liters per minute, roughly a quarter of cardiac output.
- Dual supply: portal vein delivers about 75% of the flow; hepatic artery delivers about 25% of the flow but about half of the oxygen.
- Drainage: three main hepatic veins (right, middle, left) plus several short hepatic veins straight into the inferior vena cava (IVC) behind the liver.
- Covered by Glisson's capsule, a thin fibrous coat. Under the capsule the tissue is soft and friable.

**Portal triad (Glissonian pedicle)**

The portal vein, hepatic artery and bile duct travel together inside a fibrous sheath (an extension of Glisson's capsule) from the hilum into every segment. This sheath is what makes blunt liver resection possible: it is tough while the surrounding parenchyma is weak.

**Couinaud segments**

| Segment | Location | Main drainage |
|---|---|---|
| I | Caudate lobe, wrapped around the IVC | Directly into IVC |
| II | Left lateral, upper | Left hepatic vein |
| III | Left lateral, lower | Left hepatic vein |
| IVa / IVb | Left medial (quadrate area), upper and lower | Middle hepatic vein |
| V | Right anterior, lower | Middle and right hepatic veins |
| VI | Right posterior, lower | Right hepatic vein |
| VII | Right posterior, upper | Right hepatic vein |
| VIII | Right anterior, upper | Middle and right hepatic veins |

Each segment has its own portal pedicle. A segment can be removed without starving the others if its pedicle is tied and the transection follows segment boundaries.

**Surface landmarks**

- **Cantlie's line**: an imaginary line from the gallbladder fossa to the IVC. It marks the plane of the middle hepatic vein and divides the functional right liver from the functional left liver. This is NOT the falciform ligament.
- **Falciform ligament / umbilical fissure**: divides the left lateral section (II, III) from segment IV.
- **Ligaments holding the liver**: round ligament (ligamentum teres, the remnant umbilical vein), falciform, left and right triangular, coronary ligaments, lesser omentum (hepatogastric and hepatoduodenal ligaments).

## Why Blunt Technique Suits the Liver

A sharp knife cut through liver opens every vessel in the path at once. Dozens of portal and hepatic vein branches bleed simultaneously from a raw surface and the cut ends retract into soft parenchyma where they are hard to grab.

Blunt technique reverses this:

1. Crushing or pinching destroys the soft hepatocyte plates.
2. The fibrous portal sheaths and hepatic vein walls survive the force.
3. These survivors are left standing as small "bridges" across the transection gap.
4. Each bridge is seen, tied (or clipped or sealed) and only then divided.

The liver is effectively dissected vessel by vessel. Bleeding comes from small things that were missed, not from everything at once.

## Techniques of Parenchymal Transection

### Finger fracture (digitoclasia)

Popularized by Tien-Yu Lin in 1958.

1. Score Glisson's capsule along the planned line with a knife (capsule is tough and will not fracture cleanly).
2. Pinch the parenchyma between thumb and index finger in small bites, or push with the fingertip.
3. Hepatocytes crumble. Vessels and ducts remain as strands in the gap.
4. Tie or clip each strand on the side that stays, then divide.
5. Continue bite by bite until the specimen is free.

Strengths: needs nothing but hands; fast; good tactile feedback.
Weaknesses: coarse; tears small hepatic vein branches more often than a clamp; more blood loss than clamp crushing.

### Clamp-crush (Kellyclasia)

The standard against which modern devices are still measured.

1. Score the capsule.
2. Place a Kelly clamp across about 5 mm of parenchyma, parallel to the transection line.
3. Close the clamp fully. The parenchyma is crushed flat; vessels remain.
4. Open the clamp, suction the debris and blood.
5. Identify exposed structures. Small ones (under about 2 to 3 mm) can be sealed with cautery or tied. Larger ones are tied with silk on the remnant side, then divided.
6. Repeat in small bites.

Strengths: controlled; cheap; low blood loss when combined with inflow occlusion and low venous pressure.
Weaknesses: slow in fibrotic (cirrhotic) liver because the parenchyma resists crushing.

### Suction dissection

The suction tip itself is used as a blunt instrument. It is drawn along the transection line, sucking and scraping away parenchyma while keeping the field dry. Vessels resist and remain. This mimics the modern ultrasonic aspirator (CUSA), which liquefies parenchyma with ultrasound and sucks it away while sparing vessels.

With magic suction, this is the most precise option available to the medieval surgeon: suction tip in one hand, fine clamp or tie in the other.

### Modern devices (for reference) and medieval substitutes

| Modern | Function | Substitute |
|---|---|---|
| CUSA (ultrasonic aspirator) | Fragments and removes parenchyma, spares vessels | Suction tip dissection |
| Water-jet dissector | High-pressure fluid washes away parenchyma | Possibly a fine barrier jet if barrier magic can propel fluid |
| Bipolar sealing / harmonic scalpel | Seals vessels up to about 7 mm | Silk ties; magic knife if it seals; hot iron (crude) |
| Staplers | Divide and seal large pedicles and hepatic veins | Suture ligation; transfixion sutures; barrier clamp |
| Saline-linked radiofrequency | Precoagulates the line | Not available |
| Argon beam / topical hemostats | Seals raw surface | Pressure, omentum, barrier pressure, cautery iron |

## Inflow Control

### Pringle maneuver

Described by James Hogarth Pringle in 1908.

- A finger is passed through the epiploic foramen (foramen of Winslow) behind the hepatoduodenal ligament.
- The ligament (containing portal vein, hepatic artery, common bile duct) is compressed with fingers, a soft clamp or a tape tourniquet.
- This stops all inflow to the liver.
- Normal liver tolerates intermittent clamping: about 15 minutes on, 5 minutes off, for a long total. Continuous clamping up to roughly 60 minutes is tolerated by healthy liver. Cirrhotic liver tolerates much less.
- Bleeding that continues during a Pringle comes from the hepatic veins (backflow) or from an aberrant artery (a replaced left hepatic artery in the lesser omentum, from the left gastric artery).

**Barrier magic version**: a small barrier plane compressing the hepatoduodenal ligament acts as a perfect atraumatic clamp with adjustable pressure, no instrument in the field and instant release.

### Selective inflow control

Instead of clamping everything, the surgeon dissects into the hilum and ties only the portal branch and arterial branch to the part being removed. The part darkens (demarcation line), showing exactly where to transect. This is the basis of anatomic resection.

### Glissonian pedicle approach

The whole sheath (artery, vein, duct together) to a segment or section is encircled with a finger or clamp from outside the liver, without opening the sheath and tied en bloc. Fast and suits blunt technique.

## Outflow Control and Venous Pressure

Back-bleeding from hepatic veins is the main bleeding source once inflow is clamped. Hepatic veins have no valves and connect straight to the IVC.

**Low central venous pressure (low CVP)**

- Keep venous pressure low (modern target below about 5 mmHg) during transection.
- Methods: restrict fluids before and during transection, deepen anesthesia, tilt the patient slightly head-down to protect against air embolism while keeping venous filling low.
- After the specimen is out, give fluid back freely.

**Air embolism**: open hepatic veins under low pressure can suck air into the circulation. Signs: sudden drop in blood pressure, gasping, cyanosis, a "mill-wheel" churning sound over the heart. Response: flood the field with saline, press the opening closed, tilt head-down and left side down, attempt aspiration if a central line exists.

**Barrier magic version**: a barrier film laid over each hepatic vein opening the instant it appears prevents both bleeding and air entry. This removes one of the largest dangers of liver surgery.

**Total vascular exclusion**: clamping inflow plus IVC above and below the liver. Without a way to support the circulation this crashes the blood pressure in many patients. A medieval surgeon should avoid it except in extremis.

## Step by Step: Left Lateral Sectionectomy (Segments II and III)

The easiest anatomic liver resection. Good choice for tumors, hydatid cysts or trauma confined to the left lateral section.

1. **Position and anesthesia**: supine, arms out. Deep anesthesia (ether). Large-bore access for fluids.
2. **Incision**: upper midline from xiphoid to just above the umbilicus, or a left subcostal incision.
3. **Exploration**: inspect the whole liver and abdomen. Palpate both lobes bimanually for other lesions.
4. **Mobilization**:
   - Tie and divide the round ligament; use it as a handle.
   - Divide the falciform ligament up toward the diaphragm.
   - Divide the left triangular ligament (contains small vessels and sometimes a bile duct; tie it).
   - Divide the left coronary ligament toward the left hepatic vein, stopping before reaching it.
   - Open the lesser omentum; check for an aberrant left hepatic artery and tie it if the section is coming out.
5. **Inflow control**: encircle the hepatoduodenal ligament ready for a Pringle (tape or barrier).
6. **Mark the line**: just left of the falciform ligament and umbilical fissure. Score the capsule with the knife.
7. **Transect**: clamp-crush or suction dissection from the lower edge upward. Tie each bridging structure.
8. **Pedicles**: the portal pedicles to segments III and II emerge from the umbilical portion of the left portal vein. Tie them inside the parenchyma on the left of the fissure, so that segment IV pedicles are spared. Use transfixion suture ligatures (a stitch through the pedicle, then tied around it, so it cannot slip off).
9. **Left hepatic vein**: last structure. Clamp it inside the parenchyma or just outside, divide, oversew the stump with a running silk suture. If the middle hepatic vein joins it as a common trunk, tie only the left branch.
10. **Release Pringle**. Check the raw surface.
11. **Hemostasis**: suture ligate any spurting point. Press warm moist linen on the surface for several minutes. Cautery iron or barrier pressure for ooze.
12. **Bile leak test**: press clean white linen onto the raw surface. Yellow-green staining marks a leaking duct; suture it.
13. **Drain**: optional; a boiled drain such as a waxed linen wick or a metal or glass tube brought out through a separate stab wound. Removed in a few days if no bile.
14. **Close** the fascia with strong silk or linen; skin with silk.

## Step by Step Outline: Right Hepatectomy (Segments V to VIII)

Major operation. Removes about 60 to 65% of the liver. Survivable only if the remaining left liver is healthy.

1. Long right subcostal incision, extended left or up the midline (inverted L or "Mercedes" style).
2. Fully mobilize the right liver off the diaphragm: divide right triangular and coronary ligaments; lift the right lobe forward.
3. Dissect off the IVC from below upward, tying each short hepatic vein (there may be ten or more; some are large). Divide the hepatocaval ligament.
4. Remove the gallbladder (it sits on the transection line).
5. Hilar dissection: identify and tie the right hepatic artery and right portal vein. Tie the right hepatic duct inside the liver later to avoid injuring the left duct. Or use the Glissonian approach for the whole right pedicle.
6. The right liver turns dark: demarcation follows Cantlie's line.
7. Encircle the right hepatic vein outside the liver; tie or clamp it now or at the end.
8. Low CVP; Pringle ready.
9. Transect along the demarcation line, keeping just to the right of the middle hepatic vein. Tie the segment V and VIII branches draining into the middle hepatic vein.
10. Divide the right hepatic vein, oversew both ends.
11. Hemostasis, bile check, drain, close.

Main dangers: hemorrhage from the IVC or short hepatic veins, bile leak, injury to the left bile duct, liver failure from too small a remnant.

## Wedge (Non-anatomic) Resection

For small superficial lesions at the edge of the liver.

1. Place stay sutures (deep mattress stitches) around the area to be removed, or use a barrier to compress the margin.
2. Crush-clamp or finger-fracture through the parenchyma in a V shape with about 1 cm margin around the lesion.
3. Tie bridging vessels.
4. Close the raw surface with large blunt-needle mattress sutures through the capsule, or pack omentum into the defect.

## Liver Trauma

**Injury grades (AAST, simplified)**

| Grade | Injury |
|---|---|
| I | Small capsular tear or small subcapsular hematoma |
| II | Laceration 1 to 3 cm deep |
| III | Laceration over 3 cm deep or large expanding hematoma |
| IV | Disruption of 25 to 75% of a lobe |
| V | Disruption over 75% of a lobe, or juxtahepatic venous injury (retrohepatic IVC, main hepatic veins) |
| VI | Hepatic avulsion |

**Approach**

1. Open the abdomen fast with a long midline incision.
2. Pack all four quadrants with linen to tamponade, then remove packs one quadrant at a time.
3. **Manual compression**: squeeze the liver together with both hands to close the fracture.
4. **Pringle**: if bleeding stops, the source is portal or arterial. If it continues, suspect hepatic vein or IVC.
5. **Simple tears**: hepatorrhaphy. Large curved blunt needle with silk or catgut; deep horizontal mattress sutures through the capsule on either side, tied gently so they do not cut through. A pad of omentum or an absorbable bolster under the ties helps.
6. **Deep bleeding fractures**: finger-fracture the wound open to expose the bleeding vessel at its base and tie it directly (Pachter's technique). This is better than blindly sewing a cavity closed over a bleeding vessel, which causes hematoma and abscess.
7. **Devitalized tissue**: resectional debridement, removing dead liver along the fracture with finger fracture rather than a formal anatomic resection.
8. **Omental pedicle packing**: a tongue of greater omentum with its blood supply intact is stuffed into the cavity to fill dead space and promote clotting.
9. **Damage control (perihepatic packing)**: if the patient is cold, bleeding diffusely and failing, compress the liver with linen packs above, below and in front (not stuffed into the wound), close the abdomen temporarily and stop. Rewarm, resuscitate, return in 24 to 48 hours to remove packs.

**Retrohepatic IVC / hepatic vein injury (Grade V)**

This is among the deadliest injuries in surgery. Modern options include the atriocaval shunt (Schrock 1968) or venovenous bypass. Medieval surgeon without barrier magic: packing only, high death rate.

**Barrier magic changes this.** A barrier sleeve placed around the torn vena cava from inside or a barrier patch pressed over the hole while blood flow continues in the vessel is effectively a magical shunt. The surgeon can then sew the defect under the barrier, shrinking it as the repair advances.

## Hydatid Cyst of the Liver

Common wherever sheep, dogs and people live together (much of medieval Europe and the Middle East). Caused by *Echinococcus granulosus*.

1. Expose the cyst. Surround it with pads soaked in a scolicidal agent: hypertonic saline (about 15 to 20% salt solution). Spillage of cyst fluid can seed new cysts throughout the abdomen and cause anaphylaxis.
2. Aspirate the clear fluid through a large needle connected to suction.
3. Inject hypertonic saline into the cavity, wait about 10 to 15 minutes, aspirate again.
4. Open the cyst and remove the white laminated membrane and daughter cysts with suction and spoons.
5. Inspect the cavity for bile staining (communication with a bile duct). Suture any opening. Do not use hypertonic saline if bile is present in the fluid: it damages bile ducts (sclerosing cholangitis).
6. Either remove the fibrous pericyst wall (pericystectomy, blunt dissection along the plane between pericyst and liver) or fill the cavity with omentum (omentoplasty).

**Barrier magic**: a barrier dome sealed around the cyst before puncture makes spillage impossible. This turns a risky operation into a safe one.

## Liver Abscess

- **Pyogenic** (from bowel infection, appendicitis, biliary infection): open or needle drainage through the shortest route, irrigation, drain left in place. Without antibiotics, drainage alone is the key treatment and must be complete.
- **Amoebic** (from *Entamoeba histolytica*, often after dysentery): modern treatment is drugs. Medieval: repeated needle aspiration of the "anchovy paste" pus. Emetine (from ipecac root, a New World plant) treats amoebiasis if available through trade.
- Approach through the bed of the abscess where it touches the abdominal wall, or below the ribs, avoiding the pleural cavity.

## Limits of Resection and Regeneration

- **Future liver remnant (FLR)**: at least about 20 to 25% of total liver must remain if the liver is healthy. At least about 30% if fatty or damaged; about 40% if cirrhotic.
- Without imaging, estimate by segment count: removing the left lateral section (II, III) takes about 15 to 20%; left hepatectomy about 35%; right hepatectomy about 60 to 65%; extended right (trisectionectomy, IV to VIII) about 75 to 80%.
- **Regeneration**: hepatocytes reenter the cell cycle within a day. Most lost volume returns within about 4 to 8 weeks. Function recovers before volume.
- Cirrhotic livers regenerate poorly. Heavy drinkers (common in an alcohol-rich medieval world) are poor candidates for major resection. Signs of cirrhosis on the table: firm, nodular, shrunken liver; enlarged veins around the stomach and umbilicus.

## Postoperative Care and Complications

| Problem | Signs | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Bleeding | Falling pressure, rising pulse, pale, blood in drain | Return to surgery; packing |
| Bile leak | Green or yellow fluid in drain, fever, pain | Keep drain in; most small leaks close; large ones need re-operation |
| Liver failure | Jaundice, confusion, easy bleeding, fluid in abdomen, low blood sugar | Glucose (honey water, sugar solution), rest, low protein diet, no hepatotoxic drugs |
| Hypoglycemia | Sweating, confusion, seizures | Frequent sugar by mouth or rectally; glucose IV if a sterile solution is available |
| Subphrenic abscess | Fever after day 5, hiccups, right shoulder pain | Drainage |
| Pleural effusion | Breathlessness, dullness at right base | Drain if large |
| Ascites | Swelling | Salt restriction |

Avoid chloroform as an anesthetic for liver patients: it is directly toxic to the liver. Ether is the better choice.
