# History of Explosives and Propellants

> Earth invent loot. Chemistry vs manufacture vs safe firearm use as three separate achievements; black powder to smokeless to high explosives; Renaissance / caveman-gun feasibility ladder. Not Caldris law and no manufacturing recipes. Powder chemistry, raw materials and era verdicts: `GunpowderFirearms.md`. Firearm performance: `../../../Combat/Firearms.md`. Caldris street baseline: `../../Tech/Technology.md`. Nav: `Index.md`.

The history of explosives is particularly useful for worldbuilding because the chemistry of an explosive, the ability to manufacture it, and the ability to use it safely in a firearm are three very different technological achievements.

A Renaissance alchemist might understand how to produce a violently energetic substance centuries before his civilization could reliably manufacture smokeless ammunition.

For a primitive or "caveman" gun, there are several possible technological paths, ranging from historical black powder to experimental 19th-century propellants.

---

## 1. Historical progression of explosives

| Period | Development | Historical significance |
|---|---|---|
| 9th-11th centuries | Black powder in China | The first known chemical explosive; eventually used in fire lances, bombs, rockets, and guns |
| 13th-15th centuries | Gunpowder spreads and improves | European and Asian guns become increasingly practical; grain consistency improves |
| 16th-18th centuries | Refined black powder | Better mills, more standardized manufacture, increasingly reliable firearms and artillery |
| Early 1800s | Percussion ignition | Chemical primers gradually replace flint and burning matches |
| 1840s | Guncotton and nitroglycerin | Energetic nitrate esters introduce an entirely different class of explosive chemistry |
| 1860s | Better guncotton processing; dynamite | More practical industrial blasting explosives |
| 1884 | Paul Vieille's Poudre B | First widely successful modern single-base smokeless military propellant |
| 1887-1889 | Ballistite and cordite | Double-base propellants introduce nitroglycerin alongside nitrocellulose |
| Late 1800s-WWI | Picric acid and TNT | High explosives become central to artillery shells and military engineering |
| 1930s-WWII | Triple-base propellants; wider RDX use | Specialized military explosives and propellants with increasingly engineered characteristics |

These milestones broadly follow the histories documented by the Royal Society of Chemistry, Science History Institute, and Nobel Foundation.

## 2. The basic chemistry

There are three especially important ways of producing an energetic material.

**A. Combine a fuel with an oxidizer.** Black powder belongs to this family. Rather than relying on oxygen from the surrounding air, it carries an oxidizing ingredient within the powder itself.

**B. Chemically modify a combustible molecule.** Nitrocellulose and nitroglycerin are nitrate esters. Their energetic chemistry comes from chemically attached nitrate ester groups, not simply from being soaked in acid or having oxidizer dust mixed with them.

**C. Manufacture an inherently explosive compound.** TNT, RDX, PETN, and similar materials store energy in their molecular structures and can undergo extraordinarily rapid decomposition under appropriate conditions.

Another distinction matters just as much:

- **Deflagration:** A chemical reaction propagated through burning. This is how ordinary gun propellants are intended to work.
- **Detonation:** A shock-driven chemical reaction that propagates supersonically through explosive material.

A firearm needs gas released at a rate that its barrel and chamber can withstand. It does not simply need the most energetic explosive available.

A more powerful chemical isn't automatically a better propellant. Indeed, that assumption is an excellent source of believable failures for a fictional inventor.

## 3. Black powder, the medieval and Renaissance solution

**Historical feasibility: Low-moderate difficulty.** Practical with medieval technology once the necessary materials and expertise are available.

Black powder is a mixture of three familiar substances:

| Substance | Chemical role | Historical source |
|---|---|---|
| Saltpeter (potassium nitrate) | Oxidizer | Nitrate-bearing earth, cave deposits, managed nitre beds |
| Charcoal | Principal fuel | Wood processed by charcoal burners |
| Sulfur | Combustible component that helps ignition and flame propagation | Naturally occurring sulfur deposits and mineral trade |

The critical chemistry is the distinction between a fuel and an oxidizer. Saltpeter allows the powder to react rapidly without needing to draw oxygen from the outside air.

Black powder produces expanding gases, heat, and considerable solid residue. That residue explains the heavy smoke and fouling of historical firearms.

The National Park Service documents both its historical ingredients and extensive saltpeter production at Mammoth Cave.

### Narrative example

The gunsmith's first problem was not finding something that burned. Charcoal burners supplied him with more fuel than he could use, and traders brought yellow sulfur from the mountains. His problem was saltpeter.

He purchased the pale crystals from collectors who worked nitrate-rich cave deposits. The supply was unreliable, expensive, and controlled by merchants who understood its military importance.

Eventually, the powder mill delivered its first batches. Some produced disappointing shots. Others burned more vigorously, leaving the weapon buried in smoke and black residue.

The gunsmith soon learned that the strength of a powder was not the only measure of its quality. A charge had to behave consistently from one shot to the next.

For worldbuilding: Black powder is the natural starting point for a primitive firearm. Its main difficulties are supply, consistency, moisture, storage, and the quality of the gun itself.

## 4. Guncotton, nitrocellulose

**Historical feasibility: High difficulty.** First developed in the 19th century, with major stability problems.

This is where the chemistry becomes dramatically different from black powder.

Cellulose is the structural carbohydrate found in cotton, linen, wood, and other plants. Its molecules contain numerous hydroxyl groups (-OH).

Nitrocellulose is produced by chemically converting some of those hydroxyl groups into nitrate ester groups (-ONO2).

Conceptual chemistry:

```
Cellulose -> Cellulose nitrate
Ordinary plant polymer -> Chemically modified energetic polymer
```

Historically, this chemistry involved nitric and sulfuric acids. Nitric acid provides the nitrate chemistry; sulfuric acid helps drive the transformation by controlling the chemical environment and water.

This is a chemical transformation of the cellulose molecule, not simply an absorption of liquid into cotton fibers.

The amount of nitrate ester substitution matters greatly. Nitrocellulose is actually a family of related materials, ranging from forms used in coatings and early plastics to highly energetic guncotton.

### Narrative example

The alchemist turned from mineral powders to the fibers of plants. Cotton, linen, and the fine pulp of wood all contained the same fundamental material: cellulose.

He believed that the recently discovered chemistry of powerful mineral acids could change the character of the fibers themselves.

His early experiments yielded material that still resembled ordinary cotton, yet behaved alarmingly differently around flame. Where untreated cotton smoldered and charred, the altered fibers flashed with extraordinary speed.

But the discovery created a new problem. Samples that initially appeared satisfactory deteriorated in storage. Some became discolored, developed an acrid odor, or turned dangerously unstable.

His achievement was not yet a practical gunpowder. He had discovered an energetic material without discovering how to manufacture a dependable propellant.

### Why this was historically difficult

The Royal Society of Chemistry documents serious 19th-century manufacturing accidents and the work of Frederick Abel, who greatly improved the stability of guncotton during the 1860s.

A Renaissance inventor with exceptionally advanced chemical knowledge could plausibly investigate nitrate esters. Consistently manufacturing military-quality nitrocellulose is another matter.

The main obstacles are chemical purity, residual instability, decomposition during storage, and the completely different pressure behavior of the material compared with ordinary black powder.

## 5. Nitroglycerin, an energetic liquid

**Historical feasibility: Very high difficulty.** Chemically related to guncotton, but markedly different in physical behavior.

Glycerin (glycerol) is a small organic molecule with three hydroxyl groups.

In nitrate ester chemistry, those groups can be converted into energetic nitrate ester groups. The resulting material is known historically as nitroglycerin, or more precisely glyceryl trinitrate.

Unlike nitrocellulose, which is a polymer, nitroglycerin is a relatively small molecule and is a liquid under ordinary conditions.

The important difference for a primitive gun is that nitroglycerin is not a useful replacement for loose black powder in an ordinary primitive gun.

Its energy content is impressive, but raw energy is not the same thing as controlled propulsive performance.

### Narrative example

The alchemist had already learned that vegetable fibers could be made extraordinarily energetic. He now investigated whether similar chemistry could transform an oil-like substance derived from the crafts of soapmakers.

The result was a liquid unlike the powders he knew. It appeared almost ordinary, yet its violent behavior made it profoundly unsuitable for casual handling.

He imagined that such a substance might give his weapon unprecedented power. His gunsmith disagreed. A firearm needed expanding gas delivered over a useful interval, not an uncontrolled destructive shock.

The inventor was forced to distinguish between an explosive capable of breaking stone and a propellant capable of accelerating a projectile.

### What about mixing it with clay or charcoal?

This is an important distinction.

Historical dynamite used an absorbent mineral material, diatomaceous earth, to hold nitroglycerin in a more manageable physical form. Nobel patented dynamite in 1867 (Nobel Foundation).

That did not turn it into ordinary gunpowder. Nor did the absorbent make its energetic chemistry disappear.

Charcoal, meanwhile, is an actual fuel in black powder, rather than a general-purpose chemical stabilizer.

For fiction, an alchemist who assumes any powder or earth can tame an energetic liquid would be making a scientifically believable mistake.

## 6. Smokeless powder, the real breakthrough

The breakthrough that transformed firearms was not merely the discovery of nitrocellulose.

It was learning to turn energetic materials into predictable solid propellants with controlled burning characteristics.

**Single-base smokeless powder**

Primarily nitrocellulose as its energetic ingredient. Its grains are engineered to burn in a controlled fashion rather than behaving like loose, untreated guncotton.

**Double-base smokeless powder**

Uses nitrocellulose and nitroglycerin as energetic ingredients. Examples include Nobel's ballistite and British cordite.

**Triple-base propellant**

Incorporates nitroguanidine alongside the double-base energetic components. Especially relevant to military artillery, where flame temperature and barrel erosion matter.

### What colloiding means

The word colloid in the historical discussion of smokeless powder refers to the conversion of energetic nitrocellulose into a cohesive, plastic-like material instead of a mass of loose fibers.

That physical transformation was crucial to the development of successful smokeless propellants.

Manufacturers could engineer the physical form of the final grains and their burning behavior, giving ammunition designers far more control over gas generation.

Modern propellants also incorporate specialized nonenergetic additives serving functions such as stabilization and control of burning behavior.

### Narrative example, single-base powder

The chemist abandoned the idea of using loose guncotton in his firearm.

He had come to understand that an energetic material's physical structure was nearly as important as its chemistry. His experiments turned toward producing a more uniform, cohesive substance rather than leaving it as separate cotton fibers.

He examined samples of varying shapes and densities, studying whether their behavior could ever be made sufficiently repeatable.

Months passed before he could even begin to distinguish between a material that burned impressively and one that might someday become a reliable propellant.

When the gunsmith examined the latest experimental ammunition, he refused to risk his oldest firearm. The powder was new, but the iron barrel was not.

### Narrative example, double-base powder

The next generation of the chemist's research joined two discoveries that had previously seemed incompatible: the fibrous energetic material obtained from cellulose and the powerful energetic liquid derived from glycerin.

The goal was no longer simply a powerful explosive. It was a manufactured material whose chemistry, mechanical strength, and combustion characteristics could be controlled together.

The resulting propellant offered extraordinary possibilities, but its energy and thermal behavior also demanded more from the gunmaker.

Historically, Nobel's ballistite appeared in 1887 and British cordite in 1889 (Nobel Foundation).

## 7. High explosives, an entirely different purpose

The development of high explosives and the development of firearm propellants overlapped historically, but they solved different problems.

| Material | Chemical family | Principal historical application |
|---|---|---|
| Nitroglycerin | Nitrate ester | Industrial blasting and energetic formulations |
| Dynamite | Explosive formulation | Mining, demolition, construction |
| Picric acid | Nitroaromatic compound | Explosive fillings for military shells |
| TNT | Nitroaromatic compound | Military shells, demolition charges |
| PETN | Nitrate ester | Specialized explosive applications |
| RDX | Nitramine | Military high explosives |
| HMX | Nitramine | Specialized high-performance explosives |

A chemist could discover one of these substances without having any practical method of using it in a gun.

TNT, for example, became successful as a shell filling because of its properties as a manufactured high explosive. It was not intended to serve as ordinary firearm powder.

An important correction to the earlier explanation: high explosives do not all consist of single molecules containing their own fuel and oxidizer. Some effective high-explosive formulations contain multiple substances.

Likewise, adding an absorbent or binder does not automatically change an explosive from a detonating material into a dependable deflagrating propellant.

Why TNT fails as a propellant despite matching double-base energy: `GunpowderFirearms.md`. Worked indestructible-gun numbers: `Indestructible357Magnum.md`.

## 8. What a Renaissance inventor could realistically accomplish

Difficulty considers chemical knowledge, supplies, equipment, reliability, and suitability for firearms, not merely whether a laboratory reaction is theoretically possible.

| Achievement | Difficulty | Plausibility |
|---|---|---|
| Obtain historical black powder | 1/5 | Routine where the trade exists |
| Establish dependable black-powder supplies | 2/5 | Very plausible with money and labor |
| Improve quality and consistency of period gunpowder | 3/5 | Plausible skilled specialist |
| Develop dependable primitive firearms | 3/5 | Historically demonstrated |
| Investigate primitive nitrate ester chemistry | 4/5 | Exceptional alchemist with unusual knowledge |
| Discover guncotton-like energetic material | 4/5 | Possible as an advanced fictional breakthrough |
| Produce reliable nitrocellulose propellant | 5/5 | Needs substantial advances in chemical industry |
| Produce reliable double-base smokeless powder | 5/5 | A major industrial undertaking |
| Establish modern military explosive production | 5/5 | Demands a large specialized chemical infrastructure |

There is no absolute rule that says a Renaissance civilization could never discover a 19th-century chemical.

What matters is the difference between discovering a reaction, isolating a substance, manufacturing it reliably, and using the resulting product safely.

A genius might leap ahead in chemical theory. He cannot easily leap ahead in precision instruments, metallurgy, trained labor, or industrial quality control.

## 9. Applying this to a caveman gun

For a primitive firearm, divide the technological progression into four stages.

**Stage I, Crude black-powder gun**

A basic firearm using established gunpowder technology. Smoke, fouling, inconsistent performance, and poor metallurgy are major problems.

**Stage II, Refined historical firearm**

Better workmanship, more consistent powder, and improved ignition. This stage gives substantial practical improvements without inventing an entirely new chemistry.

**Stage III, Experimental energetic chemistry**

Discovery of guncotton-like material and other energetic compounds. The character has advanced beyond his civilization's chemical knowledge, but his discoveries are not yet trustworthy firearms propellants.

**Stage IV, Industrial propellant manufacturing**

Reliable smokeless ammunition becomes possible only alongside better chemical manufacturing, standardization, and firearm engineering.

A particularly interesting possibility is that the character understands modern chemistry but must work with primitive equipment.

He might know exactly what nitrocellulose is, understand the function of nitric and sulfuric acids, and recognize the difference between a high explosive and a propellant.

That knowledge makes him extraordinarily advanced compared with a Renaissance alchemist. Yet it does not automatically make him capable of manufacturing safe cartridges.

The most believable source of tension is the gap between knowing the chemistry and having the technology to control it.

For his first functioning gun, historical black powder is entirely credible. For his first smokeless gun, the real achievement would be developing not just a new chemical but a repeatable manufacturing process and a firearm engineered for its pressure characteristics.
