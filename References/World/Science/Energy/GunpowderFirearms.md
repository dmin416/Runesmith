# Gunpowder and Firearms

Hub: `../Science.md`. Earth encyclopedia for invent beats. No manufacturing recipes. Caldris street baseline stays personal-firearms-niche (`../../Tech/Technology.md`). Non-powder mechanical launch: `Projectiles.md`. Recoil: `Kinetic.md`.

## Powder Options by Era

### Medieval Tech Base

- **Refined black powder:** Medieval powder was weak and inconsistent mainly because of impure saltpeter and poor granulation. With full knowledge it matches 19th century quality. It still produces heavy white smoke. Roughly half its mass becomes solid residue that absorbs moisture and corrodes iron.
- **Sulfurless black powder:** A real historical variant. It smells less sulfurous and leaves less corrosive fouling. It is harder to ignite and slightly weaker.
- **Smokeless:** Alchemists had strong acids in small quantities, so nitrocellulose is chemically possible. The real barriers are consistent quality and long-term stability. Unstable early guncotton caused deadly magazine explosions in the 1860s. Realistically a tiny elite supply.

### Victorian Tech Base

- **Single-base smokeless (nitrocellulose only, like France's Poudre B in 1884):** The cleanest practical option. Almost no smoke, minimal fouling and less barrel wear. Nitrocellulose dates to 1846, so with full knowledge it could appear in the 1850s.
- **Double-base (adds nitroglycerin, like Ballistite and Cordite):** More energy and better humidity resistance. Burns hotter and erodes barrels faster.
- **Triple-base (adds nitroguanidine, first synthesized in 1877):** Cooler burn, low muzzle flash and the least barrel erosion. Historically a WWII development but within Victorian chemistry reach.
- **Primers:** Victorian mercury fulminate and potassium chlorate primers left corrosive salts and mercury residue. Lead-free non-corrosive priming chemistry was within reach, since a key compound was first made in 1858.

### Cleanest Theoretical Setup

Stabilized single-base or triple-base smokeless propellant with a lead-free non-corrosive primer and a jacketed or lead-free bullet.

- Combustion products are mostly nitrogen, water vapor, carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide.
- Carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides only matter in enclosed spaces.
- Lead from primers and bare lead bullets is the largest real health hazard in shooting. Removing lead matters more than the powder choice.

## Double-Base Powder with Indestructible Metal

Nitrocellulose plus nitroglycerin is double-base smokeless powder, the family that includes Ballistite and Cordite. Indestructible metal removes its main drawback, which is hot gas eroding the barrel. That makes double-base the strongest practical choice in this setting.

Nitrating the ingredients is only the first step. Three problems separate raw nitrated material from a usable propellant:

- **Stability:** Crude nitrocellulose and nitroglycerin carry leftover acid that makes them decompose over time. Without thorough purification and a stabilizer, stored powder can self-ignite.
- **Burn control:** Loose nitrocellulose and liquid nitroglycerin detonate rather than burn progressively. They have to be combined into a solid gel and formed into grains or cords of a set shape. Grain geometry controls gas production and turns an explosive into a propellant.
- **Consistency:** Batch-to-batch uniformity decides whether every shot performs the same.

Nitrocellulose existed in 1846 but usable smokeless powder did not arrive until 1884. These three problems account for most of that gap.

With indestructible metal, chamber pressure stops being limited by the barrel. Recoil, the shooter's body and the projectile surviving the pressure become the new limits.

## Raw Materials

| Material | Purpose | Medieval Source | Victorian Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cellulose | Base of nitrocellulose | Cotton, linen rags | Cotton, cotton linters, wood pulp |
| Strong nitric acid | Nitration | Small alchemical batches from saltpeter | Industrial chemical works |
| Strong sulfuric acid | Used with nitric acid in nitration | Small-scale distillation of green vitriol or burned sulfur | Industrial lead-chamber plants |
| Glycerin | Base of nitroglycerin | Soap-making byproduct (usually discarded) | Recovered and sold by soap makers |
| Clean water and mild alkali (chalk or soda) | Washing out leftover acid | Available | Available |
| Gelatinizing solvent | Combines ingredients into a solid gel | Alcohol easy; ether and acetone in small amounts | Ether and alcohol (Poudre B) or acetone (Cordite) |
| Stabilizer | Long-term storage safety | Refined petroleum or wax as a weaker substitute | Mineral jelly (petroleum jelly), available from the 1870s |

**Primer materials:** Victorian primers used mercury fulminate made from mercury, nitric acid and alcohol, often mixed with potassium chlorate. Both eras can source all of these.

**Sneeze test:** Properly washed and stabilized double-base powder is hard to set off by bumping or dropping. Raw nitroglycerin is the sensitive one. Gelling it into the nitrocellulose tames it.

**Era verdict:** Victorian is fully feasible at industrial scale. Medieval is feasible in workshop quantities. Acid volume and purity are the bottleneck, not the knowledge.

## TNT as Propellant

| Substance | Energy per kg |
|---|---|
| Black powder | ~3 MJ |
| Single-base smokeless | ~3.7 MJ |
| Double-base smokeless | ~4.5 to 5 MJ |
| TNT | ~4.2 to 4.6 MJ |
| Nitroglycerin | ~6.3 MJ |

TNT holds about the same energy as double-base powder. The difference is speed. Powder burns over milliseconds and pushes the bullet steadily. TNT detonates at about 6,900 m/s.

- .50 BMG chamber pressure is about 55,000 psi. TNT detonation pressure is about 2.7 million psi, roughly 50 times higher.
- In an indestructible gun, the shock crushes the bullet and sends it out as a spray of deformed fragments. Much of the energy is lost as shock and heat. Recoil arrives as a sharp hammer blow.
- TNT is hard to set off with a normal primer. It usually just burns or fizzles.
- The net result is a worse bullet than ordinary powder.

---

## More Powder

An indestructible barrel removes the pressure ceiling, but powder gas has a speed limit. Conventional propellant tops out around 5,500 to 6,500 fps, which is where modern tank sabot rounds sit.

Doubling the charge in a .50 gives roughly 25 to 35 percent more velocity, not double. A longer barrel adds more than extra powder. Light gas guns using compressed hydrogen reach 20,000+ fps but are lab equipment beyond the reach of any powder gun.

## Ned acid (powder feedstock invent)

Companion biology invent. Character peg: `../../../People/Ned.md`. Species body: `../../Fauna/NeedleWorm.md`.

Ned produces cellulose silk, glycerin from his hemolymph and acid made by eating his own extremely hard spikes.

### Strength vs Concentration

- **Strength:** How completely an acid gives up its hydrogen ions. The molecule fixes it.
- **Concentration:** How little water is mixed in.
- **What nitration cares about:** Concentration matters most. Nitrating a molecule releases water, and that water weakens the acid as it works. That is why nitric acid is paired with sulfuric acid. The sulfuric acid soaks up the water and keeps the nitric acid working.

### Acid Strength Scale

Below pH 0 chemists use the Hammett scale. Each step of 1 means 10 times stronger.

| Substance | Acidity |
|---|---|
| Vinegar | pH ~2.5 |
| Stomach acid | pH 1 to 2 |
| Car battery acid | Below pH 1 |
| Concentrated sulfuric acid | Hammett -12 |
| Fluoroantimonic acid (strongest known) | Hammett ~-28, about 10^16 times stronger than pure sulfuric acid |

### Too Weak, Right, Too Strong

- **Too wet or weak:** Cellulose only partly nitrates. The result is collodion, the low-energy nitrocellulose Victorians used for photography plates and liquid bandages. It burns but makes poor propellant.
- **Strong and dry:** Full nitration into guncotton, the propellant grade.
- **Superacid:** Stronger is not better. A superacid chars, dissolves or breaks down cellulose and glycerin instead of nitrating them. Ned's acid only helps if its extra power goes into nitrating rather than eating the raw material.

### Real Animal Precedents

- Ants spray formic acid.
- Vinegaroons (whip scorpions) spray acetic acid at about 85 percent strength.
- Tun snails secrete saliva containing sulfuric acid to break down the shells of their prey.
- Bombardier beetles keep chemicals in separate sacs and mix them in an armored reaction chamber to fire a boiling spray. This is a model for a gland that keeps reagents apart until use.
- The stomach makes hydrochloric acid with proton pumps and protects itself with a mucus lining.
- Tunicates (sea squirts) are the only animals known to make cellulose.
- Many cold-climate insects load their hemolymph with glycerol as antifreeze.

### How Ned Could Work

- **Spikes as feedstock:** Sulfuric acid needs sulfur. Nitric acid needs nitrogen. Ned's spikes can be mineralized stores of sulfur and nitrogen compounds that he grinds down and digests to rebuild as acid. Eating his own spikes recycles the elements instead of creating them from nothing. Spike hardness affects chewing and armor. Acid strength comes from the chemistry inside the gland.
- **Two glands or one:** Two glands, one nitric and one sulfuric, mirror the real mixed-acid setup. One magical gland producing a single acid that both nitrates and absorbs water replaces both.
- **Self-protection:** Acid sacs lined with chitin and mucus like a stomach, with a separate output duct like a bombardier beetle.
- **Water control:** A worm is mostly water. A gland that pumps out acid with almost no water in it is the most magical part of Ned and the part that decides whether his acid makes guncotton or collodion.

### Ned Acid Formula

**S** = Ned's acid nitrating power relative to normal strong mixed acid.

**Ned acid needed = Normal acid × p / S**

Example: 10x powder at normal power (p = 0.1) with Ned acid at S = 2 needs 5 percent of the normal acid.

### Ned Production Bottleneck

- **C, G and A** = Ned's daily output of cellulose, glycerin and acid, each as a fraction of what one normal batch needs.
- **Batches per day = the smallest of C, G and (A × S)**
- **Normal-power rounds per day = Batches per day × Normal rounds per batch × M**

Ned's output is capped by whichever of the three he makes least of relative to need. A higher energy multiplier M stretches every batch into M times as many normal-power rounds.
