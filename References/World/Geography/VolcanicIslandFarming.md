# Farming a Volcanic Island: Wind, Rain and Farm Locations

A guide to how wind and rain move around a volcanic island with a central peak and where farms fit best. Built around Hawaiian patterns with Koloa maoli and Nēnē flocks in mind.

## Part 1: How Wind Works on an Island

### Prevailing Wind by Latitude

The island's latitude sets its main wind direction.

| Latitude | Prevailing Wind | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0° to 30° (tropics) | Trade winds from the east (northeast in the north, southeast in the south) | Steady most of the year. Hawaii sits here. |
| 30° to 60° (temperate) | Westerlies from the west | Stormier, more variable, stronger winter storms. |
| 60° to 90° (polar) | Polar easterlies | Cold, harsh, poor farming. |
| Monsoon regions | Reverses by season | Wet season from one side, dry season from the other. |

For a Hawaii-style island, the trades blow from the northeast to east-northeast about 70 to 90 percent of the time, strongest in summer.

### Windward vs. Leeward

```
            TRADE WINDS  →→→→
                                  ▲ Summit
                                 /  \
        Clouds stack            /    \        Dry, sunny,
        and rain out  ☁☁☁     /      \       rain shadow
                     ☁☁☁    /        \
   WINDWARD (wet)  ~~~~~~~~/          \~~~~~~~~  LEEWARD (dry)
   Lush, rainy, eroded                          Calm, warm, sunny
```

- **Windward side:** Faces the wind. Moist ocean air is forced up the volcano, cools and drops its rain. Lush forests, deep valleys, constant clouds, streams that run year-round.
- **Leeward side:** Sheltered behind the mountain. Air descending the far side warms and dries out, creating a rain shadow. Sunny, warm, dry, often calm.
- **Rainfall gap:** On tall Hawaiian volcanoes the windward slopes can get 10 to 30 times the rain of the leeward coast only 30 to 50 miles away.

### Where the Rain Falls by Elevation

- **Tall volcano (over about 2,000 m):** Rain peaks on the windward slope around 600 to 1,200 m. Above about 2,000 to 2,400 m sits the trade wind inversion, a warm layer that caps the clouds. Above it the summit is dry, sunny and cold.
- **Short volcano (under about 2,000 m):** Clouds reach the summit, so rain peaks at or near the top. Kauai's Waiʻaleʻale is one of the wettest spots on Earth for this reason.
- **Cloud belt:** Mid-elevations stay wrapped in cloud. Trees comb water out of the fog, adding moisture even without rain.

### Daily Wind Cycles

On top of the trades, every island breathes on a daily cycle.

- **Day: sea breeze and upslope wind.** Land heats faster than water. Air rises off the warm slopes and draws ocean air inland and uphill.
- **Night: land breeze and downslope wind.** Slopes cool faster. Cold air slides down the mountain and out to sea.
- **Leeward effect:** On a sheltered leeward coast where trades are blocked, this cycle takes over. Morning sun gives way to afternoon clouds and showers as the sea breeze pushes moist air up the slope. Kona coffee depends on exactly this pattern.

### Wind Acceleration and Calm Zones

- **Corners and channels:** Wind speeds up as it squeezes around the island's ends and through channels between islands. Island tips are the windiest spots.
- **Saddles and gaps:** Low passes between two peaks funnel the trades into strong, steady gap winds.
- **Lee eddies:** Directly behind a large volcano, the air can swirl into calm zones or even reverse, sometimes blowing gently from the west.
- **Ridgetops:** Fully exposed. Strong, constant wind.
- **Valley floors:** Sheltered from side winds, but valleys aligned with the wind act as wind tunnels.

### Storm Winds

- **Kona storms:** Winter lows that flip the wind to come from the south or southwest. They dump heavy rain on the normally dry leeward side, which floods because its land isn't built for water.
- **Hurricanes:** Rare in Hawaii, usually approaching from the east or south. Destroy crops, trees and buildings island-wide.

### Temperature with Elevation

Air cools about 6.5°C per 1,000 m (about 3.5°F per 1,000 ft).

| Elevation | Climate | Example Crops |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 300 m | Hot, tropical | Coconut, taro, banana, sugarcane, breadfruit |
| 300 to 900 m | Warm, mild | Coffee, breadfruit, sweet potato, citrus |
| 900 to 1,800 m | Cool, temperate | Cabbage, onions, potatoes, lettuce, pasture |
| 1,800 m and up | Cold, dry, frost possible | Pasture at best, mostly barren near the summit |

## Part 2: Volcano Age Shapes the Land

The same volcano looks completely different depending on age. Pick the stage that fits the story.

| Stage | Landscape | Farming |
|---|---|---|
| **Young / active (Big Island)** | Smooth shield slopes, fresh lava fields, few valleys, active rift zones | Thin rocky soil. Fertile ash pockets. Lava risk. |
| **Mature (Maui)** | Valleys starting to cut in, some soil depth, dormant summit | Good soils, mixed terrain, low lava risk. |
| **Old / eroded (Kauai, Oʻahu)** | Deep amphitheater valleys, sheer cliffs, broad coastal plains, reefs | Deep red clay, abundant water, no lava risk. |
| **Very old (atolls)** | Volcano sunk away, only a reef ring left | Sand, salt, coconut and little else. |

### Lava Hazard (Active Volcanoes)

- **Rift zones:** Cracks running from the summit down the flanks. Eruptions break out along them and lava flows downhill from there.
- **Safest ground:** Upslope of or far to the side of rift zones, on older flows, or on high ridges that divert flows.
- **Highest risk:** Directly downslope of the summit or rift zones.
- **Kīpuka:** Islands of older forest and soil left untouched when newer lava flowed around them. Fertile, naturally walled, but sitting in a risk zone.
- **Vog:** Volcanic smog drifts downwind of the vents, which on a trade wind island means the leeward and southern sides. It damages leaves, rusts metal and irritates lungs.

### Water Underground

- **Basal springs:** Rainwater soaks through porous lava and seeps out at sea level as freshwater springs along the coast, even on the dry leeward side.
- **Dike water:** Old volcanic dikes trap water inside the mountain. Tunnels or natural springs tap it at mid-elevations.
- **Streams:** Plentiful and permanent on old windward sides. Rare or seasonal on young or leeward sides, where rain sinks straight into the lava.

## Part 3: Terrain Types and Considerations

### Valleys
- **Pros:** Deep alluvial soil, reliable stream water, shelter from crosswinds and salt, ideal for flooded taro.
- **Cons:** Flash floods, shorter sun hours in narrow valleys, cold air pooling at night (frost pockets at high elevation), mosquitoes in still water.
- **Best for:** Loʻi taro, Koloa flocks, bananas along streambanks.

### Hills and Ridges
- **Pros:** Good drainage, full sun, no flooding, good views for defense and lookout.
- **Cons:** Constant wind, erosion, thin soil, no surface water.
- **Best for:** Windbreak trees, lookout posts, pasture on gentle ridges, drought-tolerant crops.

### Slopes
- **Pros:** Cold air drains away instead of pooling, good sun when facing the right way, terraces hold soil and water.
- **Cons:** Erosion without terracing, hard work to build and maintain.
- **Aspect matters:** Leeward-facing slopes are sheltered and sunny. Windward-facing slopes are wet and windy. In the northern hemisphere, south-facing slopes get more sun.
- **Best for:** Terraced dryland crops, coffee, sweet potato, orchards, Nēnē grazing.

### Coastal Plains
- **Pros:** Flat, warm, easy to work, close to fishing and the sea.
- **Cons:** Salt spray, storm surge, tsunami zone, dry on the leeward side.
- **Best for:** Coconut, sweet potato, salt-tolerant crops, fishponds, salt pans.

### Plateaus and Saddles
- **Pros:** Broad, open, flat high ground. Cool climate.
- **Cons:** Gap winds, cold nights, dry above the cloud belt.
- **Best for:** Pasture, temperate crops, Nēnē country.

### Cinder Cones (Puʻu)
- Small hills dotting a volcano's flanks.
- Their leeward sides form natural wind shelters. Their bases often collect deeper soil.
- **Best for:** Sheltered garden plots tucked on the downwind side.

## Part 4: Single Farm Location Options

### 1. Windward Valley Floor
- **Climate:** Wet, warm, sheltered, cloudy.
- **Crops:** Loʻi taro, bananas, ti, sugarcane along the banks.
- **Birds:** Prime Koloa ground. Flooded fields full of snails and insects.
- **Risks:** Flash floods, mosquitoes, botulism in still water, short sun hours.
- **Rating:** Excellent for staple food and ducks.

### 2. Windward Lower Slopes
- **Climate:** Heavy rain, steady wind, warm.
- **Crops:** Breadfruit, banana, mountain apple, rain-fed taro, agroforestry.
- **Birds:** Koloa in wet pockets. Nēnē on cleared grassy patches.
- **Risks:** Nutrient leaching, erosion, salt near the coast, landslides.
- **Rating:** Good with windbreaks and constant mulching.

### 3. Leeward Mid-Slope Cloud Belt (Kona Style)
- **Climate:** Sunny mornings, afternoon showers from the sea breeze, calm, mild.
- **Crops:** Coffee, breadfruit, sweet potato, dryland taro, avocado, citrus.
- **Birds:** Nēnē grazing orchard rows and field edges.
- **Risks:** Vog downwind of an active vent. Drier years can stress crops.
- **Rating:** One of the best all-round zones. Reliable rain without storms or floods.

### 4. Leeward Coastal Plain
- **Climate:** Hot, dry, sunny, calm.
- **Crops:** Coconut, sweet potato, gourds, kiawe-style hardwoods, salt pans.
- **Birds:** Koloa at spring-fed ponds and fishpond edges.
- **Risks:** Drought, salt, tsunami zone, Kona storm flooding.
- **Rating:** Good with basal springs or irrigation. Poor without water.

### 5. Coastal Wetland or Estuary
- **Climate:** Warm, wet, flat, where streams meet the sea.
- **Crops:** Taro, fishponds, salt-tolerant reeds and sedges.
- **Birds:** Natural Koloa habitat. Ideal duck country.
- **Risks:** Storm surge, tsunami, brackish water creeping inland.
- **Rating:** Excellent for combined fish, taro and duck farming.

### 6. Upland Cool Zone
- **Climate:** Cool, misty, mild sun.
- **Crops:** Cabbage, onions, potatoes, carrots, lettuce, temperate fruit.
- **Birds:** Nēnē on pasture. Too cool and dry for heavy Koloa use.
- **Risks:** Cold nights, occasional frost at the highest farms, fog.
- **Rating:** Excellent for crops the lowlands can't grow.

### 7. High Saddle or Plateau
- **Climate:** Cool, windy, dry above the cloud belt.
- **Crops:** Pasture, hardy grains or roots.
- **Birds:** Nēnē heartland. Wide grazing range.
- **Risks:** Gap winds, cold, little water, exposure.
- **Rating:** Good for grazing only.

### 8. Kīpuka Pocket
- **Climate:** Depends on elevation and side. Naturally ringed by lava rock.
- **Crops:** Anything suited to its zone. Old soil makes it fertile.
- **Birds:** Easy to contain flocks thanks to the natural rock walls.
- **Risks:** Sits in an active lava zone. Isolated.
- **Rating:** Excellent for a hidden or fortified farm. Dramatic story setting.

### 9. Terraced Amphitheater Valley Head
- **Climate:** Very wet, enclosed, waterfalls everywhere.
- **Crops:** Stepped loʻi terraces fed by waterfalls.
- **Birds:** Koloa working each terrace level.
- **Risks:** Landslides, rockfall, floods, very limited sun.
- **Rating:** Beautiful and defensible but hard to work.

### 10. Leeward Cinder Cone Shelter
- **Climate:** Sunny and dry with a wind shadow behind the cone.
- **Crops:** Vegetables, melons, herbs in sheltered beds.
- **Birds:** Small Nēnē group kept away from the vegetables.
- **Risks:** Limited water. Small area.
- **Rating:** Good as a protected garden plot.

## Part 5: Combined Farm Systems

### A. The Ahupuaʻa (Mountain-to-Sea Strip)
The traditional Hawaiian model. A wedge-shaped land division running from the summit to the reef.
- **Uplands:** Forest for timber, birds and water protection.
- **Mid-slopes:** Dryland crops and orchards.
- **Valley floor:** Loʻi taro fed by the stream.
- **Coast:** Fishponds, salt, coconut and reef fishing.
- **Birds:** Nēnē on the upper grassland and orchards. Koloa in the loʻi and fishpond edges.
- **Strengths:** Self-sufficient. Water flows through every zone and returns to the sea. Every household gets a share of every resource.
- **Story value:** Ready-made social structure with chiefs, stewards and commoners sharing one strip.

### B. Valley and Ridge Pair
- **Valley floor:** Loʻi taro and Koloa.
- **Ridges above:** Windbreak trees, lookout posts and Nēnē pasture.
- **Strengths:** Water below, sun and wind protection above. Ridges guard the valley.
- **Weakness:** Steep climbs between zones.

### C. Twin Coast
- **Windward holdings:** Taro, bananas and Koloa in the wet valleys.
- **Leeward holdings:** Sweet potato, coconut and salt on the dry coast.
- **Strengths:** If one side suffers drought or flood, the other keeps producing. Trade between the two halves.
- **Weakness:** Travel around or over the island to manage both.

### D. Cloud Belt and Coast
- **Mid-slope:** Coffee, breadfruit and Nēnē in the leeward cloud belt.
- **Coast below:** Fishponds, spring-fed Koloa ponds and coconut.
- **Strengths:** Two of the most pleasant climates on the island. Calm, sunny, reliable.
- **Weakness:** Vog if an active vent sits upwind.

### E. Kīpuka Patchwork
- **Layout:** Several kīpuka connected by trails across lava fields.
- **Strengths:** Each pocket is naturally walled and easy to defend. Different elevations give different crops.
- **Weakness:** Lava risk. Hard travel across rough rock.
- **Story value:** Hidden farms, secret routes and settlements under the shadow of an active volcano.

### F. Saddle Ranch and Valley
- **High saddle:** Nēnē flocks grazing open grassland.
- **Valley below:** Loʻi taro and Koloa.
- **Strengths:** Splits the birds by habitat. Geese away from crops, ducks in the water.
- **Weakness:** Cold, windy work in the saddle.

### G. Vertical Terrace Farm
- **Layout:** One long slope terraced from coast to upland.
- **Bottom terraces:** Flooded loʻi with Koloa.
- **Middle terraces:** Dryland taro, sweet potato, breadfruit.
- **Top terraces:** Cool-climate vegetables and Nēnē pasture.
- **Strengths:** Water flows down through every level. Compact and easy to oversee.
- **Weakness:** Enormous labor to build and maintain.

### H. Estuary Fortress
- **Layout:** Coastal wetland with fishponds, loʻi and a rock-walled compound on a nearby hill or cinder cone.
- **Strengths:** Food from fish, taro and ducks in one place. High ground for defense and tsunami escape.
- **Weakness:** Exposed to storms and raids from the sea.

### I. Full Island Circuit
- **Layout:** A network of farms covering every zone: windward valleys, leeward cloud belt, uplands, saddles and coasts.
- **Strengths:** Total resilience. Every crop the island can grow. Drought, flood or eruption on one side never stops production.
- **Weakness:** Needs a large population and strong leadership to run.
- **Story value:** Fits a kingdom or chiefdom ruling the whole island.

## Part 6: Quick Placement Rules

- **Taro and Koloa:** Windward valleys, estuaries and spring-fed coastal ponds. Wherever water flows steadily.
- **Nēnē:** Leeward slopes, uplands, saddles and orchard rows. Dry, grassy ground away from leafy crops.
- **Vegetables:** Sheltered leeward pockets, upland cool zones or behind windbreaks.
- **Orchards and coffee:** Leeward cloud belt.
- **Coconut and salt-tolerant crops:** Coast, especially leeward.
- **Pasture:** Uplands, saddles and gentle ridges.
- **Avoid:** Ground directly downslope of active vents or rift zones, narrow valley mouths during the wet season, unprotected windward coastline for tender crops and low coastal ground with no escape from tsunamis.

## Part 7: Seasonal Notes

- **Summer:** Trades at their strongest and steadiest. Windward side wet, leeward side driest. Hurricane season peaks.
- **Winter:** Trades weaken and break more often. Kona storms hit the leeward side with heavy rain. Big northwest swells hit north shores.
- **Bird calendar:** Koloa nest year-round with a peak from November to May, the wet season. Nēnē breed August to April, so ganders guard hardest through winter. Remap Earth months to the Caldris calendar when a beat needs dates.
