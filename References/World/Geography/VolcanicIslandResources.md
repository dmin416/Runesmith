# Volcanic Island Resources: Old Eroded Island with Recent Renewed Volcanism

Companion to `VolcanicIslandEcology.md`. Crops and farming systems: `VolcanicIslandCrops.md`. Places: `Places.md` (Dragnis-class islands).

## The Geological Baseline

This island is an old hotspot volcano that went quiet long ago and has now reawakened. Real models:

- **Oʻahu:** the Koʻolau shield died about 1.8 million years ago. Rejuvenated eruptions (the Honolulu Volcanics) built Diamond Head, Punchbowl, Koko Head, Hanauma Bay and Salt Lake Crater long afterward.
- **Kauaʻi:** the Kōloa Volcanics erupted across an already deeply eroded island.
- **Haleakalā (Maui):** an old volcano with eruptions within the last thousand years.
- **La Palma and Tenerife (Canaries):** old edifices with modern eruptions, including La Palma in 2021.

The result is two islands layered on top of each other.

**The old island** brings:
- Deep weathered soils, laterite and bauxite.
- Mature streams, deep valleys and sea cliffs.
- Exposed volcanic plumbing (dikes and intrusive cores).
- Broad coral reefs, uplifted limestone, lagoons and mature forest.

**The new volcanism** brings:
- Fresh lava, cinder cones, tuff cones and crater lakes.
- Fumaroles, sulfur and geothermal heat.
- Mantle nodules (peridot and associated minerals).

Renewed eruptions on old islands tend to be alkalic. That means the magma is low in silica and gas-rich. It often carries chunks of mantle rock to the surface. Wherever this magma meets groundwater or seawater, the eruptions turn explosive.

The most interesting resources form where the new heat and lava meet the old rock, reefs and water.

### Notes and Assumptions
- Hawaiian names and sites are used as references because Hawaiʻi is the best-studied example of this island type. Other real islands are named where they fit better.
- Climate is assumed to match Hawaiʻi: tropical, in the trade-wind belt around 20°N.
- Resources are listed for a preindustrial to early-industrial society unless marked as needing industrial processing.
- "Plausible" means documented on comparable real islands. "Fictional stretch" means not documented on ocean islands.

## In the Ground

### Old Island Rock and Construction Materials
- **Weathered basalt:** abundant. Much of it is rotted to clay near the surface. Sound building stone comes from stream beds, sea cliffs and fresh quarry faces.
- **Dense dike rock:** the old volcano's plumbing is exposed in eroded valleys as thousands of vertical rock sheets (the Koʻolau dike complex on Oʻahu is the model). Dikes yield the hardest, finest-grained stone for adzes, grinding stones and anvils.
- **Gabbro and intrusive cores:** coarse crystalline rock from the old magma chamber, exposed in deeply eroded calderas. Tough, attractive stone for monuments and millstones.
- **Stream cobbles:** pre-sorted, rounded stone for walls, paving, slingstones and hearth stones.
- **Alluvium:** thick valley-floor deposits of gravel, sand and silt. Used for construction fill and as farmland.

### Coral Limestone and Carbonate Rock
An old island has had time to grow reefs and then lift or strand them.
- **Uplifted reef limestone:** stranded by past high sea levels. Oʻahu's ʻEwa Plain is reef rock about 120,000 years old. Uses include building blocks, lime burning, mortar, plaster and whitewash.
- **Eolianite:** lithified coastal sand dunes, found on Kauaʻi, Molokaʻi and Oʻahu. Soft, easily cut stone.
- **Beachrock:** cemented beach sand. Used for slabs and grinding.
- **Karst and limestone caves:** sinkholes and solution caves in old reef rock preserve fossil bird bones, pollen and human artifacts. Makauwahi Cave on Kauaʻi is the model. They also provide shelter, collect water and serve as burial sites.
- **Lime plus volcanic ash:** with burnt limestone from the old reef and fresh ash from the new vents, the island can make full Roman-style hydraulic concrete entirely from local materials.

### New Volcanic Materials
- **Tuff:**
  - Explosive eruptions where magma hits groundwater or seawater build tuff cones such as Diamond Head and Koko Head.
  - Tuff is consolidated ash, often altered to tan or orange palagonite.
  - It is soft enough to saw into blocks, as with the yellow tuff that built much of Naples.
  - It doubles as pozzolan for concrete.
- **Cinder and scoria:** from new cinder cones. Used for gravel, lightweight aggregate and drainage.
- **Fresh basaltic glass:**
  - Pele's hair, Pele's tears and limu o Pele form near active fountains.
  - Black glassy crusts form on fresh flows.
  - Unlike a young shield island, this glass is limited to the new eruption zones.
- **Pumice:** minor. More common if the renewed magma is gas-rich or more evolved.
- **Basalt fiber and cast basalt:** fresh flows provide clean, unweathered feedstock for melting.

### Mantle Nodules and Gems
Rejuvenated alkalic eruptions are the best setting on an ocean island for gem minerals carried up from the mantle.
- **Peridot (gem olivine):**
  - Occurs in dunite and lherzolite nodules torn from the mantle.
  - Gem peridot from San Carlos, Arizona comes from exactly this kind of alkali basalt.
  - A rejuvenated island has far better odds of gem-size stones than a young shield.
  - Non-gem olivine is used as foundry sand, refractory brick, a magnesium source and in carbon capture through weathering.
- **Chrome diopside:** a vivid green pyroxene, also found in the nodules. Usable as a gem.
- **Spinel:** dark crystals in the nodules. Mostly black to brown and small.
- **Sunstone (labradorite feldspar):** gem feldspar crystals that grow in some basalt flows, as in Oregon. Copper-bearing stones show red and green colors.
- **Garnet pyroxenite:** Salt Lake Crater on Oʻahu is famous for nodules containing garnet. Nanometer-scale diamond has been reported in them, invisible to the eye and of no economic value.
- **Sapphire and zircon:** alkali basalts carry these in continental volcanic fields such as eastern Australia and Thailand. On an ocean island they are a fictional stretch.

### Old-Flow Minerals (Vesicle and Fracture Fillings)
Time and groundwater fill the gas bubbles and cracks in old lava.
- **Zeolites:** chabazite, phillipsite, natrolite, analcime and others. Uses include water filtration, ion exchange, animal feed additives and desiccants.
- **Agate, chalcedony, jasper and common opal:** fill cavities and veins. Useful for tools, beads and carving.
- **Hyalite opal:** clear, glassy opal coating cracks in volcanic rock. Glows green under ultraviolet light.
- **Amethyst:** forms in vesicles of thick old basalt piles, as in the geodes of Brazil and Uruguay. Plausible in small amounts.
- **Calcite and Iceland spar:** clear calcite crystals in cavities of altered basalt (Helgustaðir, Iceland). Used for optics.
- **Stream-tumbled stones:** erosion frees these minerals, so they collect as pebbles in streams and on beaches.

### Old Caldera Alteration Zones
Deeply eroded calderas expose rock that was cooked by hot fluids for hundreds of thousands of years.
- **Typical minerals:** chlorite, epidote, clays and quartz veins.
- **Pyrite:** used for fire-starting when struck with hard stone and as a source of sulfur and sulfuric acid. Known as "fool's gold."
- **Trace chalcopyrite and other copper sulfides.**
- **Manganese oxides:** black crusts and pigment.

### Lava Tubes and Caves
- **Old lava tubes:** collapsed, root-filled and partly clogged with sediment. Many are still usable for shelter, burial or storage.
- **New lava tubes:** clean and recently formed near the active vents. Some may still be hot.
- **Cave minerals:**
  - Old tubes hold calcite and gypsum crusts plus silica coatings.
  - New tubes hold sulfate salts and glassy lava stalactites.
- **Limestone caves:** see Coral Limestone above.
- **Guano:**
  - Seabird colonies on cliffs and offshore islets deposit guano.
  - Over time, guano over limestone turns into phosphorite (phosphate rock), as on Nauru and other raised reef islands.
  - Guano supplies saltpeter (for gunpowder) and phosphate (for fertilizer).

### Water
An old island has far more surface water than a young one. Erosion has carved valleys and weathering has produced clay layers that slow infiltration.
- **Perennial streams and waterfalls:** used for irrigation, water wheels and hydropower.
- **Dike-impounded aquifers:** high-level water trapped between dikes inside the eroded volcano core. Tapped by tunnels.
- **Basal lens:** fresh water floating on seawater inside the island. The caprock of sediment and reef limestone along the coast holds it in, so coastal wells can be artesian.
- **Springs:** abundant at valley heads, cliff bases and the shoreline.
- **Wetlands and marshes:** in valley mouths and coastal plains.
- **Crater lakes and maars:**
  - Explosive vents leave craters that fill with water.
  - Salt Lake Crater on Oʻahu once held a salt lake that was used as a salt source.
  - Depending on groundwater and seawater connection, these lakes can be fresh, brackish, salty or acidic.
- **Lava-dammed lakes:** new flows that run down old valleys can block streams and create lakes.
- **Upland bogs:** high, wet plateaus become peat bogs. Kauaʻi's Alakaʻi Swamp is the model.

### Heat
- **Geothermal:** fresh magma beneath old, fractured, water-soaked rock produces a vigorous hydrothermal system. That means more hot water and steam than on a dry young shield.
- **Hot springs:**
  - Used for bathing, cooking, laundry, dyeing, tanning and drying.
  - Hot water circulating through old reef limestone deposits travertine (banded carbonate stone used for building).
  - Hot water circulating through basalt deposits siliceous sinter.
- **Steam vents:** used for steam cooking, sterilizing and drying.
- **Geothermal brines:** can carry lithium, boron, silica, manganese and zinc leached from the old rock.
- **Fuel-free process heat:** smelting, glassmaking, ceramics and salt boiling.
- **Other geothermal uses:** greenhouse heating, crop and timber drying, warm-water fish farming and hydrogen sulfide as a chemical feedstock.

### Soils and Weathering Products
- **Deep old soils (oxisols and ultisols):** heavily leached, red and acidic. Low in nutrients unless managed.
- **Laterite and bauxite:**
  - Thick iron-aluminum crusts on old, wet uplands.
  - Kauaʻi and Maui hold low-grade ferruginous bauxite, surveyed in the mid-1900s and never mined.
  - Hawaiian laterites are notably high in titanium (upper-end weathered soils can exceed 10% TiO₂; never mined there). Detail: `../Science/Metallurgy/Titanium.md`.
- **Fresh ash on old soil:** new ashfall blankets leached ground with fresh nutrients and rejuvenates farmland downwind of the vents.
- **Alluvial valley soils:** the best farmland on the island. Deep, fertile and well-watered.
- **Clays:**
  - Kaolinite, halloysite and gibbsite from deep weathering.
  - Valley and lagoon muds are finer and better sorted.
  - Usable for pottery and brick with tempering. Far better than anything on a young shield island.
- **ʻAlaea (red earth):** iron oxide clay used as pigment, dye and medicine. Also mixed with sea salt.
- **Bog iron:** iron oxides precipitate in wetlands and acidic bogs. Smeltable on a small scale.
- **Peat:** upland bogs accumulate peat. It can be cut, dried and burned as fuel, which partly offsets the island's lack of coal.

### Lake, Spring and Hydrothermal Deposits
- **Diatomite:** crater lakes and maars build up layers of diatom shells. Uses: filtration, mild abrasive, insulation and the absorbent used to stabilize dynamite.
- **Bentonite and smectite clays:** from hydrothermal alteration of ash. Uses: absorbent, fuller's earth for cleaning wool and oil, drilling mud, cosmetics and binder.
- **Lake evaporites:** salt crater lakes can deposit gypsum, Epsom salt, natron or soda. Natron is a classic flux for glassmaking and soap.
- **Mineral springs:** carbonated soda springs, iron springs and sulfur springs, plus therapeutic muds.
- **Natural carbon dioxide:** can be captured for carbonation or dry ice.
- **Steam condensate:** naturally distilled water from vents.
- **Geothermal scale:** silica and metal crusts that build up in hot springs and pipes.

### Glassmaking Constraint
- **No quartz sand:** basalt islands lack quartz, so beach sand makes poor glass. Silica for glass has to come from sinter, chalcedony, diatomite or remelted basaltic glass.

### Specialized Tool and Craft Stone
- **Imu (earth oven) stones:** dense basalt that will not crack or explode when heated.
- **Household and work stone:** poi pounders, stone lamps, net sinkers, sling stones, polishing stones and grinding slabs.
- **Game and ritual stone:** ʻulu maika (stone rolling disks), kōnane game boards cut into flat rock and carved stone images (kiʻi pōhaku).
- **Petroglyph surfaces:** smooth pāhoehoe flows are the main canvas for Hawaiian rock carvings.
- **Volcanic bombs, tumuli and pressure ridges:** distinctive shapes useful as landmarks, boundary markers or ritual stones.

### Mineral Pigments
- **Red:** ʻalaea clay, red oxidized cinder and hematite.
- **Yellow:** sulfur and yellow iron oxides (limonite) from altered ground.
- **White:** kaolin and lime.
- **Black:** manganese oxides, magnetite and charcoal.
- **Green:** olivine sand and chlorite-rich altered rock. Weak as pigments.

### Fossils and Subfossils
- **Reef fossils:** preserved in the uplifted limestone.
- **Extinct fauna:** bones in caves and sinkholes, including the moa-nalo (giant flightless ducks) and flightless ibises.

### Metals: What Is Realistic
| Metal | Realistic source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | Bog iron, laterite, magnetite and ilmenite placers in streams and beaches, possible skarn (see Contact Zones) | Best iron prospects of any version of this island |
| Aluminum | Bauxitic laterite | Needs industrial processing |
| Titanium | Laterite, ilmenite / titanomagnetite placers | Needs industrial processing for metal; pigment and Fe–Ti slag possible earlier. `../Science/Metallurgy/Titanium.md` |
| Manganese | Oxide crusts at hot springs and in old alteration zones | Pigment and alloy use |
| Copper | Native copper in old basalt vesicles, trace sulfides in old calderas, possible skarn | Small deposits at most |
| Nickel, chromium | Mantle nodules, olivine-rich cumulates | Traces |
| Rare earths, niobium | Carbonatites on alkalic ocean islands (Fuerteventura, Cape Verde) | Plausible if the renewed magma is strongly alkalic |
| Gold, silver, tin, lead | Essentially absent | Hot fluids can scavenge traces, never ore-grade on a real hotspot island |

## Contact Zones: Where New Volcanism Meets the Old Island

Most of what makes this island unique forms in these zones.

- **Lava over reef limestone:**
  - Lava bakes the limestone into quicklime and marble.
  - Magma intruding into limestone at depth produces skarn: garnet, wollastonite, magnetite and sometimes copper minerals.
  - Skarns are a classic iron and copper ore type, rare on ocean islands, which makes them a plausible find.
- **Magma meeting groundwater or the sea:**
  - Steam explosions build tuff cones and maars.
  - The blasts throw up blocks of old reef, old lava and mantle nodules, sampling every layer under the island in one place.
- **Lava down old valleys:**
  - Flows fill canyons, dam streams, form lakes and reroute rivers.
  - New waterfalls appear where streams spill over or around the flows.
- **Lava through forest:**
  - Tree molds (hollow casts of trunks) form, as at Lava Trees State Monument.
  - Charcoal layers and buried soils are preserved under the flow.
- **Kīpuka:**
  - Patches of old forest and soil left as islands inside new lava.
  - They hold older ecosystems, hidden ruins and isolated populations.
- **Lava into lagoons and reefs:**
  - Steam explosions and fish kills.
  - New black sand mixing with white coral sand.
  - Fresh hard substrate on which new reef can later grow.
- **Ash on wet old slopes:** heavy rain on fresh ash triggers mudflows (lahars) down valleys. The deposits leave fertile new valley floors.
- **New heat in old altered rock:**
  - Hydrothermal fluids leach metals from the old basalt and redeposit them in veins: pyrite, manganese oxides, silica and minor copper.
  - Hot springs build sinter and travertine terraces.
- **Lava or heat reaching upland bogs:** peat fires that smolder underground for long periods.

## In the Mountain

### Old Highlands
- **Eroded ridges and pali (cliffs):** knife-edge ridges and amphitheater-headed valleys. Defensive terrain with natural fortress sites.
- **Upland plateau and bogs:**
  - Cloud-soaked high ground with peat, stunted forest and rare plants.
  - Refuge for endemic birds.
- **Elevation:** older islands have subsided and eroded, so the old summit is lower and lacks snow. The new cones may add height.
- **Adze-quality stone:** dense dike rock exposed along ridges and valley walls.

### New Vents, Cones and Fumaroles
- **Cinder cones, spatter cones, tuff cones and maars:** clustered along fissures across the older landscape.
- **Sulfur:**
  - Crusts and crystals at fumaroles and solfataras.
  - Uses: gunpowder, matches, sulfuric acid, fungicide, rubber vulcanizing, fumigation and medicine.
- **Fumarole sublimates:**
  - Halite, sylvite (potash) and sal ammoniac (ammonium chloride, an alchemy staple and soldering flux).
  - Alum, gypsum, iron chlorides and specular hematite.
  - Trace metals in vent crusts and condensates, including selenium, tellurium, molybdenum, cadmium and minute gold. Scientifically real. Never economic.
- **Acid-altered ground:** bleached white, yellow and red rock containing kaolin, opal and alunite (an alum source).
- **Gases:**
  - Carbon dioxide pools in craters and low valleys. This is far more dangerous on an island with deep valleys and crater lakes.
  - Sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide form vog.
  - Laze forms where lava reaches the sea.
- **Crater lakes:** water sources, ritual sites and potential limnic eruptions (sudden CO2 release, as at Lake Nyos in Cameroon).
- **Lava lakes or active vents:** constant heat and light.

### Slopes: Plants
The old island has had time to grow rich soils and complex forest.

**Native species**
- **Koa:** large old-growth trees. Used for canoes, weapons, bowls and furniture.
- **ʻŌhiʻa lehua:** very hard wood, used for posts, tools and weapons. Also the first colonizer of the new flows.
- **ʻIliahi (sandalwood):** fragrant, high-value export timber.
- **Hāpuʻu tree fern:** pulu fiber and a famine starch.
- **Olonā:** among the strongest plant fibers. Used for cordage and nets.
- **Hala (pandanus):** coastal groves for mats, baskets and sails.
- **Loulu palm,** many medicinal plants and a deep stock of endemic species in the bogs and cloud forest.

**Additional timber**
- **Kauila:** extremely dense, used for spears and tools.
- **Kou and milo:** carving wood for bowls.
- **Hau:** light, flexible wood for outrigger booms, with bark fiber for rope.
- **Wiliwili:** very light wood for floats and surfboards.
- **Naio:** false sandalwood.
- **Māmane:** hard upland wood.
- **ʻOhe:** bamboo, introduced by Polynesians.

**Thatch and weaving**
- Pili grass and ʻuki sedge for thatch.
- Makaloa sedge for fine mats.

**Dyes**
- ʻŌlena (turmeric) for yellow.
- Noni bark for red and yellow.
- Kukui soot for black.
- Lichens for various colors.
- Manganese for black and kaolin for white.

**Fish poisons**
- ʻAuhuhu and ʻākia, crushed and used to stun fish in pools. Also usable as a story poison.

**Wild foods**
- Hōʻiʻo fern shoots.
- ʻŌhelo berries.
- Pia (Polynesian arrowroot) for starch.
- Wild yams (pī and hoi). Hoi is toxic unless properly prepared.

**Other plant and fungal resources**
- **Mosses and ferns:** used for packing, insulation, bedding and wound dressing.
- **Wood ash potash:** leached to make lye for soap and glass.
- **Fungi:** edible species, medicinal species and wood-rot species in wet forest.

**Medicinal plants (traditional Hawaiian use)**
- Noni: fruit and leaves for wounds, boils and internal complaints.
- ʻAwapuhi kuahiwi (shampoo ginger): sap used as shampoo and for sores.
- Māmaki: leaves brewed as tea.
- Koʻokoʻolau: leaves brewed as a tonic tea.
- ʻUhaloa: root used for sore throats.
- Kukui: nut oil and sap used as a laxative and for skin.
- ʻŌlena: turmeric root for ear, sinus and skin complaints.

**Fragrance**
- Maile vine: fragrant leaves for lei.
- Mokihana: anise-scented berries found only on Kauaʻi.
- ʻIliahi (sandalwood): wood and oil.

**Saps, oils and adhesives**
- ʻUlu (breadfruit) latex: used to caulk canoes and as birdlime to trap birds for feathers.
- Kukui and coconut oil: lamp fuel, wood finish and skin oil.
- Poi and starch pastes: glue for kapa and light repairs.

**Fiber from introduced plants**
- Coconut husk coir: braided into sennit (ʻaha), the main lashing cord for houses and canoes.
- Ti leaves: rain capes, sandals and wrapping.

**Fire-making**
- Fire plow: a hard stick rubbed along a groove in soft wood. Hau and olomea were preferred woods.

**Fermented and intoxicating drinks**
- ʻAwa (kava): sedative drink with ritual use.
- Ti root: baked for sweetness. Later distilled into ʻokolehao.
- Sugarcane: juice and later rum.

**Polynesian-introduced "canoe plants"**
- **Staples:** kalo (taro), ʻulu (breadfruit), ʻuala (sweet potato), niu (coconut), maiʻa (banana) and kō (sugarcane).
- **Kukui (candlenut):** lamp oil, torches, dye and polish.
- **Wauke (paper mulberry):** kapa bark cloth.
- **Kī (ti):** wrapping, thatch, a sweet edible root and liquor.
- **Others:** ʻawa (kava), noni, ipu gourds and ʻōhiʻa ʻai (mountain apple).

**Agriculture**
- Old valleys support large irrigated taro terraces (loʻi) fed by ʻauwai ditches.
- Dry slopes support field systems for sweet potato and dryland taro.
- Later introductions include coffee, sugar, pineapple, macadamia and cattle ranching.

**Climate bands**
- A wet windward side, dry leeward side, rain shadows, cloud forest and upland bog.
- The new eruptions add barren lava fields cutting across these bands.

### Slopes: Animals
- **Birds:**
  - Seabird colonies on sea cliffs and offshore islets supply eggs, meat, feathers and guano.
  - Forest birds supply red and yellow feathers for chiefly cloaks.
  - Nēnē (Hawaiian goose) and native waterbirds live in wetlands.
- **Mammals:** the Hawaiian hoary bat is the only native land mammal.
- **Kāhuli (tree snails):** brightly banded land snails. Their shells were strung into lei.
- **Reptiles:** geckos and skinks arrived with Polynesian voyagers.
- **Polynesian introductions:** pigs, dogs, chickens and rats.
- **Post-contact introductions:** goats, cattle, sheep and deer.
- **No snakes and no large native predators.**

### Freshwater Life
- **ʻOʻopu gobies:** fish that climb waterfalls using a suction-cup fin.
- **ʻŌpae:** freshwater shrimp.
- **Hīhīwai:** stream snails.
- **Native waterbirds:** found in wetlands and taro terraces.
- **Taro terraces as fish farms:** loʻi double as fish and shrimp ponds.

### Streams, Wind and Energy
- **Streams:** permanent, with large waterfalls. Strong hydropower potential.
- **Wind:** steady trade winds.
- **Geothermal:** stronger than on a young shield island because of water-rich old rock.
- **Fuel:** peat and abundant hardwood charcoal.

## In the Ocean

### Shoreline
- **Sea cliffs and sea caves:** the old island's wave-cut cliffs, as on Kauaʻi's Nā Pali coast. They host seabird colonies and hidden landings.
- **Sand types:**
  - White carbonate sand from coral and shell dominates most beaches.
  - Black sand appears near the new flows.
  - Green olivine sand appears near olivine-rich vents.
  - Salt-and-pepper beaches form where the sources mix.
- **Coastal plains:** sediment and uplifted reef create flat land for settlement, salt works and fishponds.
- **Sea salt:** made in clay-lined pans and basalt hollows, as at Hanapēpē on Kauaʻi. Also collected from salt lakes in coastal craters.
- **Fishponds (loko iʻa):** large walled ponds in bays and lagoons. An old island with a broad reef flat supports extensive aquaculture.
- **Tide pools:** ʻopihi (limpets), crabs, sea urchins and many edible limu (seaweeds).
- **Driftwood:** Pacific Northwest logs reach the island. They are less critical when native timber is plentiful.
- **Lava deltas:** new, unstable land at the active flow fronts.
- **Drowned river valleys:** the old island's flooded valleys become deep natural harbors. Pearl Harbor is one.
- **Flotsam and drift:**
  - Pumice from distant eruptions.
  - Glass floats.
  - Shipwreck timber, metal and cargo.

### Reefs, Lagoons and Shallows
- **Broad fringing reef:** possibly with a barrier reef and lagoon as the old island subsides. Far richer than the reef on a young island.
- **Seafood:** reef fish, heʻe (octopus), spiny lobster, eels, crabs and shellfish.
- **Coral:** live and dead coral for lime, mortar and whitewash.
- **Shells:**
  - Cowries (octopus lures and trade).
  - Cone shells.
  - Niʻihau-type small shells for high-value lei.
- **Pearls:**
  - Black-lipped pearl oysters (*Pinctada margaritifera*) thrive in calm lagoons.
  - Pearl Harbor on Oʻahu was named for its pearl beds.
- **Sea turtles:** meat and shell.
- **Hawaiian monk seals.**
- **Sharks:** teeth for leiomano weapons.
- **Offshore islets:** seabird colonies, guano and phosphate.

### Open Ocean
- **Pelagic fish:** ahi (tuna), aku (skipjack), mahimahi, ono, marlin, swordfish, opah and flying fish.
- **Squid.**
- **Whales:**
  - Humpbacks seasonally.
  - Sperm whales supply ambergris, spermaceti oil and ivory teeth.
- **Ocean energy:** waves, tides and thermal gradients.

### Marine Byproducts
- **Sea cucumbers:** bêche-de-mer was a major historical Pacific export to China.
- **Seaweed processing:**
  - Seaweed ash yields iodine and potash.
  - Some seaweeds yield agar and carrageenan.
  - Seaweed works as fertilizer.
- **Bittern:** the brine left over from salt making. It contains magnesium, bromine and potassium salts.
- **Animal byproducts:**
  - Shark liver oil and fish oil for lamps and waterproofing.
  - Fish skin leather and fish glue.
- **Slate pencil urchin spines:** used as natural drawing tools and as files and abrasives.
- **Sponges:** for cleaning and padding.
- **Coral sand:** used for liming acidic farm soil.

### Tool Materials and Marine Toxins
- **Fishhooks:** made from pearl shell, turtle shell, bone and hardwood.
- **Stingray spines:** barbed tips for spears in parts of the Pacific.
- **Cowrie shells:** octopus lures and scrapers.
- **Pufferfish:** carry tetrodotoxin, a nerve poison with no antidote.
- **Cone snails:** venomous harpoon-like stings. Some species can kill a person.
- **Box jellyfish:** arrive on Hawaiian shores on a regular lunar cycle about 8 to 10 days after the full moon.
- **Fire coral and stinging hydroids:** cause painful welts on contact.

### Deep Sea
- **Submerged reef terraces:** drowned reefs step down the island flank from past sea levels. Good fishing structure.
- **Deep seawater:**
  - Cold (around 6°C) and nutrient-rich, drawn from roughly 600 to 900 m.
  - Uses include cooling, aquaculture, drinking water and thermal energy.
  - The old island's broader shelf puts deep water farther from shore than on a young island.
- **Ferromanganese crusts:** cobalt, nickel, manganese, platinum, tellurium and rare earths. They grow thicker with time, so an old island's flanks and nearby seamounts carry thicker crusts.
- **Polymetallic nodules:** manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt on the abyssal plain (Clarion-Clipperton Zone model).
- **Hydrothermal vents:** possible on the island flank near the renewed activity. Iron-oxide mats and vent life.
- **Precious corals:**
  - Black coral at about 30 to 100 m.
  - Pink and gold coral at roughly 350 to 500 m.
  - Old slopes have had longer to grow mature beds.
- **Deep bottomfish:** ʻōpakapaka and onaga.

## Geographic and Strategic Resources
- **Natural harbors and sheltered anchorages.**
- **Defensible terrain:** pali cliffs and ridge-top fortress sites.
- **Flat uplifted reef plains:** room for towns, fields and later airfields.
- **Freshwater gaps:** places where ships can resupply.
- **Navigation landmarks:** peaks, cones and vent steam plumes visible far out to sea.
- **Warning signs:** earthquake swarms, ground swelling, spring temperature changes, dying vegetation and animal behavior.

---

## Cultural Resources
- **Archaeology:** petroglyph fields, heiau (temple) platforms, old trails, burial caves, kīpuka ruins and objects sealed beneath old flows.
- **Knowledge as a resource:** navigation lore, eruption histories preserved in chant and oral tradition, water rights systems and fishing rules.
- **Trade goods unique to the island:** peridot, Pele's hair, black coral, Niʻihau-type shells, sandalwood, red and yellow feather work, red ʻalaea salt and kapa cloth.

## Weather and Seasons
- **Two traditional seasons:** kau (dry, roughly May to October) and hoʻoilo (wet, roughly November to April).
- **Trade winds:** blow from the northeast most of the year. They bring rain to the windward side and carry vog away to the leeward side.
- **Kona winds:** southerly winds that replace the trades for days at a time, mostly in winter. They bring storms to the usually dry side and push vog across settled areas.
- **Rainfall extremes:** Mount Waiʻaleʻale on Kauaʻi averages around 9,500 mm of rain a year. Leeward coasts on the same island chain can get under 300 mm.
- **Temperature:** mild at sea level (roughly 20 to 30°C year-round). Cooler with elevation.
- **Ocean swells:** large north swells in winter and south swells in summer. They decide which coasts are safe for landing and fishing.
- **Hurricanes:** rare and severe. Hurricane ʻIniki struck Kauaʻi in 1992.
- **Distant tsunamis:** earthquakes across the Pacific send tsunamis to the island, as in 1946 (Aleutians) and 1960 (Chile), both of which devastated Hilo.

---

## Seasonal Availability
- **Makahiki:** a season of roughly four lunar months beginning with the rising of the Pleiades (Makaliʻi) in autumn. Harvest tribute was collected and war was forbidden.
- **Fishing kapu:** certain fish were closed to harvest during spawning. Aku and ʻōpelu traditionally alternated seasons of open and closed fishing.
- **Humpback whales:** present roughly December to April.
- **Seabirds:** nest seasonally. Eggs and chicks are only available part of the year.
- **Breadfruit:** fruits heavily in summer and autumn. Little or none for the rest of the year.
- **Box jellyfish:** monthly arrivals about 8 to 10 days after the full moon.
- **Storms and swells:** winter north swells close some coasts to canoes and reef gathering.

---

## Food Preservation and Processing
- **Salting and sun-drying:** fish, octopus and meat.
- **Smoking:** fish and meat over hardwood fires.
- **Poi and paʻi ʻai:** pounded taro. Paʻi ʻai is a dense, hard-pounded form that keeps for long periods and travels well. Poi ferments and sours over days.
- **Pit fermentation:** breadfruit and other starches buried in leaf-lined pits, practiced across the Pacific. Keeps for months to years.
- **Fishponds:** a living pantry. Fish are held alive until needed.
- **Containers:** gourds, wooden calabashes and ti-leaf bundles.
- **Cooking methods:** imu earth ovens, steam vents and hot springs.

## What Must Come from Outside
For a society that wants to move beyond stone and small-scale iron:
- Tin and copper in quantity (for bronze).
- Lead, gold and silver.
- Quartz sand or bulk high-grade silica.
- Coal and petroleum.
- High-grade iron ore for large-scale industry.

## Absent or Scarce Resources and Realistic Fixes

### Compared with a young shield island
**Gains:**
- Real limestone and lime.
- Better clay.
- Peat as fuel.
- Bog iron and placer iron.
- Thicker timber stands.
- Permanent streams.
- Rich reefs, lagoons and pearls.
- Gem-quality peridot from mantle nodules.
- Agate, zeolites and amethyst from old flows.
- Phosphate rock.
- Possible skarn iron and copper.

**Losses:**
- Less fresh glass overall.
- Lower old summit with no snow.
- Smaller areas of continuous active lava.

### Still absent or scarce
- Coal, oil and natural gas.
- Gold, silver, tin and lead.
- True flint (chert can form in limestone in small amounts).
- Large obsidian sources.

### Fixes that stay grounded
- **Obsidian and pumice:** give the renewed magma an evolved character, as in Iceland, the Canaries or the Azores.
- **Rare earths, niobium and phosphate:** add carbonatite (Canaries, Cape Verde).
- **Gold, silver and copper:** add a subduction component (Japan, Indonesia, Philippines, New Zealand) for epithermal gold-silver and porphyry copper.
- **Seafloor resources:** crusts, nodules, precious coral and ambergris.

## Story Decisions This File Does Not Cover
These are choices, not facts, so they are left to the story:
- **Island size and height:** sets rainfall, number of valleys, amount of farmland and population. For scale, pre-contact Hawaiʻi supported an estimated several hundred thousand people across all its islands using these resources.
- **Population and political structure.**
- **Technology level** and how much contact the island has with the outside world.
- **Distance to other lands** and the trade routes that connect them.
- **Eruption details:** scale, frequency and the location of the new vents relative to settlements.
- **Settlement history and timeline.**
- **Which hazards strike during the story.**

## Narrative Use

### Grounded Story Hooks
- **Established civilization disrupted:** the old island supports large populations, terraced valleys, fishponds and long-held land claims. New eruptions destroy and redraw all of it.
- **Long dormancy:** the volcano has been quiet for generations, centuries or longer. Its return reads as an ancient story coming true. Oral tradition may remember earlier eruptions as myth.
- **Kīpuka:** islands of old forest and old villages cut off inside new lava. They hold hidden refuges, isolated communities and preserved ruins.
- **Buried history:** lava covers old temples, roads and burial grounds. Steam blasts uncover sections of them.
- **New economy:**
  - Sulfur works, hot spring settlements, peridot digging, tuff quarries, geothermal forges and glassworks.
  - Old powers that controlled water, timber and farmland face new powers controlling heat, sulfur and gems.
- **Fully local gunpowder:** sulfur from the new vents, saltpeter from cave and islet guano, charcoal from old-growth hardwood.
- **Fully local concrete:** reef lime plus volcanic ash.
- **Iron age within reach:** bog iron, placer magnetite, peat fuel and geothermal heat together make small-scale ironworking plausible.
- **Water conflicts:** lava dams streams, creates new lakes, dries old irrigation systems and diverts water to different landholders.
- **Hazards:**
  - Carbon dioxide pooling in valleys and crater lakes.
  - Lahars down old valleys.
  - Steam explosions at the coast and in wet ground.
  - Earthquake-triggered cliff collapses.
  - Smoldering peat fires, vog and laze.
  - Flank collapse and tsunamis.
  - Acid rain from vog contaminating rain catchment water and leaching metals from roofs and pipes.
  - Fluorine in ash poisoning grazing animals, as after Iceland's Laki eruption in 1783.
  - Ashfall damaging crops and collapsing roofs.

### Fantasy Hooks: Real Properties and Lore
These are real traits, beliefs and histories that a magic system could build on.

- **Peridot:** historically believed to ward off evil spirits and nightmares. Hawaiian tourist lore often calls olivine "Pele's tears."
- **Pele's hair and Pele's tears:** named for the volcano goddess Pele. Hawaiian tradition holds that taking lava rock or sand from the island brings Pele's displeasure.
- **Hawaiian deities tied to the landscape:**
  - Pele (volcanoes) and her sister Hiʻiaka (forest and healing).
  - Kamapuaʻa (rain, wet forest and pig form), Pele's rival.
  - Poliʻahu (snow goddess of Mauna Kea).
- **Sulfur:** the "brimstone" of scripture, tied to the underworld and divine punishment. Burned for purification and fumigation in ancient rites.
- **Real alchemical reagents:** sulfur, sal ammoniac and alum, all collected at fumaroles.
- **Lodestone and magnetic rock:**
  - Lightning strikes can turn magnetite-rich rock into natural lodestone.
  - Cooling lava locks in the direction of Earth's magnetic field.
  - Strongly magnetic lava flows can deflect compass needles.
- **Iceland spar:** splits light into a double image. Norse sagas describe a "sunstone" that revealed the sun through clouds. Calcite is a leading candidate for it.
- **Dike swarms:** long, straight walls of rock radiating from the old volcanic center across the island.
- **Vent gases:** one hypothesis holds that gases rising from a fault at Delphi helped induce the oracle's trances. Volcanic gases can cause dizziness, visions and unconsciousness.
- **Crater lakes:**
  - Carbon dioxide releases can kill silently, as at Lake Nyos in 1986.
  - Lake Waiau on Mauna Kea is sacred in Hawaiian tradition. Families have placed infants' umbilical cords there.
- **Lava tubes and burial caves:** traditionally hidden resting places for the bones (iwi) of ancestors, protected by kapu.
- **Kīpuka:** isolated refuges where older forest and rare species survive inside newer lava.
- **Peat bogs:** preserve bodies and objects for thousands of years, as with the bog bodies of northern Europe.
- **Sharks:** often ʻaumakua (ancestral guardian spirits) in Hawaiian belief.
- **Ambergris:** its origin was a mystery for centuries. Medieval Europe used it as medicine, perfume and a supposed plague remedy.
- **Precious coral:** black coral is Hawaii's state gem. Red coral in the Mediterranean was worn for centuries as an amulet against the evil eye.
