## Iron Sources: Young vs Old

| Source | Typical Fe (reported or calculated) | Main impurities | Island age | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Titanomagnetite black sand | 45 to 58% Fe in magnetic concentrate | TiO₂ 5 to 15%, V₂O₅ 0.2 to 0.5%, SiO₂ glass films, MgO | Young coasts with recent littoral explosions | Best young-island ore; NZ Taranaki analog ~8% TiO₂ |
| Lava flow oxides (crushed, magnet-separated) | 15 to 35% Fe in concentrate | Al₂O₃, CaO, MgO as silicates | Young flows | 15 to 30 kg rock per kg oxide concentrate |
| Laterite / ferruginous crust | 35 to 55% Fe as goethite / hematite | Al₂O₃ 10 to 25%, TiO₂ 5 to 15%, SiO₂ | Old wet uplands | Kauaʻi / East Maui bauxite surveys, never mined |
| Mantle nodules (serpentinized) | 5 to 15% Fe in rock | MgO dominant, Ni in olivine | Rejuvenated alkalic vents on old islands | Better for Ni and Mg than for bulk Fe |
| Bog / wetland iron | 40 to 60% Fe in dried mud | Organic matter, Mn | Old valleys, bogs | Small bloomery feed |
| Ferruginous bauxite | 20 to 40% Fe | Al₂O₃ 20 to 40%, TiO₂ | Old islands | Often processed for Al with Fe-rich red mud |
| Red cinder / scoria | 10 to 20% Fe | Al-Si glass, high porosity | Any cinder cone | Flux or lightweight aggregate; poor ore |
| Fresh basalt (bulk) | 11 to 13% Fe total | Locked in silicates | Young | Flux and construction, not direct ore |

### How island age changes iron ore

| Stage | Dominant iron form | Collection character |
|---|---|---|
| Active shield (0 to 0.5 Ma) | Titanomagnetite in black sand; oxides on flow tops | Beach and littoral cliff layers; strong magnets |
| Middle (1 to 2 Ma) | Mix of sand, stream heavies, beginning laterite | Mixed routes; sand supply may shrink as glass weathers |
| Old (3 to 6 Ma) | Laterite goethite / hematite; some sand at river mouths | Pit laterite on ridges; wash and roast upgrades |
| Atoll / drowned | None in rock | Import only |

Weathering removes Mg, Ca, Na, K, and much Si from basalt while Fe and Ti accumulate in red soils. Young islands export iron as **dense sand grains**; old islands export iron as **soft crusts and fines** that need agglomeration before smelting.

## Titanomagnetite: Why Hard to Smelt

### What it is

**Titanomagnetite** is a solid solution between magnetite (Fe₃O₄) and ulvospinel (Fe₂TiO₄). Titanium substitutes for Fe in the spinel structure. Hawaiian grains are often **subhedral, fine, and coated in basaltic glass**. Vanadium substitutes for Fe at hundreds to thousands of ppm (0.02 to 0.05 wt% V₂O₅ typical in NZ ironsand analogs).

### Problems

| Problem | Mechanism | Symptom in smelt |
|---|---|---|
| Stiff Ti slag | TiO₂ raises slag viscosity and melting point | Incomplete separation, trapped metal, furnace hang-ups |
| TiC / TiN "bears" | Ti reacts with C or N at high temperature | Hard inclusions in bloom, broken tools |
| Lower yield | Ti reports to slag and bears | More charcoal per kg iron vs hematite ore |
| Fine grain | Sand size below 1 mm | Furnace packing, poor permeability without agglomeration |
| Attached glass | Hyaloclastite and tachylite rims | Extra SiO₂ and Al₂O₃; fayalite slag |
| V in metal | V partitions partly to iron | Desirable for steel microalloying; complicates some slag uses |

### Solutions

| Approach | When to use |
|---|---|
| **Route H1** reduction roast + wet magnetic separation | Default for high-Ti sand; makes sponge iron powder and Ti-rich residue |
| Agglomeration (tatara pack, pellets, briquettes) | Any direct smelt of sand |
| Higher flux ratio (CaO + MgO) | Counter fayalite and stiff slag |
| Lower smelt temperature and shorter tap | Bloomery rather than long BF campaign on Ti sand |
| Accept Ti-V slag as co-product | Feed `TitaniumProcessing.md` chloride route after upgrade |
| Pre-roast to hematite | Improves permeability; magnetizing roast for laterite |

### Historical parallels

| Site | Lesson for Hawaiian ore |
|---|---|
| **Japanese tatara** (masa vs akome) | Masa is high-grade sand; akome is clay-bound low grade. Hawaiian sand behaves like **akome with Ti**, needing careful flux and lower temperature. |
| **New Zealand ironsand** (Taranaki) | Direct smelt in rotary kiln / melter at NZ Steel: iron for steel, TiO₂ in slag. Proof that Ti sand can feed industry if slag is valued. |
| **Panzhihua (China)** | Titanomagnetite with V: blast furnace with controlled slag chemistry; V recovered from slag. Model for **integrated Fe-Ti-V** thinking. |

## Collecting Iron Sand (Young Islands)

### Where

- **Black sand beaches** fed by recent littoral explosions or eroding glassy cliffs (design analogs: Punaluʻu, Pololū, Waipiʻo, new beaches after 2018-type eruptions).
- **Stream mouths** after storms: heavy layer on top of mixed sand.
- **Behind lava deltas** in the surf zone where waves winnow ash.

Avoid coral-rich mixed beaches unless you acid-wash carbonate first (`Sands.md`).

### Field procedure

1. **Test spot:** Drag a strong ferrite or Nd magnet in a plastic bag through dry sand at several points. Dark streak that jumps to the magnet marks titanomagnetite-rich zones.
2. **Collect the dark layer:** Scrape the top 2 to 5 cm where magnet pull is strongest. Skip pink or white carbonate streaks.
3. **Wash:** Rinse salt and mud in fresh water. Swirl in a bucket; pour off floating organics and light glass.
4. **Dry:** Spread on mats; sun dry or warm air. Salt left on grains causes spitting in roast.
5. **Magnetic hand clean:** Pass dry sand near magnet; collect black cling. Repeat until nonmagnetic tail is mostly glass and olivine.
6. **Wet magnetic clean (optional upgrade):** Slurry sand in water. Stir with magnet under water; lift magnetic fraction. Settle and decant fines.
7. **Optional grind:** If grains are glued in glass lumps, jaw crush then roll mill to liberate. Re-wash and re-magnet.
8. **Lodestone:** A natural magnetite lodestone (rare in Hawaiʻi) or a forged magnet can sort small batches without imports.

### Grade expectations (magnetic fraction, washed)

| Product | Fe (approx) | TiO₂ (approx) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw beach magnetic grab | 35 to 50% Fe | 5 to 12% | Needs wash |
| Washed LIMS-style concentrate | 45 to 58% Fe | 8 to 15% | H1 roast or agglomerated smelt |
| Nonmagnetic tail | 5 to 15% Fe | 1 to 4% | Olivine, glass; Mg or construction |
| NZ Taranaki reference concentrate | ~56% Fe | ~8% TiO₂ | External benchmark |

## Extracting Oxides from Lava Rock

Use when beaches are protected, scarce, or carbonate-mixed but fresh **`aʻa`** and **`pāhoehoe`** are abundant inland.

### Procedure

1. **Choose rock:** Flow tops with visible oxide staining, dense tachylite crust, or vesicle linings rich in magnetite. Avoid heavily olivine-poor glass if you need easier crushing.
2. **Fire-set (optional):** Stack boulders; burn charcoal underneath overnight to induce thermal shock. Quench with water. Reduces jaw work.
3. **Crush:** Hammer to fist size, then jaw or stamp mill to minus 10 mm.
4. **Grind:** Ball mill or rotary querns to minus 0.5 mm if you need liberation from silicates.
5. **Wash:** Slurry and tabling removes light glass and feldspar.
6. **Gravity:** Shaking table or spiral if available; dense fraction is richer in oxides.
7. **Magnetic:** Dry or wet LIMS on the heavy fraction.

**Yield rule of thumb:** **15 to 30 kg fresh basalt per 1 kg magnetic oxide concentrate**, depending on flow texture and grind fineness.

**Basalt as flux:** The nonmagnetic tail (plagioclase, pyroxene, glass) is ideal **silicate flux** for smelting the concentrate, provided you balance CaO with shell or limestone if available.

## Collecting Laterite Iron (Old Islands)

### Where

- **Red upland ridges** with iron-stained boulders and crumbly crust (oxisol / ultisol).
- **Exposed road cuts** and stream banks on old shields (Kauaʻi / East Maui type ferruginous bauxite).
- **Rejuvenated cone slopes** where fresh ash caps older laterite.

### Identify good ore

| Field sign | Likely mineral | Expected Fe after upgrade |
|---|---|---|
| Hard red nodules | Hematite / goethite | 50 to 60% Fe |
| Yellow-brown crumbly layer | Goethite | 40 to 55% Fe |
| Dark magnetic soil after rain | Magnetite from roast or natural | 45 to 55% Fe |
| High clay, low density | Poor ore | Below 35% Fe, skip |

### Collect and upgrade

1. **Collect:** Break crust; shovel nodules and fines. Separate roots and organic litter.
2. **Wash:** Trommel or screen at 5 mm; clay slimes carry Al and Si into overflow.
3. **Screen:** Keep 0.1 to 10 mm for agglomeration; ultrafines need briquetting.
4. **Roast:** Open heap or kiln at 600 to 900°C with air to convert goethite to hematite and drive off water.
5. **Magnetizing roast (optional):** 500 to 700°C with slight reducing atmosphere (CO from charcoal) to form magnetite for magnetic pick-up.
6. **Magnetic separation:** As for sand.

### Grade table (laterite iron products)

| Product | Fe | TiO₂ | Al₂O₃ | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raw crust | 30 to 45% | 3 to 10% | 15 to 30% | Wet and clayey |
| Washed + screened | 40 to 50% | 5 to 12% | 10 to 20% | Smeltable with flux |
| Roasted hematite | 50 to 58% | 5 to 12% | 8 to 15% | Good for bloomery |
| Magnetic after M-roast | 45 to 55% | 5 to 12% | 8 to 15% | Easier handling |

## Ore Prep and Agglomeration

Fine Hawaiian sand and laterite fines will not percolate in a stack without binding.

| Method | Feed | Binder / fuel | Size target | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tatara pack** | Sand + charcoal layers | None; alternating layers in clay furnace | 30 to 100 mm layers | Small high-carbon bloom, Japanese style |
| **Hand briquettes** | Ore fines + molasses or starch + charcoal dust | 5 to 10% binder | Walnut size | Bloomery, H1 feed before roast |
| **Pellets** | Ore + bentonite or clay slip | 1 to 2% clay | 10 to 20 mm spheres | Cupola or small BF if you have a kiln |
| **Sinter (crude)** | Ore + flux + coke fines on grate | Ignition from charcoal | 20 to 50 mm clinker | Only if you can sustain 1100°C+ draft |
| **Charcoal sizing** | N/A | Hardwood or coconut shell | 20 to 80 mm for furnace; fines for briquettes | Uniform permeability |

**Charcoal:** No coal on island. Coconut shell charcoal is dense and low ash; **`kiawe`** (mesquite) and **`ohia`** hardwood give longer furnace life. Crush and screen to remove fines that choke the stack.

## Flux and Slag Design

### Goals

- Lower slag melting point below metal tapping temperature.
- Keep TiO₂ and Al₂O₃ in slag, not as viscous mass around bloom.
- Avoid pure **fayalite** (2FeO·SiO₂) slag that is sticky and S-retaining.

### Fayalite issue with Ti and Al

High SiO₂ from glass rims plus FeO from reduction favors fayalite. TiO₂ and Al₂O₃ raise viscosity further. Symptom: metal in gelatinous slag, failed tap.

**Fix:** Add **CaO** and **MgO** to push composition toward olivine / melilite field and fluidify slag. MgO also matches Hawaiian olivine availability.

### Hawaiian flux materials

| Material | CaO / MgO / SiO₂ role | Source island age |
|---|---|---|
| Crushed coral / shell | CaO | Old islands with reef |
| Burned reef limestone | CaO (quicklime) | Old uplifted reef |
| Olivine sand (green sand) | MgO, SiO₂ | Young beaches |
| Crushed basalt tail | SiO₂, Al₂O₃, CaO from plagioclase | Any |
| Salt (Na₂O) | Lowers melt, risky if excessive | Seawater |
| Wood ash | K₂O, CaO, SiO₂ | Any forest |
| Red cinder | Al-Si, some Fe | Cinder cones |

### Guidelines by ore

| Ore type | CaO : SiO₂ : MgO starting ratio (weight, crude) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ti magnetite sand | 1 : 1.5 : 0.5 plus 10 to 15% extra CaO from shell | High Ti needs more CaO |
| Laterite hematite | 1 : 2 : 0.3 | Less Si from ore; watch Al₂O₃ |
| Lava oxide concentrate | Match Si from tail; add shell until slag taps | Use own gangue as flux |

### Practical test-smelt method

1. Mix **100 g ore** + **30 g charcoal** + flux variants in crucible or small bloomery pot.
2. Blow or fire to 1150 to 1200°C for 20 to 30 minutes.
3. Freeze slag button; section and polish.
4. **Good slag:** glassy, green-black, breaks cleanly from metal bead.
5. **Bad slag:** sticky, stringy, metal scattered as droplets. Add CaO and retry.

Record Ti-bearing slag buttons for chlorination feed tests (`TitaniumProcessing.md`).

## Route H1: Reduction Roast + Magnetic Separation

**Recommended default for high-Ti black sand.** Separates most Ti from iron before any liquid smelt, cuts TiC bears in bloomery, and produces a **Ti-rich residue** for pigment or chloride feed.

### Chemistry (simplified)

| Step | Reaction | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Reduction | Fe₃O₄ + C → 3Fe + CO / CO₂ (and partial FeO) | Also reduces some ilmenite boundaries |
| Carbide risk | TiO₂ + C → TiC (undesired) | Control temperature and time |
| Roast product | Magnetic Fe / Fe₃O₄ mix | Nonmagnetic Ti-rich oxide residue |

### Equipment

- Sealed or semi-sealed retort: clay drum in charcoal bed, or steel drum with vent hole and clay seal.
- Thermocouple or color pyrometer target **1000 to 1150°C**.
- Ball mill or mortar for wet grind after roast.
- Wet magnetic separator: strong magnet in slurry, or improvised LIMS tray.

### Procedure

1. **Mix:** 100 parts washed magnetic sand + **30 to 40 parts charcoal** by weight. Optional **1 to 2 parts salt cake (Na₂SO₄)** to flux and break glass coatings (adds Na and S risk; test small first).
2. **Load retort:** Pack mix; seal lid with clay except vent for initial moisture.
3. **Heat:** Ramp in charcoal fire or kiln. Hold **1000 to 1150°C** for **45 to 90 minutes** depending on batch size. Vent CO safely outdoors.
4. **Cool:** Slow cool in retort or bury in ash to limit reoxidation.
5. **Grind wet:** Mill to minus 200 mesh in water slurry.
6. **Wet magnetic separation:**
   - **Magnetic fraction:** iron powder **85 to 95% Fe** if roast was strong and wash repeated.
   - **Nonmagnetic tail:** Ti residue **30 to 50% TiO₂** with Fe and Si impurities.
7. **Upgrade Ti tail (optional):** Acid leach (HCl or H₂SO₄) of nonmagnetic fraction removes much Fe and Mg; residue can reach **60 to 80% TiO₂** for chlorinator trials. See `TitaniumProcessing.md`.

### Products

| Product | Composition | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Iron powder | 85 to 95% Fe, low Ti | Forge weld to bloom or crucible melt to cast iron |
| Ti residue | 30 to 50% TiO₂ (up to 60 to 80% after acid) | Pigment, soda roast, or TiCl₄ feed |
| Char ash | K, Ca silicates | Field flux or discard |

Links: **`TitaniumBootstrap.md`** for first metal; **`TitaniumProcessing.md`** for chlorination and Kroll.

## Route H2: Tatara and Bloomery

Direct smelt of agglomerated sand or H1 powder when you want a **single furnace tradition** and can accept Ti-V slag.

### Small bloomery build

| Part | Spec |
|---|---|
| Shaft | 30 to 50 cm ID clay or stone stack, 80 to 120 cm tall |
| Tuyere | One ceramic pipe 2 to 3 cm ID, 15° upward, 10 cm above floor |
| Floor | Sloped clay with tap arch to slag pit |
| Bellows | Two leather bags or water-wheel blower; target 100 to 200 L/min at smelt |
| Charge | Walnut-size ore + charcoal 1:1 to 1:1.5 by volume, layered |

**Run:** Preheat 30 min. Add charge every 10 to 15 min. Tap slag when runny (every 30 to 60 min). After 3 to 6 hours, open and extract **bloom** (sponge iron). Expect **20 to 40 kg bloom per 100 kg sand charge** on Ti ore (lower than hematite).

### Tatara-style build (small)

| Part | Spec |
|---|---|
| Furnace | Clay **`tatara`** box ~40 cm wide, 60 cm deep, open top |
| Layers | Alternate **charcoal** and **ore sand** with thin clay **`ama`** partition optional |
| Air | Side tuyeres or natural draft only for tiny runs; bellows for production |
| Duration | 24 to 72 hours for small box; pull **`kera`** (blooming mass) once |

**Run:** Keep top charcoal glowing. Do not flood with ore fines. Pull bloom while hot; hammer on anvil to expel slag.

### Adjustments for Hawaiian Ti sand

| Issue | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Ti slag viscosity | Increase CaO flux 15 to 25% vs mainland sand |
| Permeability | Briquette sand; never charge wet |
| Bears | Prefer **H1 powder** for bloomery feed instead of raw sand |
| V in iron | Accept microalloying or oxidize slag for V recovery later |
| Yield | Expect **2 to 3x charcoal** vs classic hematite bloomery |

## Route H3: Blast Furnace and Cast Iron

### Basics

Continuous counterflow: ore + flux + charcoal (or coke if imported) descends; hot air enters tuyeres. Produces **liquid cast iron** and fluid slag. Requires strong draft, water-cooled tuyeres at scale, and stable charge.

### Small charcoal blast furnace

| Parameter | Small island BF |
|---|---|
| Height | 3 to 6 m if stone stack |
| bosh diameter | 0.6 to 1.0 m |
| Production | 100 to 500 kg hot metal per day if staffed |
| Flux | Shell lime + olivine sand |
| Limit | Ti sand causes difficult slag; use **pelletized H1 iron** or low-Ti laterite for BF |

### Hawaiian ore limits

| Ore | BF suitability |
|---|---|
| H1 iron powder (recharged) | Moderate; recycle slag for Ti build-up control |
| Raw titanomagnetite sand | Poor unless blended with limestone and low-Ti ore |
| Laterite hematite | Good with CaO flux |
| NZ-style melter | Industrial analog; not cottage |

### Casting and cupola

- **Cast iron:** Tap liquid into molds for pots, weights, anvils. High Si from charcoal is normal.
- **Cupola:** Re-melt pig and scrap with coke or charcoal; faster than BF for foundry work. Ti-rich scrap raises slag volume; keep separate slag pots.

## Consolidating Bloom and Sponge

| Feed | Method | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Bloomery bloom | Forge weld at yellow heat; fold and hammer | Wrought iron bar, low C |
| H1 iron powder | Compact in mold, forge weld, or sinter in crucible | Sponge bar |
| Sponge (porous) | **Crucible melt** under flux (easier than steel MP) | Cast iron ingot |
| Sponge | Direct forge without melt | Iron with voids; repeat passes |

## Steelmaking

Starting from wrought iron or decarburized cast iron.

### Methods

| Method | Input | Process outline | Island feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cementation | Wrought bar + charcoal pack | Heat days in sealed clay box; C diffuses in | Good with charcoal |
| Shear steel | Cemented bars | Forge weld stacked bars | Good |
| Crucible steel | Cast iron + flux + glass | Melt in closed crucible; control C | Good small batches |
| Finery | Cast iron | Oxidize C on hearth to wrought | Skilled; charcoal heavy |
| Puddling | Cast iron | Rotating hearth oxidizes C | Needs large hearth |
| Malleable iron pack | White cast + iron oxide | Anneal pack days | Foundry follow-on |
| Bessemer | Molten cast iron | Air blow through metal | Needs large scale; Ti-V may interfere |

### Heat treatment (wrought and low alloy)

| Treatment | Temperature / medium | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Full anneal | 900°C, slow cool | Soft for filing |
| Normalize | 850°C, air cool | Uniform grain |
| Harden (C steel) | 780 to 850°C, water or oil quench | Hard edge |
| Temper | 200 to 400°C after quench | Toughness |
| Case harden | Cementation + quench | Hard skin |

### Testing

- **Spark test:** Low Ti steel sparks similar to mild carbon steel; heavy Ti slag inclusions show as dark chips when filing.
- **File test:** Bite on hardened sample.
- **Bend test:** Wrought should bend 90° without cracking.
- **V benefit:** Retained V from sand can raise hardenability slightly at same carbon.

## Iron Byproducts: Ti Slag and Vanadium

### Ti slag from smelt

Slag from H2/H3 on titanomagnetite is **20 to 40% TiO₂** depending on flux and reduction. Cool, crush, magnet-remove metal droplets.

| Use | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Chlorinator feed | Crush to minus 100 mesh; reduce CaO and MgO if possible |
| Cement additive | Ti slag as mineralizer (test setting time) |
| Pigment raw | Grind; may need roast and acid leach for whiteness |

See **`TitaniumProcessing.md`** slag and synthetic rutile sections.

### Vanadium recovery (outline)

1. **Source:** Titanomagnetite sand or Ti slag with **0.2 to 0.5% V₂O₅**.
2. **Salt roast:** Mix slag or concentrate with **NaCl or Na₂SO₄ + limestone**; roast 850°C with air. Vanadium → soluble sodium vanadate.
3. **Leach:** Hot water leach; filter.
4. **Precipitate:** Adjust pH; add **NH₄Cl** to precipitate **ammonium metavanadate**.
5. **Calcine:** Heat to **500 to 600°C** for **V₂O₅** (orange-red powder).
6. **Ferrovanadium:** Reduce V₂O₅ with aluminum or silicothermic in furnace; or add V₂O₅ to steel melt.

## Aluminum

### Gibbsite in Kauaʻi / East Maui bauxite

Old wet tropical weathering leaves **gibbsite (Al(OH)₃)** with iron and titanium in ferruginous bauxite. Grades are **low by global standards** but are the only island Al ore.

### Ore selection

| Layer | Al₂O₃ (approx) | Fe₂O₃ | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface crust | 25 to 35% | 20 to 40% | Soda sinter |
| High-gibbsite pocket | 40 to 50% | 10 to 25% | Best Bayer candidate if pure enough |
| Red mud after leach | 10 to 20% | 40 to 60% | Brick or discard |

### Soda sinter alumina route (no autoclave)

1. **Mix:** Bauxite + **Na₂CO₃** (from trona import or soda ash from kelp / salt process) ~1:0.3 to 1:0.5.
2. **Sinter:** 900 to 1100°C in kiln → **NaAlO₂** plus iron-rich clinker.
3. **Leach:** Hot water dissolves aluminate.
4. **Precipitate:** CO₂ or seed Al(OH)₃; wash.
5. **Calcine:** 1000 to 1200°C → **Al₂O₃**.

### Bayer note

If caustic **NaOH** is available (chloralkali plant), classic **Bayer** at **140 to 250°C** in autoclave gives cleaner alumina. Island constraint is **pressure vessel**, not ore.

### AlCl₃ chlorination

Roast alumina with carbon and **Cl₂** → **AlCl₃** (subsequent Deville or electrolysis routes). Deadly gas; closed system. See **`TitaniumBootstrap.md`** for shared Cl₂ plant logic.

### Deville Na reduction

AlCl₃ + Na (from NaOH electrolysis) → Al + NaCl in retort. Batch, expensive Na, but no cryolite.

### Hall-Heroult

Needs **cryolite (Na₃AlF₆)** or fluoride imports and **large DC power**. Alumina density **3.97 g/cm³**; metal **2.70 g/cm³**. **Verify finished bar at 2.70 g/cm³** with water displacement.

## Magnesium from Olivine and Seawater

### Olivine HCl route

1. **Digest:** Crushed olivine (green sand or nodules) in **HCl** → **MgCl₂** solution + **SiO₂** residue.
2. **Remove Fe / Ni:** Oxidize and precipitate Fe(OH)₃; Ni follows (see **`Nickel.md`**).
3. **Precipitate:** **Mg(OH)₂** with lime or NaOH.
4. **Calcine:** **MgO** (caustic calcined or dead burned).

### Seawater / bittern

- Evaporate seawater or solar pans → **MgCl₂** in bittern after NaCl drops.
- Precipitate **Mg(OH)₂** with lime (burned coral).

### Magnesia grades

| Grade | Source | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Caustic calcined MgO | 700 to 900°C | Chemical, rubber |
| Dead burned | 1500°C+ | Refractory |
| Epsom salt | MgSO₄ from MgO + H₂SO₄ | Medicine, fertilizer |

### Metal

- **Electrolysis** of molten **MgCl₂** ( Dow process ) needs dry anhydrous salt and power.
- **Thermal:** Pidgeon process (silicothermic on dolomite) needs dolomite import unless you make CaMg(CO₃)₂ from lime + seawater Mg.

## Silicon and Silica

### Hawaiian silica sources (no quartz sand)

| Source | SiO₂ form | Purity path |
|---|---|---|
| Olivine HCl residue | Amorphous silica gel | Wash, calcine |

- **Acid leach** of olivine digest silica removes Mg and Fe traces.

## Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine

### Chlorine

See **`TitaniumBootstrap.md`** and chloralkali sections in **`MaterialPurification.md`**: **NaCl brine electrolysis** → **Cl₂**, **H₂**, **NaOH**. Required for TiCl₄ and AlCl₃ chains.

### Bromine

1. Concentrate **bittern** from solar salt works (Mg-rich liquor).
2. Add **Cl₂** to oxidize Br⁻ → Br₂.
3. **Steam distillation** of liquor; condense red **Br₂** liquid.

### Iodine

| Route | Steps |
|---|---|
| Kelp / limu ash | Burn seaweed; leach ash; acidify; oxidize with Cl₂ or MnO₂; sublimate I₂ |
| Bittern | Lower concentration than Br; often co-recover with Br |

Store I₂ with ventilation; sublimation stains and irritates lungs.

## Sulfur and Fumarole Minerals

**Kīlauea and active vents (Earth):** park protection applies on real Hawaiʻi. Design islands may allow supervised collection away from tourist zones.

### Sources

- **Sulfur banks** at fumaroles: native **S**, **H₂S** gas, sulfate alteration.
- **Submarine vents:** not cottage accessible.

### Fumarole minerals (table)

| Mineral | Formula / type | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfur | S | H₂SO₄, gunpowder, vulcanization |
| Gypsum | CaSO₄·2H₂O | Plaster |
| Thenardite | Na₂SO₄ | Salt cake for roast flux |
| Alunite | KAl₃(SO₄)₂(OH)₆ | Alum historically |
| Opaline silica | SiO₂·nH₂O | Silica feed after calcine |
| Halite | NaCl | Salt, chloralkali |
| Sal ammoniac | NH₄Cl | Flux, smoke |

### Contact process note

**S + O₂ → SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄** with V₂O₅ catalyst. Island can supply **S**, **O₂** (from electrolysis), and **V** from slag. Catalyst and corrosion-resistant towers are the hard part.

## Phosphorus and Nitrogen

### Phosphorus sources

| Source | Status | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Guano | Historical NW Hawaiian islands; **now protected** | P₂O₅ fertilizer |
| Bones | Fish and livestock | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ after calcine |
| Apatite in basalt | Trace | Not economic |

**Phosphoric acid:** H₂SO₄ on bone ash or guano → **H₃PO₄**. **Superphosphate:** acid on rock phosphate.

### Elemental phosphorus (outline only)

**Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + C + SiO₂** in electric furnace → **white P (P₄)**. **White P is pyrophoric and poison.** Store under water. Red P is safer but still toxic. Do not run this without full hazard plan.

## Nickel, Cr, Co, Trace

### Nickel

Olivine in lava and green sand carries **0.2 to 0.4 wt% Ni** in crystal form, not as sulfide ore. Full hydromet, electrowinning, and alloy paths: **`Nickel.md`**.

### Chromium

Only trace **Cr-spinel** in nodules. **Not practical** for ferrochrome or stainless on island alone.

### Cobalt

Follows Ni in olivine leach and laterite if present at ppm levels. No dedicated Co ore.

### Cu / Zn / precious

Basalt ppm levels, no mineable sulfide bodies on ocean islands in this model. **Not extractable** without imports.

## Clays, Cement, Zeolites, Industrial Minerals

### Clay by island age

| Island age | Clay type | Pottery quality |
|---|---|---|
| Young | Allophane, imogolite from glass | Short, plastic when wet, cracks on fire without temper |
| Old | Kaolinite, halloysite, gibbsite | Better ceramics with grog temper |
| Lagoon mud | Fine silts | Brick if burned |

### Pozzolan cement

**Volcanic ash + lime + water** → hydraulic mortar (Roman style). Tuff and palagonite on rejuvenated cones are ideal.

### Portland-type

Need **limestone + clay + kiln at 1450°C**. Old island limestone plus ash/clay is feasible with fuel cost.

### Zeolites

Phillipsite, chabazite, clinoptilolite in vesicles and ash layers. Uses: **water softening, ammonia removal, animal feed, desiccant**.

| Coral sand | Limestone precursor, slow cement |
| Olivine | Foundry sand, Mg source, slow CO₂ weathering research |

## Cross-links and order of operations

Suggested tech ladder for a Dragnis-class island:

1. Collect and wash **magnetic sand** or **laterite** (`Sands.md`).
2. **Route H1** for iron powder and Ti residue.
3. Forge weld or crucible **cast iron**; charcoal **cementation** for steel.
4. Parallel: **salt → chloralkali → Cl₂** for Ti and Al (`TitaniumBootstrap.md`).
5. Upgrade Ti residue to chlorinator feed (`TitaniumProcessing.md`).
6. **Olivine → Mg, Ni** (`Nickel.md`).
7. **Old island lime + ash → cement and glass** (`Resources.md`).

Earth legal and cultural protection of beaches and parks is real; treat unrestricted loot tables as **fiction-facing design**, not a guide to taking material from Hawaiʻi today.
