### Volcano life cycle

| Stage | Lava type | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preshield | Alkalic basalt | Short | Submarine |
| Shield | Tholeiitic basalt | ~0.5 to 1 Ma | 95 to 98% volume |
| Postshield | Alkalic, hawaiite, mugearite, trachyte | Hundreds of kyr | Steeper, cinder cones |
| Erosional | None | Long | Valleys, cliffs, reefs |
| Rejuvenated | Nephelinite, basanite, alkalic | Brief | Diamond Head, Koko Head, Punchbowl |
| Atoll / guyot | None | Ma to tens of Ma | Sink, reef, drown |

### Typical tholeiite chemistry

| Oxide | Weight % |
|---|---|
| SiO₂ | 49 to 52 |
| TiO₂ | 2 to 3 |
| Al₂O₃ | 12 to 14 |
| FeO + Fe₂O₃ | 11 to 13 |
| MgO | 7 to 10 (higher in picrites) |
| CaO | 10 to 11 |
| Na₂O + K₂O | 2.5 to 3 |

About half of basalt is SiO₂ by weight but none is free quartz. Silica is locked in plagioclase, pyroxene, olivine and glass.

## Sand Types

### Black sand

- Sideromelane (fresh translucent brown glass), tachylite (opaque black glass), basalt fragments, olivine / plagioclase / pyroxene, minor magnetite and titanomagnetite.
- Lava enters ocean → hyaloclastite / littoral explosions; waves erode cliffs and flows.
- Soft vs quartz; alters to palagonite. Beaches last decades to centuries without new supply.
- Examples: Punaluʻu, Pohoiki (2018), Kehena, Richardson, Waipiʻo, Pololū, Waiʻānapanapa. Kaimū buried 1990; new black sand nearby.

### Green sand

Olivine concentrated by waves (density ~3.3). Papakōlea (Mahana Bay) from Puʻu Mahana tuff. Olivine streaks on many black beaches.

### White / tan (carbonate)

Coralline red algae (often largest share), coral, mollusk shell, Halimeda, foraminifera, echinoid bits. Parrotfish grind and excrete sand. Examples: Hāpuna, Kāʻanapali, Wailea, Lanikai, Kailua, Hanalei, Poʻipū.

### Red sand

Oxidized cinder / scoria. Kaihalulu (Hāna, Maui).

### Mixed and imported

Many beaches mix carbonate and volcanic (salt-and-pepper). Waikīkī has received offshore and (historically) California sand.

## Young, Middle, Old Islands

| Feature | Young (Big Island) | Middle (Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi) | Old (Oʻahu, Kauaʻi, Niʻihau) | Atolls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Activity | Active / dormant | Haleakalā dormant; others extinct | Extinct ± rejuvenated vents | None |
| Landscape | Shields, fresh lava | Partly eroded | Deep valleys, cliffs | Low reef islets |
| Reefs | Limited | Moderate | Extensive | Whole island |
| Beach sand | Black glass, olivine; few white | Mixed | White carbonate; black near streams | 100% carbonate |
| Soils | Thin andisols | Moderate | Thick red laterite (oxisols, ultisols) | Thin carbonate |
| Ti location | Fresh oxides | Mixed | Concentrated in weathered soils | None |
| Free silica | None | Minor secondary chalcedony / opal | Minor secondary silica | None |

### Weathering sequence (young → old)

1. Fresh basalt and glass
2. Glass → palagonite and clays (allophane, imogolite)
3. Olivine and pyroxene break first (Goldich)
4. Plagioclase → kaolinite / halloysite
5. Si, Ca, Mg, Na, K leach away
6. Fe oxides, gibbsite, Ti oxides accumulate
7. Red laterite and ferruginous bauxite

## Silica and Titanium in Hawaii

### Silica

- **Quartz:** essentially absent from native rock. Quartz in Hawaiian sand is almost always imported or human-derived.
- Trachyte (e.g. Puʻu Waʻawaʻa) is the most silica-rich Hawaiian lava and still yields negligible quartz.
- **Secondary:** chalcedony and opal in vesicles/cracks of weathered basalt; opaline crusts at fumaroles (Kīlauea sulfur banks); minor cristobalite / tridymite.
- Pele's hair / tears: basaltic glass, not silica minerals.
- Construction sand: mostly crushed basalt, permitted carbonate, imports, recycled glass.

### Titanium

- Fresh basalt: 2 to 3% TiO₂ in titanomagnetite, ilmenite, pyroxene (relatively Ti-rich vs many basalts).
- Black sand magnetic fraction: mostly titanomagnetite. Ilmenite minor. No mainland-style mineral sand grades.
- Old island soils: Fe-Al-Ti residues. Kauaʻi and East Maui ferruginous bauxite / laterite. Some surface horizons reported with double-digit % TiO₂. Investigated mid-1900s; never mined.
- Why no mining: grades below commercial sands, fine intergrown oxides, land/cultural/legal constraints, no local processing.

## Separating Hawaiian Sands

### Carbonate from volcanic

Density overlap (calcite 2.71, aragonite 2.93, glass 2.7 to 3.0, plagioclase 2.6 to 2.76).

- **Best:** dilute HCl (~10%) until fizz stops. CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Weigh before/after for % carbonate. Keep dilute and brief (strong acid attacks olivine/glass).
- Gentler: acetic acid. Flotation possible but acid is simpler at small scale.

### Magnetic / gravity

- LIMS: magnetite / titanomagnetite.
- Nd magnet: ilmenite, weak olivine, some pyroxene (mixed product).
- Olivine vs glass: gravity (3.2 to 3.4 vs ~2.8) or color sorting.
- Glass vs crystals: heavy liquid ~2.7 floats vesicular glass; microscope for bubbles / no cleavage.
- Quartz: nothing native to separate. Quartz present = imported material.

### Suggested lab sequence

1. Rinse salt, dry. Sieve and weigh fractions.
2. LIMS → weigh magnetic.
3. Dilute HCl on nonmagnetic → weigh carbonate loss.
4. Heavy liquid ~2.9 to 3.0: sink = olivine / pyroxene / heavies; float = glass / plagioclase.
5. Nd magnet on sink.
6. Microscope each fraction.

## Earth Hawaii Legal Status

- State law prohibits taking sand, dead coral, coral rubble, rocks or beach/marine deposits from beaches and nearshore waters (narrow exceptions).
- Commercial sand needs permits; limited to approved inland or offshore sources.
- National parks: no removing sand or rocks.
- Papakōlea and notable beaches protected under these rules.
- Agricultural import rules restrict soil and some materials into Hawaii.
