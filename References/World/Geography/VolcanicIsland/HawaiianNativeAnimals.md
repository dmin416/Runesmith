## 6. Native Animals (Arrived Without Humans)

Key: † extinct, (w) extinct in the wild, (E) endangered

Everything native reached Hawaii by flying, drifting on ocean currents, rafting on debris or riding in the mud and feathers of birds. That filter explains what is missing: no native land mammals besides bats, no amphibians, no land reptiles, no ants, no mosquitoes and no freshwater fish that could not survive the ocean.

### Land mammals

- **Hawaiian hoary bat ('ōpe'ape'a) (E):** about 5 oz, solitary, roosts in trees, eats moths and beetles. Found on all main islands, most common on the Big Island. State land mammal
- **Synemporion keana†:** a small extinct bat known only from fossil bones in caves. Disappeared after Polynesian arrival

### Marine mammals

- **Hawaiian monk seal (E):** about 1,600 left. One of the most endangered seals on Earth. Pups mostly in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands. Hunts eels, octopus and reef fish on the seafloor
- **Humpback whale:** thousands migrate from Alaska every winter (December to April) to breed and calve in warm shallow water
- **Hawaiian insular false killer whale (E):** a resident population of about 150 to 200
- **Spinner dolphin:** rests in shallow bays by day and hunts deep water at night
- **Others:** bottlenose dolphin, pantropical spotted dolphin, rough-toothed dolphin, Risso's dolphin, melon-headed whale, pygmy killer whale, short-finned pilot whale, sperm whale, Blainville's beaked whale, Cuvier's beaked whale, dwarf and pygmy sperm whales, Bryde's whale, fin whale, sei whale, minke whale

### Extinct birds known from fossils

These vanished mostly within a few centuries of Polynesian arrival. Rats, pigs, dogs, hunting and lowland forest clearing all contributed.

- **Moa-nalo†:** giant flightless ducks with heavy turtle-like beaks. They filled the role of deer and tortoises as the main large browsers
 - O'ahu moa-nalo†
 - Maui Nui large-billed moa-nalo† (Maui, Moloka'i, Lāna'i)
 - Small-billed moa-nalo† (Maui)
 - Turtle-jawed moa-nalo† (Kaua'i)
- **Giant Hawaii goose†:** Big Island. Huge, nearly flightless
- **Nēnē-nui†:** Maui. A larger, more terrestrial relative of the nēnē
- **Hawaiian eagle†:** a relative of the white-tailed sea eagle. Top predator on O'ahu, Moloka'i and Maui
- **Stilt-owls†:** four species, one each on Kaua'i, O'ahu, Moloka'i and Maui. Long legs and short wings for chasing birds through the forest understory
- **Flightless ibises†:** Moloka'i and Maui. Probed leaf litter for snails and insects
- **Flightless rails†:** at least 10 species, roughly one or more per island
 - Hawai'i rail† (survived until 1884)
 - Laysan rail† (survived until 1944)
- **Two extra crow species†:** on O'ahu and Moloka'i
- **Kaua'i finch† and several other fossil honeycreepers**

### Forest birds

#### Hawaiian honeycreepers

One finch ancestor arrived about 5 to 7 million years ago. It split into more than 50 species with bills shaped for nectar, seeds, insects, fruit and wood. This group is one of the clearest examples of adaptive radiation anywhere. Mosquito-borne avian malaria now confines most survivors to high elevations.

**Nectar feeders**
- 'I'iwi: scarlet with a long curved bill for lobelia flowers. Threatened
- 'Apapane: crimson. The most common surviving honeycreeper
- 'Ākohekohe (crested honeycreeper) (E): Maui only
- Hawai'i mamo†
- Black mamo† (Moloka'i)

**Generalists**
- Hawai'i 'amakihi, O'ahu 'amakihi, Kaua'i 'amakihi: yellow-green. The most disease-tolerant species
- 'Anianiau: Kaua'i only
- Greater 'amakihi†

**Insect gleaners and bark probers**
- 'Akiapōlā'au (E): Big Island. Uses a short lower bill to chisel bark and a long curved upper bill to extract grubs, filling the woodpecker role
- Hawai'i 'akepa (E): Big Island. Twisted bill tips for prying open buds
- Maui 'akepa†
- O'ahu 'akepa†
- 'Akeke'e (E): Kaua'i
- 'Akikiki (Kaua'i creeper): close to extinction, with the wild population nearly gone
- 'Alauahio (Maui creeper) (E)
- Hawai'i creeper (E)
- Kākāwahie (Moloka'i creeper)†
- Kaua'i nukupu'u†
- Maui nukupu'u†
- O'ahu nukupu'u†
- Kaua'i 'akialoa†
- O'ahu 'akialoa†
- Hawai'i 'akialoa†
- Lāna'i 'akialoa†

**Seed and fruit eaters**
- Palila (E): Big Island. Depends almost entirely on māmane tree seeds on Mauna Kea
- Kiwikiu (Maui parrotbill) (E): fewer than 200 left
- 'Ō'ū†: fig-like fruit specialist
- Lāna'i hookbill†

- Nihoa finch (E): survives on Nihoa

**Other**
- Po'ouli†: Maui. Ate snails. Discovered in 1973. The last known bird died in 2004

#### Hawaiian honeyeaters ('ō'ō family)

An entire bird family that is now completely extinct. Black with yellow feather tufts that were used for royal cloaks.

- Kaua'i 'ō'ō†: last heard in 1987
- O'ahu 'ō'ō†
- Bishop's 'ō'ō† (Moloka'i)
- Hawai'i 'ō'ō†
- Kioea†

#### Other forest birds

- **'Alalā (Hawaiian crow) (w):** gone from the wild since 2002. Captive breeding continues. A key seed disperser for large-fruited native plants
- **'Io (Hawaiian hawk):** Big Island. The only surviving native hawk
- **Pueo (Hawaiian short-eared owl):** hunts by day. May have become established only after Polynesian rats provided prey
- **'Elepaio:** three species (Hawai'i, O'ahu, Kaua'i). Flycatchers. Featured in Hawaiian canoe-building lore
- **Thrushes**
 - 'Ōma'o: Big Island
 - Puaiohi (E): Kaua'i
 - Kāma'o (large Kaua'i thrush)†
 - 'Āmaui (O'ahu thrush)†
 - Oloma'o (Moloka'i and Lāna'i)†
- **Millerbirds**

### Water birds

- **Nēnē (Hawaiian goose):** state bird. Descended from Canada geese. Down to about 30 birds in the 1950s. Now over 3,000
- **Koloa (Hawaiian duck) (E):** threatened by hybridization with feral mallards
- **Laysan duck (E):** fossils show it once lived on the main islands. Now survives on Laysan, Midway and Kure
- **'Alae ke'oke'o (Hawaiian coot)**
- **'Alae 'ula (Hawaiian gallinule):** in legend, brought fire to humans
- **Ae'o (Hawaiian stilt):** long pink legs
- **'Auku'u (black-crowned night heron)**

### Seabirds

Before people, seabird colonies covered the main islands from beaches to mountain slopes. Their guano was a major fertilizer for inland forests.

**Albatrosses**
- Laysan albatross: Midway holds the largest colony on Earth. The oldest known wild bird, "Wisdom," still breeds there in her 70s
- Black-footed albatross
- Short-tailed albatross: rare visitor at Midway

**Petrels and shearwaters**
- 'Ua'u (Hawaiian petrel) (E): nests in burrows near the summit of Haleakalā
- 'A'o (Newell's shearwater) (E): nests on Kaua'i. Fledglings get disoriented by artificial lights
- Wedge-tailed shearwater ('ua'u kani)
- Christmas shearwater
- Bulwer's petrel
- Bonin petrel
- Band-rumped storm petrel (E)
- Tristram's storm petrel

**Boobies, frigatebirds and tropicbirds**

- Masked booby
- Great frigatebird ('iwa): steals food from other seabirds in flight
- Red-tailed tropicbird (koa'e 'ula)

**Terns and noddies**

### Reptiles

- **Green sea turtle (honu):** grazes seaweed on reefs and basks on beaches. Most nesting happens at French Frigate Shoals
- **Hawksbill turtle (honu'ea) (E):** fewer than 150 nesting females. Nests on the Big Island, Maui and Moloka'i
- **Visitors:** leatherback, olive ridley and loggerhead turtles pass through offshore
- **Yellow-bellied sea snake:** rare drifter
- **No native land reptiles**

### Freshwater animals

All native stream animals spend their larval stage at sea and climb back up streams as juveniles.

**Fish ('o'opu)**
- 'O'opu nākea: the largest native goby
- 'O'opu 'alamo'o: climbs waterfalls hundreds of feet high using a suction disc formed from its fused pelvic fins
- 'O'opu nōpili: also a waterfall climber. Scrapes algae off rocks
- 'O'opu naniha: stays in lower stream reaches
- 'O'opu 'akupa: a sleeper. Predator of the lower stream
- Āholehole (Hawaiian flagtail): moves between ocean and stream mouths

**Invertebrates**
- 'Ōpae kala'ole: stream shrimp that climbs waterfalls
- 'Ōpae 'oeha'a: native river prawn
- Hīhīwai: stream snail. Traditional food
- Hapawai: brackish-water snail
- Native damselfly larvae

**Anchialine pools** (brackish pools in lava connected underground to the sea)
- 'Ōpae 'ula: tiny red shrimp. Can live 20 years or longer
- Metabetaeus lohena: predatory shrimp that eats 'ōpae 'ula

### Marine life

### Land invertebrates

Hawaii has roughly 5,000 to 10,000 native insect species. Nearly all are found nowhere else. Many lineages evolved from a single arrival.

**Insects**
- **Kamehameha butterfly:** state insect. Larvae feed on native māmaki
- **Blackburn's blue butterfly:** the only other native butterfly
- **Blackburn's sphinx moth (E):** large hawkmoth. Larvae feed on native 'aiea
- **Hawaiian Drosophila:** about 1,000 species, roughly a quarter of all known fruit fly species. Includes about 100 picture-wing flies with patterned wings and elaborate courtship displays
- **Carnivorous inchworms:** about 20 species of caterpillars that stand still on leaves and snap up flies that touch them. Behavior known almost nowhere else
- **Hyposmocoma moths:** over 350 species. Some larvae live underwater. One species attacks and eats snails
- **Yellow-faced bees:** about 60 species. The only native bees. Several are endangered
- **Pinao (giant Hawaiian dragonfly):** largest dragonfly in the United States
- **Megalagrion damselflies:** about 23 species. Some have larvae that live on land in leaf litter, an extremely rare trait
- **Laupala crickets:** among the fastest-speciating animals known
- **Koa bug:** shield bug. Larger and more colorful than most relatives
- **Plagithmysus longhorn beetles:** wood borers. Many specialize on one tree species
- **Rhyncogonus weevils:** mostly flightless
- **Flightless ground beetles:** hundreds of species that lost flight after arriving
- **Hawaiian lacewings**
- **Nesophrosyne leafhoppers:** dozens of species
- **Kaua'i cave planthopper:** eyeless. Feeds on tree roots in lava tubes
- **Wēkiu bug:** lives only on the frozen summit of Mauna Kea. Feeds on insects blown up the mountain that freeze on the snow

**Spiders**
- **Happy-face spider:** translucent body with patterns resembling smiling faces
- **Spiny-leg Tetragnatha spiders:** one lineage split into dozens of species that evolved similar forms independently on separate islands
- **Kaua'i cave wolf spider (E):** eyeless. Found only in a few lava tubes in Kōloa

**Other arthropods**
- **Kaua'i cave amphipod (E):** eyeless. Feeds on decaying roots
- **Native centipedes and millipedes**
- **Native pseudoscorpions**

**Land snails**

Over 750 native species once existed. This was among the richest land snail faunas on Earth. Most are now extinct.

- **O'ahu tree snails (Achatinella):** about 40 species. Colorful shells used in lei. Most are extinct. "Lonely George," the last of his species, died in 2019
- **Partulina, Newcombia, Perdicella:** tree snails on other islands
- **Amastrid snails:** ground-dwelling. Nearly all extinct
- **Succineid snails**
- **Endodontid snails:** tiny. Nearly all extinct
