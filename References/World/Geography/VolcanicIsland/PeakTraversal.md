## 1. The Terrain Problems

### 1.1 Olympic Peninsula: Peak to Peak Across a Valley

The Olympic Mountains are a compact dome of steep, glacier-carved peaks with deep U-shaped valleys between them.

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Mount Olympus summit | 2,432 m (7,980 ft) |
| Hurricane Ridge (visitor area) | ~1,600 m (5,240 ft) |
| Hoh River valley floor near the rainforest | ~180 m (600 ft) |
| Typical relief from ridge crest to valley floor | 1,200 to 2,000 m |
| Precipitation, west slopes (Olympus massif) | 4,000 to 5,000+ mm/yr water equivalent, mostly snow up high |
| Precipitation, Sequim (rain shadow, NE) | ~400 mm/yr |

**Working scenario used for the math below:** two summits at roughly 2,000 to 2,400 m, separated by 3 to 6 km of horizontal distance, with a valley floor at 300 to 600 m. That geometry matches the Bailey Range, the Olympus massif and the Hurricane Ridge to Elwha/Lillian drainages.

**Climate drivers that matter for every method:**

- Pacific storms arrive from the southwest October through April. Winter gusts on exposed crests like Hurricane Ridge regularly exceed 100 km/h. The ridge is named for them.

- Summer brings a marine layer that fills the lowlands and valleys with cloud while summits sit in sun. Cloud base and fog are a constant visibility limiter.

- Freezing level drops below the summits for most of the year. Rime ice coats exposed structures.

- Afternoon sea breezes push west-to-east down the Strait of Juan de Fuca and northerly onshore flow hits the north faces of the front ranges.

### 1.2 Hawaiʻi: Summit to Coast

| Summit | Elevation | Nearest practical coast | Straight-line distance | Average gradient |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mauna Kea | 4,207 m (13,803 ft) | Hāmākua coast (NE) | ~30 km | ~14% (8°) |
| Mauna Kea | 4,207 m | Hilo | ~42 km | ~10% (5.7°) |
| Mauna Kea | 4,207 m | Kawaihae (W, leeward) | ~45 km | ~9.3% (5.3°) |
| Mauna Loa | 4,169 m | Kona or Kaʻū coasts | 40 to 50 km | 8 to 10% |
| Haleakalā | 3,055 m (10,023 ft) | Kaupō (S) | ~15 km | ~20% (11°) |
| Haleakalā | 3,055 m | Kīhei (W) | ~21 km | ~14.5% (8.3°) |

**Atmosphere at the summit of Mauna Kea:**

- Pressure ~600 hPa, about 59% of sea level. Oxygen partial pressure drops by the same fraction. Hypoxia is a real factor for anyone exerting at the top.

- Air density ~0.80 kg/m³ versus 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level (about 65%).

- Northeast trade winds blow 20 to 40 km/h below a **trade-wind inversion** at roughly 1,800 to 2,400 m. The summit usually9ٹ7y sits above the inversion in dry, stable air. The windward (NE) slopes below the inversion carry a persistent orographic cloud band from roughly 600 to 2,000 m.

- Gap winds accelerate through the Waimea saddle between Kohala and Mauna Kea.

## 2. Core Physics That Governs Every Method

### 2.1 Energy Budget

Gravitational potential energy: **E = m·g·h**

| Case (90 kg person + gear) | Energy |
|---|---|
| Climb 1,000 m | 0.88 MJ mechanical. At ~22% human efficiency: ~4.0 MJ metabolic (~950 kcal) |
| Climb 2,000 m (valley floor back up to peak) | 1.77 MJ mechanical, ~1,900 kcal metabolic |
| Descend Mauna Kea summit to sea (4,207 m) | 3.71 MJ (~1.03 kWh) that must be dissipated as heat or recovered |
| Descend Haleakalā (3,055 m) | 2.70 MJ (~0.75 kWh) |

Every descending method is really a method of **disposing of energy safely**. Every peak-to-peak method that loses altitude in the middle must **buy the altitude back** with muscle, fuel, rising air or stored momentum.

### 2.2 Glide Ratio Versus Terrain Gradient

A glider descending at glide ratio G covers G meters forward for each meter lost. Its glide path gradient is 1/G.

| Craft | Glide ratio | Glide path gradient |
|---|---|---|
| Wingsuit | 2.5 to 3.5 | 29 to 40% |
| Ram-air skydiving canopy | 2.5 to 3 | 33 to 40% |
| Speed wing | 3 to 5 | 20 to 33% |
| Paraglider (intermediate) | 8 to 9 | 11 to 12.5% |
| Paraglider (competition) | 11 to 12 | 8 to 9% |
| Flex-wing hang glider | 12 to 15 | 6.7 to 8.3% |
| Rigid-wing hang glider | 18 to 20 | 5 to 5.6% |
| 15 m sailplane | 40 to 45 | 2.2 to 2.5% |
| Open-class sailplane | 60 to 70 | 1.4 to 1.7% |

**Key rule:** if the glide path gradient is steeper than the average terrain gradient, the craft hits the mountainside before reaching the coast in still air. This single comparison decides most of the Hawaiian options in Section 5.

### 2.3 Wind Changes Effective Glide

Effective glide ratio over the ground = G × (ground speed / airspeed).

Example: paraglider, G = 9, trim airspeed 37 km/h:

- 20 km/h headwind → ground speed 17 km/h → effective glide **4.1:1**

- 20 km/h tailwind → ground speed 57 km/h → effective glide **13.9:1**

### 2.4 Thin Air at Altitude

For the same lift, true airspeed scales with **1/√ρ**. At Mauna Kea summit density (0.80 kg/m³) every wing must fly about **24% faster** than at sea level. Foot launches need a longer, faster run. Landing speeds rise. Rotor and propeller hover power rises by the same factor because ideal hover power is P = √(T³ / 2ρA).

### 2.5 Mountain Wind Phenomena

- **Ridge (slope) lift:** wind striking a slope is deflected upward. Vertical component ≈ U·sin(α). A 10 m/s wind on a 30° slope gives up to ~5 m/s of lift near the face. The usable band extends to roughly 1 to 2 times ridge height above the crest.

- **Lee sink and rotor:** downwind of a crest the air descends and can tumble in horizontal-axis rotors. Rotor turbulence can exceed the structural or controllability limits of light aircraft.

- **Mountain (lee) waves:** stable air flowing over a ridge oscillates downstream. The Froude number Fr = U / (N·h) predicts behavior (U = wind speed, N = Brunt-Väisälä frequency ~0.01 s⁻¹, h = ridge height). With U = 20 m/s and h = 1,500 m, Fr ≈ 1.3: right in the band for strong waves. Wave lift can exceed 5 m/s and extend many kilometers above the summits.

- **Venturi and gap acceleration:** wind compressed over a crest or through a saddle accelerates. Summit gusts commonly run 1.5 to 2 times the free-air speed.

- **Valley winds:** daytime heating drives upslope (anabatic) flow; night cooling drives downslope (katabatic) drainage into the valley floor.

### 2.6 Cable Mechanics (Zip Lines, Trams, Bridges)

For a taut cable with horizontal tension H, span L and weight per meter w, the mid-span sag is approximately:

**f ≈ w·L² / (8·H)**

Rewriting with the material's breaking length (the length of cable that would snap under its own weight, B = breaking force / w) and a design9ٹRר factor DF:

**f / L ≈ L · DF / (8·B)**

This is the governing law of giant spans: **sag ratio grows linearly with span** and a thicker cable does not help because thickness raises both strength and weight equally. Only a better strength-to-weight material reduces it.

| Material | Approximate breaking length |
|---|---|
| Steel wire rope (6×19 IWRC) | ~17 km |
| High-strength steel wire (bare) | ~23 km |
| Aramid (Kevlar) rope | ~100+ km |
| UHMWPE (Dyneema/Spectra) rope | ~150 to 200 km (fiber ~370 km) |
| Carbon fiber tow | ~200+ km |

## 3. Olympic Peninsula: Peak-to-Peak Methods

### 3.1 Massive Zip Line

**Reference points:** the longest single zip line span on record is at Jebel Jais (UAE) at about 2.83 km. The longest unsupported gondola span (Whistler Peak 2 Peak) is about 3.03 km. A 3 to 6 km Olympic crossing is at or beyond the frontier.

**Cable sizing worked example.** One-inch (26 mm) steel wire rope: mass ~2.7 kg/m (w ≈ 26.5 N/m), breaking force ~460 kN. Allowable horizontal tension H = 150 kN (design factor ~3).

| Span | Mid-span sag | Sag ratio | End tension | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 km | ~22 m | 2.2% | ~151 kN | Routine engineering |
| 3 km | ~200 m | 6.6% | ~160 kN | Record class but buildable |
| 5 km | ~550 m | 11% | ~165 kN+ | Huge belly; parabolic approximation weakening |
| 10 km | would need H ≈ 660 kN for 500 m sag | n/a | exceeds 460 kN breaking force | Impossible in steel |

**The peak-to-peak trap.** A zip line runs on gravity alone. Energy conservation means the rider can never arrive higher than the start minus friction and drag losses. With two summits of similar height, the rider coasts down into the cable's belly and rolls back. Requirements for reliable arrival:

- Destination anchor lower than the start by roughly 6 to 12% of span (240 to 480 m over 4 km).

- Or a two-stage design: zip down from peak A to a valley midstation, then a powered lift or a second line from a high anchor to peak B.

- Or a powered return: motorized trolley or haul cable (at which point it is an aerial ropeway).

**Rider speed.** Force balance along the cable:

m·g·sinθ − C_rr·m·g·cosθ − ½·ρ·C_dA·v² = 0

Terminal speed: **v = √[2·m·g·(sinθ − C_rr·cosθ) / (ρ·C_dA)]**

Values: m = 90 kg, C_rr = 0.01 (trolley rolling resistance), ρ = 1.05 kg/m³ (~1,800 m), C_dA = 0.6 m² seated, 0.3 m² prone (head-first).

| Cable angle | Seated terminal speed | Prone terminal speed |
|---|---|---|
| 5° | 14.7 m/s (53 km/h) | 20.8 m/s (75 km/h) |
| 10° | 21.4 m/s (77 km/h) | 30.3 m/s (109 km/h) |
| 15° | 26.4 m/s (95 km/h) | 37.4 m/s (135 km/h) |

A 4 km ride at those speeds takes roughly 2.5 to 5 minutes. Cable angle varies along the catenary: steepest at the top anchor and flattening toward the belly.

**Hurricane Ridge wind effects on a zip line:**

- **Headwind:** a 20 m/s (72 km/h) headwind pushes a seated rider at standstill with ½·1.05·0.6·400 ≈ 126 N. On a 5° cable the gravity drive is only ~68 N. The rider stops mid-span or slides backward. On 10° (~145 N drive) the rider crawls. Storm gusts make one-direction service impossible on many winter days.

- **Tailwind:** adds speed and kinetic energy at the brake. At 30 m/s a 90 kg rider carries 40.5 kJ.

- **Crosswind:** 126 N lateral against 883 N weight swings the rider ~8° off vertical, with gust-driven pendulum oscillation.

- **Cable vibration:** steady wind sheds vortices off the cable (aeolian vibration, tens of Hz) that fatigue the wire at the clamps. Iced cables can **gallop** in low-frequency, large-amplitude waves.

- **Icing:** 25 mm of radial rime on a 26 mm cable adds ~3.6 kg/m of ice. That more than doubles w (2.7 → 6.3 kg/m), roughly doubling sag at constant tension or doubling tension at constant sag.

**Braking.** Stopping distance on an uphill runout: h = v²/2g. At 30 m/s that is a 46 m climb. Practical systems use eddy-current magnetic brakes, spring or bungee arrestors and counterweighted capture blocks.

**Installation sequence.** The first line across a deep valley is the hardest step. Options: helicopter carry, drone with a light pilot line, line-throwing launcher (a few hundred meters) or a kite. A kite flown across the Niagara Gorge in 1848 carried the first line for the Niagara suspension bridge. Pilot line pulls a cord, cord pulls a rope, rope pulls the steel.

**Synthetic cable option.** Dyneema's breaking length cuts sag dramatically (a 6 km span becomes structurally mild). Problems: trolley friction heat (UHMWPE softens near 145 to 150 °C), creep under sustained tension, UV degradation and abrasion. A practical giant span would likely use a synthetic load-bearing carrier with a steel running track or a steel track rope sized for the trolley contact only.

**Benefits:** fastest pure-gravity crossing (minutes), no pilot skill for the rider, repeatable, spectacular, low energy per trip once built.

**Limiting factors:** one-way only, destination must be lower, multi-kilometer spans approach material limits, storm winds close it most of the winter, icing, anchoring in fractured sedimentary rock, construction access, maintenance and inspection over a valley, legal status of the land.

### 3.2 Zip Down, Lift Up (Two-Stage Hybrid)

Zip from peak A to a valley midstation, then ride a powered lift (gondola, chairlift or motorized trolley) up to peak B.

- Lift energy for 90 kg over 1,800 m: 1.59 MJ ≈ 0.44 kWh per passenger before drive losses.

- Turns an impossible gravity problem into a solved engineering one.

**Benefits:** works with peaks of equal height; the descent portion is free. **Limits:** two full installations, doubled cost, still closed in high wind.

### 3.3 Aerial Tramway or 3S Gondola

A reversible tram (two cabins on haul rope, counterbalanced) or a tricable detachable gondola (3S) running peak to peak.

- Counterbalancing means only the net load difference and friction need drive power.

- Bicable and tricable systems tolerate crosswinds far better than single-rope systems. 3S gondolas typically operate in winds that would close a monocable lift.

- Towers in the valley shorten spans to buildable lengths. A clear single span is possible to ~3 km with current technology.

**Benefits:** bidirectional, weather-tolerant compared to riders on trolleys, carries gear and the injured, all ages.

**Limits:** tens to hundreds of millions of dollars, permanent towers in the valley, rime ice on haul ropes, lightning, operating crews, closure thresholds still reached in winter storms.

### 3.4 Pedestrian Suspension Bridge

Current records: Sky Bridge 721 (Czech Republic) at 721 m and 516 Arouca (Portugal) at 516 m.

- Deck sag ratio typically 1/10 to 1/15 of span. A 4 km footbridge would sag 250 to 400 m: walking down into it and climbing back out.

- Light pedestrian decks are vulnerable to aerodynamic flutter. The 1940 Tacoma Narrows Bridge failure is the canonical case: torsional flutter at only ~64 km/h wind. Hurricane Ridge storm winds would require heavy damping, stay cables or open-grating decks.

- Pedestrian-induced lateral sway (synchronous lock-in, as at London's Millennium Bridge in 2000) increases with span flexibility.

**Benefits:** bidirectional, no moving parts, walkable by anyone. **Limits:** practical spans under 1 km today, wind flutter, ice and snow load, enormous anchorages.

### 3.5 Tyrolean Traverse and Highline

Mountaineering technique: a fixed rope tensioned between anchors; the climber hangs and pulls hand over hand. Highliners walk a tensioned webbing line.

- Limited by rope length (60 to 100 m standard) and the force required to haul across a sagging span.

- Multi-kilometer highlines exist using Dyneema webbing but require days of rigging.

**Benefits:** lightweight, no infrastructure. **Limits:** short spans, very slow, physically exhausting, wind makes long lines unwalkable.

### 3.6 Foot Travel (Descend, Cross, Ascend)

The standard way. Down 1,500 to 2,000 m, ford the river, climb back up.

- Metabolic cost: ~1,900 kcal for the climb alone (90 kg, 2,000 m).

- Typical pace: descent 400 to 600 m/hr, ascent 300 to 500 m/hr in trail-less terrain. Realistic crossing time: 1 to 3 days in the Olympic interior due to brush, cliffs and glaciers.

- Glacial rivers (Hoh, Elwha, Queets) run cold, fast and opaque. Snowmelt peaks in late spring and early summer afternoons.

**Benefits:** cheapest, legal everywhere on foot, no weather minimums beyond safety. **Limits:** time, exhaustion, dense temperate rainforest brush (devil's club, slide alder), crevasses, rockfall, river crossings.

### 3.7 Ski Mountaineering

Skin up, ski down. Winter or spring snowpack covers brush and smooths terrain.

- Descent fast (thousands of meters per hour on good snow); ascent on skins 300 to 500 m/hr.

- Maritime "Cascade concrete" snowpack is heavy and wet; avalanche cycles follow storms and rain-on-snow events.

**Benefits:** faster than summer foot travel, brush buried. **Limits:** avalanche hazard, whiteout, short winter daylight, valley floors often below snowline (rain and mud on the lower legs).

### 3.8 Paraglider

**Performance:** glide 8 to 9:1, trim airspeed 36 to 40 km/h, top speed 50 to 60 km/h with speed bar, minimum sink ~1.0 m/s.

**Crossing math (still air):** 4 km gap at 9:1 loses ~445 m. Peaks of equal height mean arriving 445 m below the target summit. Arrival requires climbing back in ridge lift or thermals on peak B.

**Wind geometry that actually works:**

1. Wind must blow **from A toward B**.

2. Launch from A's windward face (the side facing away from B), climb in ridge lift above A's summit.

3. Cross downwind over A's crest and the valley. Expect lee sink behind A; cross high to stay above the rotor zone.

4. Arrive at B's windward face (the side facing A), climb in its ridge lift and top-land.

Crossing upwind toward B means arriving on B's lee side in sink and rotor: the most dangerous configuration in mountain flying.

**Hurricane Ridge storm winds:** at 50 to 100 km/h the wind exceeds the paraglider's top speed. The wing flies backward, cannot penetrate and is vulnerable to collapse in turbulence. Practical paraglider wind limit at launch is roughly 25 to 30 km/h. Paragliding at Hurricane Ridge is a calm-day or gentle sea-breeze activity, not a storm activity.

**Hurricane Ridge to Port Angeles:** ~1,600 m drop over ~17.5 km horizontal = 9.1% gradient. An intermediate paraglider at 9:1 (11.1%) falls short in still air. A competition wing or any lift along the route makes it.

**Benefits:** packs into a backpack (~5 to 8 kg), lands almost anywhere flat, quiet, cheap. **Limits:** strict wind window, valley rotor, sudden cloud, no landing options in old-growth forest, marine layer obscuring valley landing zones.

### 3.9 Hang Glider

**Performance:** glide 12 to 15:1 (flex wing) or 18 to 20:1 (rigid), speed range ~25 to 100+ km/h.

- Penetrates winds up to ~40 km/h usefully. Ridge-soars stronger conditions than a paraglider.

- 4 km crossing at 15:1 costs only ~270 m.

- Hurricane Ridge to Port Angeles: reaches easily (15:1 = 6.7% versus 9.1% terrain).

**Benefits:** much better glide and speed than a paraglider, more turbulence tolerance from a rigid structure. **Limits:** 25 to 45 kg glider requiring vehicle transport, needs a cleared steep launch ramp, needs a larger landing field, top-landing on a narrow summit is advanced.

### 3.10 Sailplane (Wave Soaring)

**Performance:** glide 40 to 70:1, cruise 100 to 200 km/h, never-exceed speed ~250+ km/h.

- The only unpowered aircraft that thrives in Hurricane Ridge storm winds. Southwesterly flow over the Olympics generates mountain waves. Wave lift can carry a sailplane far above the summits.

- Launch by aerotow from a lowland airport (Port Angeles is near sea level). No summit launch is realistic.

- A 4 km valley crossing at 50:1 costs only 80 m.

**Benefits:** handles strong wind, enormous range, can cross the whole range. **Limits:** cannot land on a peak, so it overflies rather than traverses peak-to-peak; rotor turbulence beneath waves is violent; icing in cloud; oxygen required above ~3,800 m in wave.

### 3.11 Wingsuit with Parachute

**Performance:** glide 2.5 to 3.5:1, horizontal speed 150 to 200 km/h, vertical speed ~12 to 18 m/s.

- 4 km crossing at 3:1 loses ~1,330 m. Landing on an equal peak is physically impossible. Peak A to valley floor or peak A to a much lower bench is possible.

- Ridge lift rarely exceeds 5 to 10 m/s, below wingsuit sink rate. No sustained climb.

- Proximity-flying parachute deployment typically 150 to 300 m above ground.

**Benefits:** fastest unpowered human flight, minimal gear. **Limits:** one-way down only, extreme fatality rate in proximity flight, needs a cliff exit steep enough to build speed, wind and turbulence near terrain, no landing area in dense forest.

### 3.12 Speed Flying (Mini-Wing)

A small, fast paraglider (8 to 14 m²), glide 3 to 5:1, airspeed 50 to 100 km/h.

- Flies in much stronger wind than a paraglider; descends a mountain in minutes, often skimming slopes on skis (speed riding).

- Cannot climb meaningfully. Peak to valley only.

**Benefits:** tolerates stronger wind, fast descent, compact. **Limits:** high-energy crashes, terrain proximity, no peak-to-peak.

### 3.13 Powered Paraglider and Ultralight

Engine on the back (paramotor) or ultralight trike.

- Climb rates 1 to 4 m/s. Climbing 2,000 m takes 10 to 30 minutes.

- Turns the zip line's fatal flaw (cannot gain altitude) into a non-issue.

- Still bound by the paraglider wind limit for paramotors.

**Benefits:** independent of lift, true peak-to-peak capability. **Limits:** noise, fuel, engine failure over forest with no landing zone, top-landing on peaks is hard, wind limits, airspace and land rules.

### 3.14 Helicopter

- 2,400 m is well within hover performance for most helicopters. Summit landings are routine for mountain rescue.

- Lee rotor and wind shear near ridges are the main hazards. Winter storm winds ground most operations.

- Charter cost runs from about one to several thousand dollars per flight hour.

**Benefits:** true peak-to-peak in minutes, carries gear and casualties, bidirectional. **Limits:** cost, weather, noise, landing-site availability, land-use restrictions.

### 3.15 Hot Air or Gas Balloon

- Lift per cubic meter (hot air) ≈ ρ_outside · (1 − T_outside / T_inside). Roughly 0.25 to 0.30 kg/m³ at sea level with ~100 °C envelope air.

- Steering exists only by changing altitude to find different wind layers.

**Benefits:** silent, scenic, needs no runway. **Limits:** no directional control between two specific points, mountain winds unpredictable, landing on a summit is effectively impossible, forest landings are hazardous.

### 3.16 Jet Suit and Jetpack

- Turbine jet suits (arm and back mounted) reach 80+ km/h with flight times of a few minutes. A 4 km hop takes ~3 minutes at 80 km/h.

- Peroxide rocket belts (1960s) flew ~20 to 30 seconds.

**Benefits:** genuine point-to-point flight including climbing. **Limits:** fuel load, very short endurance, extreme cost, wind sensitivity, catastrophic failure modes over a valley.

### 3.17 eVTOL and Passenger Drones

Electric multirotor or lift-plus-cruise aircraft.

- Hover power P = √(T³ / 2ρA). At 2,400 m density drops ~21%, raising hover power ~12%.

- Endurance of current battery multicopters with a passenger: roughly 15 to 30 minutes.

**Benefits:** precise point-to-point, autonomous potential, quiet compared to helicopters. **Limits:** battery energy density, cold-weather battery derating, wind gust rejection, certification, landing-site rules.

### 3.18 Person-Lifting Kite

Kite lift L = ½·ρ·V²·S·C_L. With V = 15 m/s, ρ = 1.1, C_L = 0.8: ~99 N per m². Lifting ~1,100 N (person plus rigging) needs ~11 m² of effective area plus line and kite weight. Early 1900s Cody war kites lifted observers.

- A tethered kite cannot cross a valley by itself. It can pass the pilot line for a zip line or ropeway.

**Benefits:** wind-powered, historical precedent. **Limits:** tethered to the ground, entirely wind-dependent.

### 3.19 Tunnel, Funicular, Cog Railway

- **Geology:** the Olympic core is folded and sheared marine sedimentary rock (sandstone, siltstone, shale, turbidites) scraped off the subducting plate and uplifted. A horseshoe of Crescent Formation basalt wraps the north, east and south flanks. Expect heavy water inflow, squeezing shale and fault gouge.

- **Funicular:** the steepest operating funicular (Stoos, Switzerland) climbs ~110% grade (~48°).

- **Cog railway:** Pilatus Railway reaches 48% grade.

- Combination: funicular down from A, tunnel or rail across the valley, funicular up to B.

**Benefits:** weatherproof, bidirectional, high capacity. **Limits:** cost (tens to hundreds of millions of dollars per km), years of construction, massive environmental footprint.

### 3.20 Theoretical and Extreme Concepts

**Catapult or trebuchet launch:**

- Ballistic range (no drag) R = v²·sin(2θ)/g. For a 4 km range at 45°: v ≈ 198 m/s (713 km/h).

- Accelerating to 198 m/s over 10 m demands ~1,960 m/s² ≈ **200 g**. Fatal. Over 100 m it is still ~20 g.

- Air drag on a human at 198 m/s decelerates at ~12 g, so true launch speed would need to be far higher.

- The human cannonball record is about 59 m.

- Workable only as an unmanned cargo pod with a parachute (an artillery round with a soft landing).

**Rocket hop:** a capsule with a solid motor and parachute. Physically feasible (crew escape systems do similar things) but violent and absurdly expensive per trip.

**Fulton skyhook:** a balloon-raised line snatched by a passing aircraft. Historically used to pull people off the ground into an airplane. Transfers a person from peak A into an aircraft but delivery to peak B still needs a parachute or landing.

**Tethered airship shuttle:** helium airship moored at both peaks, winching itself along a ground cable. Wind loading on the envelope (huge drag area) makes it fail in exactly the Hurricane Ridge conditions that make the region interesting.

## 5. Hawaiʻi: Summit-to-Coast Methods

### 5.1 The Deciding Comparison

| Craft | Glide path gradient | Mauna Kea to Hāmākua (14%) | Mauna Kea to Kawaihae (9.3%) | Haleakalā to Kaupō (20%) | Haleakalā to Kīhei (14.5%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Average gradients hide local detail. Upper cones are steeper than lower flanks, so real profiles favor gliders near the top and penalize them near the coast.

Still-air range from the Mauna Kea summit at best glide:

- Paraglider 9:1 → ~38 km

- Hang glider 15:1 → ~63 km

- Sailplane 50:1 → ~210 km (on paper that reaches across the ʻAlenuihāhā Channel to Maui; Hāna is ~120 km away)

### 5.2 Paraglider from Mauna Kea or Haleakalā

- Launch at 0.80 kg/m³ density means ~24% higher takeoff speed.

- Pilot hypoxia at 4,200 m degrades judgment and reaction time.

- Dark cinder slopes heat strongly; afternoon thermals can be powerful.

- **Toward Hāmākua (NE):** straight into the trades, killing effective glide (Section 2.3), then into the windward orographic cloud band at 600 to 2,000 m. Flying into cloud is flying blind into a mountainside.

- **Toward Kawaihae (W):** tailwind from the trades boosts effective glide well past the 10.8:1 requirement. Watch the gap-wind acceleration through the Waimea saddle and turbulent lee flow.

- Upper-level winds above the inversion can blow westerly and strong in winter, reversing the picture.

- Maui has an established paragliding site at Polipoli on the upper Kula slopes of Haleakalā.

### 5.3 Hang Glider from Mauna Kea

- 15:1 glide clears every coast option with altitude to spare.

- Thin air raises takeoff and landing speeds. A foot launch at 4,200 m with a 30+ kg glider is a brutal sprint in hypoxic air.

- Arrival at sea level with excess energy gives freedom to pick landing fields.

### 5.4 Sailplane from Mauna Kea

- No runway at the summit. A bungee launch off a steep cinder cone (the original 1920s German method) is the only gravity-era option.

- Once airborne, every coast on the island is within glide. Thermal and ridge lift on the flanks extend range further.

### 5.5 Wingsuit

- A 3:1 glide is steeper than every summit-to-coast gradient. A summit launch impacts the mountain flank long before the ocean.

- To reach the coast a wingsuiter would need to exit an aircraft far above the summit. For 30 km at 3:1, ~10 km of altitude loss plus deployment margin: beyond summit-launch physics and into high-altitude oxygen jumps.

### 5.6 Giant Segmented Zip Line (Mauna Kea to Coast)

A single 30 km span is impossible (Section 2.6). A chain of 1 to 2 km segments is a different matter.

- Average slope 8° → seated terminal speed ~18 m/s near sea level and ~22 m/s near the summit (thinner air, less drag).

- Ride time ~25 to 30 minutes of riding plus transfers between 15 to 30 segments.

- Total energy to dissipate: 3.71 MJ per 90 kg rider, almost all burned off by air drag at terminal speed.

- **Regenerative concept:** a motorized trolley acting as a generator could recover a fraction of the ~1 kWh per rider. Gondola systems already use counterbalancing to the same effect.

- Trade-wind cloud zone brings constant moisture and corrosion. Volcanic cinder is weak ground for anchors. Lightning exposure on a 30 km cable line is significant.

**Benefits:** gravity-powered, no pilot skill, theme-park experience. **Limits:** cost scales with length, one-way, land status, maintenance of dozens of terminals.

### 5.7 Road Descent: Bicycle, Gravity Vehicle, Longboard

**Mauna Kea:** the summit access road runs from the summit down to the Saddle Road (Daniel K. Inouye Highway) at ~2,000 m, with a steep unpaved section above the visitor station at ~2,800 m. The Saddle Road then continues down to Hilo. Total road distance is roughly 70 km.

**Haleakalā:** the summit road descends ~3,000 m to the north shore over roughly 60 km of switchbacks. Commercial downhill bike tours operate from the lower slopes outside the national park boundary.

**Brake thermodynamics (100 kg rider plus bike at a steady 40 km/h on a 10% grade):**

- Gravity power input: 100 × 9.81 × 0.10 × 11.1 m/s ≈ **1,090 W**

- Aero drag dissipates: ½ × 1.0 × 0.4 × 11.1³ ≈ **270 W**

- Brakes must absorb the remaining **~820 W continuously** for 30 to 60 minutes.

- Rim brakes heat the rim, raising inner-tube pressure toward blowout. Disc brakes shed heat to air but can fade. Pulse braking (hard short applications, then coast to cool) beats continuous dragging.

**Gravity car or soapbox:** same energy problem with more mass. Regenerative electric braking (e-bike or EV) turns the descent into battery charge.

**Benefits:** legal on public roads, no flying risk, cheap. **Limits:** brake heat, traffic, washboard gravel, cold and hypo9ٹg-	xia at the top, speed wobble on longboards.

### 5.8 Foot: The Real Summit-to-Sea Hike

**Haleakalā via Kaupō Gap:** the Sliding Sands Trail drops from near the summit into the crater, crosses to Palikū and then descends the Kaupō Trail through the gap in the crater wall to the south coast near the Piʻilani Highway. Roughly 30 km with about 3,000 m of descent. Steep, rocky and very hard on knees.

**Knee physics:** descending, leg muscles brake eccentrically. Eccentric work causes more muscle damage and soreness than climbing (delayed-onset muscle soreness). Joint loads on steep descent run several times body weight.

**Mauna Kea:** the summit trail from the visitor station climbs ~1,400 m over ~10 km. No established continuous route continues to the coast; lower flanks cross ranch land and forest reserve.

### 5.9 Snow (Seasonal)

Mauna Kea receives winter snow. Skiing and snowboarding happen informally on the upper cinder cones with vehicle shuttles. Snow coverage ends well above the coast, so snow is only a summit segment.

### 5.10 Water Routes

Streams born on the windward flanks (Wailuku River to Hilo, Hāmākua gulches) descend to the sea through waterfalls and gorges. Canyoneering descents of individual gulches are possible. A continuous summit-to-sea water route does not exist: the upper mountain is porous cinder and lava with no permanent surface streams. Windward streams flash flood within minutes of heavy rain.

### 5.11 Powered Flight from the Summit

| Method | Summit performance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Helicopter | Light piston helicopters approach their hover ceilings near 4,200 m | Turbine types handle it easily; an AS350 B3 landed on the summit of Everest in 2005 |
| Paramotor | Wing must fly ~24% faster; engine loses ~35% power without a turbo | Takeoff run long and hard in thin air |
| Hot air balloon | Lift per m³ ~35% lower than sea level | Trade winds carry it W to SW across the island toward the Kona side |
| Jet suit | Few minutes endurance | 30 km to the coast exceeds endurance |
| eVTOL | Hover power ~24% higher than sea level | Downhill flight is cheap once airborne |

### 5.12 Theoretical: Gravity Rail and Roller Coaster

A frictionless drop of 4,207 m gives v = √(2gh) ≈ **287 m/s** (1,030 km/h). Drag and controlled braking are mandatory. A rail line built on a constant ~8° grade with regenerative magnetic braking (maglev style) would recover a large fraction of the ~1 kWh per rider. A 4,207 m regenerative descent of a 10-tonne car yields ~115 kWh; such a system could help power its own uphill cars by counterbalancing.

### 5.13 Theoretical: Rolling Sphere (Zorb)

A human inside a padded inflatable ball on an 8° slope reaches terminal speeds similar to an unprotected rolling body: dozens of meters per second within a few hundred meters. Bounces and lava rock make it lethal. Purely a thought experiment.

## 7. Land Status and Regulatory Limiting Factors

**Olympic Peninsula**

- Olympic National Park covers the interior. About 95% of the park is designated wilderness (Daniel J. Evans Wilderness).

- The Wilderness Act prohibits permanent structures, motorized equipment, mechanical transport and aircraft landings in designated wilderness except for emergencies and administration.

- National Park Service regulations (36 CFR 2.17) restrict aircraft landings, parachuting and delivery or retrieval by air to locations designated by the superintendent.

- Drones are prohibited from launching or landing in national parks.

- Surrounding Olympic National Forest and state and tribal lands have separate rules. Many of the methods above are only buildable or flyable outside the park.

**Hawaiʻi**

- Mauna Kea's summit lies within a state Conservation District, hosts the observatory complex and is culturally sacred to Native Hawaiians. Construction there is heavily contested.

- Haleakalā summit and crater are within Haleakalā National Park, with the same NPS aircraft, drone and wilderness restrictions as the Olympics.

- Hawaiian airspace near the summits includes observatory and military considerations.

- Ultralight, hang glider and paraglider flight in the U.S. falls under FAA Part 103.

## 8. Summary of the Physics Verdicts

1. **Gravity-only methods can never end higher than they start.** Zip lines, gliders without lift and wingsuits all move downhill. True peak-to-peak between equal summits requires an energy input: muscle, fuel, electricity or rising air.

2. **Cable spans are capped by strength-to-weight.** Steel tops out practically around 3 to 5 km. Synthetic fibers push further but bring heat and creep problems at the trolley.

3. **Rising air is the free energy source.** Ridge lift, thermals and mountain waves let paragliders, hang gliders and sailplanes climb. The Hurricane Ridge winds are too strong for light wings much of the year but ideal for sailplanes.

4. **Wind direction relative to the gap matters more than wind speed.** Cross downwind from a windward launch to a windward arrival. Arriving on a lee face invites rotor and sink.

5. **Glide ratio versus terrain gradient decides summit-to-sea.** Wingsuits and speed wings cannot outglide Hawaiian volcano slopes. Paragliders roughly match them. Hang gliders and sailplanes beat them comfortably.

6. **Thin air at 4,200 m raises all speeds by ~24%** and cuts engine power and hot-air lift by about a third.

7. **Descending is an energy-disposal problem.** ~1 kWh per person from Mauna Kea must leave through air drag, brakes or regeneration.

Regenerative e-bike or maglev rail is the only mundane way to **recover** a fraction on Earth.

## 11. Method Selection Flowcharts (Text)

### 11.1 Olympic peak A to peak B (equal height)

1. Can you walk 1-3 days? -> Foot / ski mountaineering (only always-legal wilderness traverse).
2. Is peak B lower by >6% of span? -> Single zip may arrive without powered haul.
3. Else need: second stage lift, tram, helicopter, powered trolley, or aircraft with ridge lift.
4. Is wind >30 km/h at crest? -> Close PG; sailplane may still work in wave.
5. Is span >3 km? -> Steel zip sags >6%; consider synthetic cable or tram.

### 11.2 Mauna Kea summit to ocean

1. Compare mean gradient (9-14%) to 1/G. If G < ~7, unpowered wing cannot coast in still air.
2. Check wind: headwind to Hāmākua shrinks effective G; tail to Kona extends it.
3. If no aircraft launch at summit: road bike, hike lower, helicopter, or segmented zip fantasy.

## Appendix L. Geology and anchoring (Olympic + Hawaiʻi)

### L.1 Olympic core rock

- **Geology:** the Olympic core is folded and sheared marine sedimentary rock (sandstone, siltstone, shale, turbidites) scraped off the subducting plate and uplifted. A horseshoe of Crescent Formation basalt wraps the north, east and south flanks. Expect heavy water inflow, squeezing shale and fault gouge.

| Anchor type | Best in | Worst in | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rock bolt in basalt | Crescent rim | Fractured shale | Water drains fast in basalt |
| Deep micropile | Valley sidewalls | Soft river gravels | Hoh floodplain unsuitable |
| Deadman in soil | Moraine benches | Saturated slide slopes | Seasonal creep |
| Cliff equalization | Granite outcrops (rare) | Overhanging sandstone | Rockfall risk |

Zip and tram anchors need **million-newton** class tie-backs on 4 km spans (Section 3.1 end tensions ~160 kN horizontal, but vertical peak loads and gust amplification multiply design demand).

## Appendix P. Master comparison duplicate (author scratch)

### P.1 Olympic (condensed from Section 6.1)

Foot and ski remain the only **equal-height** peak-to-peak methods without lift or powered second stage. Everything else is down-only, overflight, or requires B lower than A by hundreds of meters.

### P.2 Hawaii (condensed from Section 6.2)

Hang glider and sailplane **clear gradients** with margin. Paraglider **clears most** routes but Hāmākua headwind and Kawaihae short glide are edge cases. Wingsuit and speed wing **fail** summit-to-coast in still air. Road bike and hike **always work** given time and brakes. Segmented zip is **engineering fantasy** at 30 km but obeys Section 2.6 physics segment by segment.

### P.3 Physics verdicts restated (Section 8)

1. Gravity-only cannot gain net elevation without external energy.
2. Steel cable spans practical cap ~3-5 km; synthetics extend with new failure modes.
3. Rising air is free altitude for wings.
4. Wind vector matters more than speed alone for crossing geometry.
5. Glide gradient vs terrain gradient gates Hawaii wings.
6. Mauna Kea thin air +24% TAS, -~third hot-air/helicopter margin.
7. Descent dissipates ~3.71 MJ (90 kg, Mauna Kea) through drag or brakes.

## Appendix AB. Crosswind pendulum (Section 3.1)

Lateral force F_lat ≈ ½·ρ·C_dA·v_wind². For v_wind = 20 m/s, ρ = 1.05, C_dA = 0.6: F_lat ≈ 126 N.

For m = 90 kg, W = 883 N, pendulum angle ≈ arctan(126/883) ≈ 8° off vertical.

Gust doubling adds 4× drag force if v doubles momentarily (cube law on wind speed for drag), swinging rider harder and loading tethers.

Olympic zip operations should publish crosswind limits separate from along-cable headwind limits.

Braking recap: 30 m/s seated rider carries 0.5·m·v² ≈ 40.5 kJ kinetic energy (Section 3.1 tailwind case). Capture track must absorb that heat without rebound into the valley.

Record anchors cited in Section 3.1: Jebel Jais single zip ~2.83 km; Whistler Peak 2 Peak unsupported gondola ~3.03 km. Olympic 3-6 km scenarios intentionally sit at or beyond those spans to stress cable law in Section 2.6.

Mauna Kea descent energy **3.71 MJ** and Haleakalā **2.70 MJ** (Section 2.1) appear again in Appendices Q, U, and X so authors do not need to recalculate mid-scene.

Thin-air **24%** faster true airspeed at the Mauna Kea summit is repeated in Sections 2.4, 5.2, and Appendix V.
