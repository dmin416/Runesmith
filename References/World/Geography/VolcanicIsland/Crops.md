# Crops: Old Volcanic Island with Renewed Volcanism

Companion to `Resources.md` and `Ecology.md`. Wind, rain and farm sites: `Farming.md`. Waterfowl: `../../Fauna/Domestic/KoloaAndNene.md`. Places: `../Places.md` (Dragnis-class islands). Runesmith / game farming brainstorm (separate): `FarmingSystems.md`.

## Notes and Assumptions
- **Setting:** an old, eroded hotspot island with recent eruptions, modeled on Hawaiʻi.
- **Climate:** tropical, in the trade-wind belt around 20°N.
- **Elevation:** the island is assumed tall enough to have upcountry land between about 600 and 1,500 m. The new volcanic cones may push higher.
- **Seasons:** kau (dry) and hoʻoilo (wet) are Hawaii-model labels. Remap to the Caldris calendar when a beat needs dates. Seasonal food detail: `Resources.md` (Seasonal Availability).
- **Soils, water, lime, guano, geothermal heat and fluorine hazards:** see `Resources.md`. This file covers what grows and how it is farmed.
- **Status codes:**
  - **N (Native):** a native plant that was cultivated or tended.
  - **T (Traditional):** brought by Polynesian voyagers before European contact.
  - **H (Historical):** grown in Hawaiʻi after contact, often commercially.
  - **P (Possible):** grows in Hawaiʻi or comparable tropical islands today, with little historical role.
- **Hawaiian names:** given where one exists.

### Era filter (story use)
Pick one stack and stick to it for a given period on the island:

| Stack | Includes | Typical story use |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-contact** | N + T only | Canoe-plant agriculture, loʻi, dryland fields |
| **Post-contact** | N + T + H | Adds rice, coffee, sugar, pineapple, cattle ranching, upcountry truck crops |
| **Full catalog** | N + T + H + P | Modern possibles for worldbuilding; not default canon |

Dragnis and similar islands default to **Pre-contact** unless a chapter establishes outside trade or plantation tech. Do not mix plantation sugar with canoe-only culture unless the timeline says so.

| Upcountry | Roughly 600 to 1,500 m, cool nights (overlaps mid-elevation above 600 m) | Temperate vegetables, white potato, onion, cabbage, strawberries, persimmon, protea, grapes |
| High elevation | Above about 1,500 m, frost possible | Pasture, low-chill temperate fruit, hardy greens |

| Fresh ash fields | Loose, fertile, holds water | Most vegetables and fruit once the ash settles |
| Old laterite uplands | Red, acidic, low in nutrients | Pineapple, coffee and tea with lime and manure |
| Geothermal ground | Warm soil, steam, heated water | Greenhouse crops, crop drying, off-season vegetables |

## Farming Systems
- **Loʻi kalo:** flooded stone-walled taro terraces fed by ʻauwai ditches. The water continues downstream to the next farm. Fish and shrimp live in the ponds.
- **Fishpond integration:** water from taro terraces drains into coastal fishponds, carrying nutrients that feed the fish.
- **Dryland field systems:** long stone-walled fields of sweet potato, dryland taro, sugarcane and breadfruit on rain-fed slopes. The Kona and Kohala field systems on the Big Island are the models.
- **Breadfruit belts:** mid-slope bands of breadfruit with an understory of other crops. Kona had a breadfruit belt known as kaluʻulu.
- **Agroforestry layers:**
  - Canopy: breadfruit or kukui.
  - Middle layer: banana and ti.
  - Ground layer: taro and sweet potato.
- **Lava pocket planting:** sweet potato and dryland taro planted in cracks and pits in young lava, filled with gathered soil and leaf mulch.
- **Rice paddies:** former taro terraces converted to rice (H-era).
- **Plantation agriculture:** large-scale sugar and pineapple with irrigation tunnels and ditches (H-era).
- **Upcountry truck farming:** vegetables and potatoes on cool slopes for local markets and export (H-era).
- **Geothermal agriculture:**
  - Heated greenhouses for tomatoes and peppers in the cool uplands.
  - Warm soils for off-season crops.
  - Steam or hot-air drying for coffee, fruit, fish and timber.

## Soil Management
Detail and mineral sources: `Resources.md` (Soils, Guano, Lime).

- **Old laterite soils:** acidic and low in nutrients. Improved with crushed coral or burnt lime, manure, guano and compost.
- **Ash soils:** fertile. They lock up phosphate, which makes guano and bone especially valuable as fertilizers.
- **Fresh ashfall:** a light dusting adds nutrients. Heavy ash buries and kills crops.
- **Fresh lava:** almost unusable without carried-in soil and mulch.
- **Alluvial valley soils:** the best farmland on the island.
- **Green manure:** pigeon pea, sunn hemp and other legumes add nitrogen.

## Volcanic Hazards to Crops and Stock
- **Vog:** sulfur dioxide yellows and burns leaves. Leafy greens and flowers suffer most.
- **Acid rain:** damages leaves and acidifies soils near the vents.
- **Ashfall:** buries fields, breaks plants and collapses greenhouse roofs.
- **Fluorine in ash:** poisons grazing animals that eat contaminated forage (Iceland Laki-type hazard). Plants may look only dusty while stock sicken.
- **Lava:** destroys fields, terraces and irrigation systems outright.
- **Water loss:** lava can bury or divert the streams that feed terraces.
- **Laze and salt spray:** kill crops near lava entering the sea.

## Pests and Diseases
- **Isolated start:** before outside contact, an island like this has few crop pests or diseases.
- **Arriving with trade:** fruit flies, rats, insects and plant diseases come with ships and cargo.
- **Island vulnerability:** a single new disease can wipe out a staple. Taro, banana and coffee in Hawaiʻi have all suffered from introduced diseases and pests.

## Idea for Applying Magic: Accelerated Growth
These are the real biological and physical limits that would still apply if growth were sped up. Whether the magic covers any of them is a story decision. Rune / skill brainstorm for farms: `FarmingSystems.md`.

### Core Principle
Every kilogram of crop is built from sunlight, water, carbon dioxide and soil nutrients. Speeding up a plant's clock without supplying more of these leaves the plant short of materials and energy.

### Inputs That Must Scale With Growth
- **Light:** crops capture only about 1 to 2% of incoming sunlight in practice.
  - The theoretical ceiling is roughly 4.6% for plants like rice, wheat and taro.
  - It is about 6% for sugarcane, corn and sorghum.
  - Doubling the growth rate needs about double the captured energy if efficiency stays constant. Forced growth often lowers efficiency, so the plant may need even more light or still grow pale, stretched and weak.
- **Water:** crops transpire a few hundred to over a thousand liters of water per kilogram of dry plant matter. Faster growth drains water faster. Dry leeward fields and irrigation systems hit their limits first.
- **Nutrients:** nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and trace minerals leave the field with every harvest. More harvests per year strip soil proportionally faster.
  - Old laterite soils would collapse within a few cycles.
  - Ash soils lock up phosphate, so phosphorus runs out first.
  - Guano, manure, fish waste, ash and crushed coral become critical resources.
- **Carbon dioxide:** dense, fast-growing crops in still air or enclosed greenhouses can draw down local carbon dioxide and slow themselves.
- **Root space:** thin soil over lava limits root mass. Fast-growing tall crops and trees on shallow soil blow over in wind.

### Biological Clocks That Do Not Speed Up on Their Own
- **Day length:** many crops flower only when days reach a certain length.
  - Examples include many rice varieties, soybeans and sugarcane flowering.
  - Breadfruit fruits on a seasonal cycle.
  - A plant can grow large quickly and still wait for the calendar.
- **Chill hours:** temperate fruit such as peach, apple and plum needs a set amount of winter cold to flower. Speed does not substitute for cold.
- **Pollination:**
  - Pollinators keep their own schedule.
  - Vanilla flowers open for about a day and need hand pollination.
  - Fruit and seed set depend on pollinators being present when flowers open.
- **Juvenile phase:** fruit trees must pass a developmental stage before bearing. Shortening it requires speeding development, not just size.
- **Lifespan:**
  - If aging speeds up with growth, perennial crops reach the end of their productive life sooner. Coffee, cacao, citrus and coconut all have finite bearing lifespans.
  - Banana: the fruiting stalk dies after fruiting; the mat continues from suckers unless aging magic hits the whole clump.

### Quality Trade-offs
- **Watery, bland produce:** fast growth produces more water and less sugar, starch and flavor per bite. Ripening chemistry takes time.
- **Weak tissue:** fast-grown grain stalks fall over (lodging). Fast-grown timber is less dense and weaker.
- **Pest and disease vulnerability:** soft, nitrogen-rich growth attracts fungi and insects.
- **Storage:** low dry-matter roots and fruit rot sooner.

### Steps That Run on Real Time After Harvest
These only speed up if the magic applies to them directly:
- Poi souring.
- Breadfruit pit fermentation.
- Cacao fermentation.
- Coffee drying.
- Vanilla curing, which takes months.
- Sugar boiling.
- Kava preparation.
- Timber seasoning.
- Fiber retting.

### Side Effects
- **Weeds and invasive plants:** they benefit as well if the magic is not targeted.
- **Pests and diseases:** generations may speed up alongside the crops.
- **Labor:** harvesting, pounding, drying and storage become the bottleneck. Fields mature faster than people can process them.
- **Markets:** surpluses crash prices and spoil before they can be eaten or traded.
- **Selective breeding:** accelerates. More generations per year means faster development of new varieties.

### Which Crops Gain the Most and Least
| Gain the most | Gain the least |
|---|---|
| Leafy greens and taro leaves | Breadfruit |
| Sweet potato | Temperate fruit (chill-dependent) |
| Taro corms | Vanilla |
| Sugarcane taken early for juice or biomass (sugar maturity still follows season) | Lychee |
| Bamboo | Mango |
| Fiber plants | Photoperiod-sensitive rice varieties |

Crops that are not day-length triggered and are harvested before flowering gain the most. Crops tied to seasons, chill or pollinators gain the least.
