### 23.3 The Full Chain

- Main islands span about 400 mi (640 km) from Niʻihau to the southeast tip of the Big Island.
- The Northwestern Hawaiian Islands extend the chain to about 1,500 mi (2,400 km): Nihoa, Mokumanamana (Necker), French Frigate Shoals, Gardner Pinnacles, Maro Reef, Laysan, Lisianski, Pearl and Hermes Atoll, Midway and Kure.
- Islands grow older and smaller to the northwest as the Pacific Plate carries them away from the volcanic hotspot under the Big Island.
- About 2,390 mi (3,850 km) from California and farther from every other continent. The most isolated major archipelago on Earth.

### 23.4 Big Island Dimensions

- About 93 mi (150 km) north to south
- About 76 mi (122 km) east to west
- Larger than all the other Hawaiian islands combined
- Measured from its base on the sea floor, Mauna Kea rises about 10,200 m, taller than Everest from base to peak.

## 24. Big Island Geology

### 24.1 The Five Volcanoes

| Volcano | Summit | Share of island area | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mauna Loa | 13,679 ft (4,169 m) | ~51% | Active; largest active volcano on Earth by volume |
| Mauna Kea | 13,803 ft (4,207 m) | ~23% | Dormant; last eruption about 4,500 years ago |
| Kīlauea | ~4,000 ft (~1,220 m) | ~14% | Among the most active volcanoes on Earth |
| Hualālai | 8,271 ft (2,521 m) | ~7% | Active; erupts every few centuries |
| Kohala | 5,480 ft (1,670 m) | ~6% | Extinct; oldest; last eruption about 120,000 years ago |

Offshore to the southeast, the seamount Kamaʻehuakanaloa is building the next island.

### 24.2 Lava Types

- **Pāhoehoe:** smooth, ropy, billowy surface. Forms from hot, fluid lava. Easier walking.
- **ʻAʻā:** rough, jagged clinker over a dense core. Forms from cooler, thicker lava. Nearly impassable on foot.
- **Cinder and spatter:** loose fragments around vents forming cones
- **Ash:** fine fragments carried by wind; weathers into fertile soil

### 24.3 Volcanic Features

- **Rift zones:** lines of weakness radiating from summits where most flank eruptions occur
- **Calderas:** summit collapse craters (Kīlauea, Mokuʻāweoweo on Mauna Loa)
- **Lava tubes:** drained conduits beneath pāhoehoe flows; caves miles long
- **Kīpuka:** islands of older land and forest surrounded by younger lava
- **Pit craters:** collapse craters along rift zones
- **Cinder cones:** hundreds dot Mauna Kea's upper slopes

### 24.4 Mauna Kea Glaciation

Ice caps covered Mauna Kea's summit during ice ages. Glacial moraines, polished rock and Lake Waiau (about 3,970 m elevation) remain.

### 24.5 Kohala and Hāmākua

Kohala's age has allowed streams to carve deep valleys on its windward side: Waipiʻo, Waimanu, Pololū and others with cliffs over 600 m tall. The Hāmākua coast is cut by dozens of steep gulches.

## 25. Climate

### 25.1 Trade Winds

Northeast trade winds blow about 70-90% of the time in summer and less often in winter. Moist ocean air rises against windward slopes, cools and rains. Leeward slopes sit in a rain shadow.

### 25.2 Trade-Wind Inversion

A layer of warm, dry air sits at about 1,800-2,400 m (6,000-8,000 ft). It caps cloud growth. Below it, windward slopes are wet. Above it, the air is dry with intense sun, frost and low humidity. The inversion explains why the summits of Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa are deserts.

### 25.3 The Kona Exception

The western slopes of Hualālai and Mauna Loa block the trade winds entirely. Daytime sea breezes push air upslope and build afternoon clouds, especially in summer. This creates a cloud belt and mesic forest at about 600-1,800 m on a coast that would otherwise be desert.

### 25.4 Seasons

- **Kau (summer, roughly May to October):** steady trades, warmer, drier on most leeward coasts, wetter in Kona uplands
- **Hoʻoilo (winter, roughly November to April):** weaker trades, cold fronts, Kona storms from the south and west, snow on the high summits

### 25.7 Fog Drip

In the cloud belts, tree leaves and mosses comb water directly from fog. Fog drip adds substantial water beyond measured rainfall in windward montane forest and the Kona cloud belt.

## 26. Soils and Water

### 26.1 Soil Types

- **Fresh lava:** no soil. Plants root in cracks where water, dust and organic matter collect.
- **Ash soils (andisols):** form on volcanic ash. Hold large amounts of water, rich in organic matter, highly fertile. Found on Kohala, Hāmākua, upper Kona and Mauna Kea's slopes.
- **Thin organic soils on young lava:** Puna and lower Kaʻū
- **Cinder:** loose, well-drained, low fertility
- **Bog soils:** waterlogged, acidic, peaty on Kohala's summit

### 26.2 Nutrient Limits

- Young lava flows are short of nitrogen. Nitrogen-fixing trees such as koa and māmane and nitrogen-fixing lichens help build fertility.
- Very old soils (hundreds of thousands to millions of years) are short of phosphorus. These conditions appear on Kohala and on older islands.

### 26.3 Fresh Water

- **Windward perennial streams:** Hāmākua gulches, Waipiʻo and Kohala valleys, Wailuku River at Hilo (the longest river on the island)
- **Leeward:** almost no permanent streams. Rain sinks into porous lava.
- **Coastal springs:** fresh groundwater seeps out at the shoreline, floating on top of seawater. Brackish anchialine pools line the Kona and Kohala coasts.
- **Fog drip and pooled rainwater:** collect in lava hollows and tree fern trunks
- **Lake Waiau:** small alpine lake near Mauna Kea's summit


# PART THREE: NATIVE TREES

Hawaiʻi's native flora holds a little over 1,000 flowering plant species, about 90% found nowhere else. All arrived across thousands of miles of ocean by wind, by floating seeds or carried by birds. With no grazing mammals, many lineages lost thorns, bitter chemicals and stinging hairs.

## 27. Species Accounts

### 27.1 ʻŌhiʻa lehua

- **Scientific name:** *Metrosideros polymorpha*
- **Family:** Myrtle (Myrtaceae)
- **Distribution:** all main islands
- **Elevation:** sea level to about 2,500 m
- **Rainfall tolerance:** from under 400 mm to over 10,000 mm per year
- **Size:** dwarf shrubs under 1 m in bogs to trees over 30 m in deep-soil wet forest
- **Leaves:** extremely variable; smooth and green in wet sites, woolly and gray in dry or high sites. New leaves (liko) are red, pink or bronze.
- **Flowers:** pompom clusters of long stamens, usually red, also yellow, orange, salmon and pink
- **Seeds:** tiny and wind-dispersed in huge numbers
- **Ecology:** the first tree to colonize new lava; dominant canopy tree of most native forests; makes up most of the native forest biomass of the state. Forms aerial roots in wet forest.
- **Varieties:** about eight, including var. *incana* (dry lowlands, woolly leaves), var. *glaberrima* (wet forest, smooth leaves), var. *polymorpha* (high elevation, woolly), var. *macrophylla* (large-leaved wet-forest form) and var. *newellii* (streamside form near Hilo)
- **Other Metrosideros species:** *M. macropus* (Oʻahu wet forest), *M. rugosa* (Oʻahu summit ridges), *M. tremuloides* (Oʻahu, drooping leaves), *M. waialealae* (Kauaʻi bogs and wet forest)
- **Wood:** reddish brown heartwood, paler sapwood; heavy (SG about 0.70-0.85); hard; interlocked and irregular grain
- **Drying:** difficult. Checks, splits and twists. Seal ends at once, shade, weight heavily and dry slowly.
- **Durability:** moderately durable
- **Uses:** posts, beams, flooring, carved images, weapons, tool handles, firewood, charcoal

### 27.2 Koa

- **Scientific name:** *Acacia koa*
- **Family:** Legume (Fabaceae)
- **Distribution:** all large islands
- **Elevation:** about 100-2,300 m
- **Habitat:** mesic and wet forest; best growth on deep ash soils
- **Size:** 15-35 m tall; old trees reach trunk diameters of 2-3 m with broad spreading crowns
- **Leaves:** seedlings bear true feathery compound leaves. Older plants switch to phyllodes, flattened sickle-shaped leaf stalks that resist drought.
- **Flowers:** pale yellow puffball clusters
- **Seeds:** flat pods; hard-coated seeds survive in soil for decades and sprout after disturbance or fire
- **Ecology:** fixes nitrogen and enriches soil. Fast growing when young. The largest native tree. Co-dominant with ʻōhiʻa in montane forest.
- **Wood:** golden to red-brown to dark chocolate; SG about 0.55-0.60; medium texture; curly, fiddleback and flame figure common; strongly chatoyant
- **Drying:** dries moderately well; low T/R ratio gives good stability; figured stock prone to distortion and needs shade and weighting
- **Durability:** non-durable to moderately durable in ground; excellent indoors
- **Uses:** canoe hulls (the largest logs), paddles, surfboards, bowls, furniture, instruments

### 27.3 Koaiʻa

- **Scientific name:** *Acacia koaia* (sometimes treated as a form of koa)
- **Distribution:** Kauaʻi, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Maui, Big Island
- **Habitat:** dry leeward slopes
- **Size:** 4-10 m; gnarled, twisted trunks
- **Leaves:** narrow phyllodes
- **Wood:** denser, harder and finer-grained than koa; dark
- **Uses:** spears, tool handles, small tools

### 27.4 Māmane

- **Scientific name:** *Sophora chrysophylla*
- **Family:** Legume (Fabaceae)
- **Distribution:** Kauaʻi, Oʻahu, Molokaʻi, Maui, Big Island
- **Elevation:** mostly 1,500-2,900 m, scattered lower
- **Habitat:** dry montane and subalpine
- **Size:** shrub to tree 15 m
- **Leaves:** feather-compound with small leaflets
- **Flowers:** bright yellow pea flowers
- **Seeds:** yellow seeds in winged pods; toxic to most animals. The palila (a honeycreeper) specializes on them.
- **Ecology:** fixes nitrogen; dominant tree of the subalpine woodland
- **Wood:** yellowish to brown; very hard and heavy
- **Durability:** very durable in ground
- **Uses:** posts, digging sticks, sled runners, firewood

### 27.5 Naio

- **Scientific name:** *Myoporum sandwicense*
- **Family:** Figwort (Scrophulariaceae)
- **Distribution:** all main islands
- **Elevation:** coast to about 3,000 m
- **Habitat:** mostly dry; also mesic
- **Size:** prostrate shrub on coasts to tree 10-15 m upslope
- **Flowers:** small, white to pink
- **Fruit:** small white to purplish berries
- **Wood:** yellowish to brown; dense; fragrant when fresh with a sandalwood-like scent ("false sandalwood")
- **Durability:** durable
- **Uses:** house framing, posts, firewood with pleasant smoke

### 27.6 ʻIliahi (Sandalwood)

- **Scientific names:**
  - *Santalum paniculatum:* Big Island, dry to mesic forest, about 450-2,500 m
  - *Santalum ellipticum:* coastal and lowland dry forest on all main islands; often shrubby
  - *Santalum freycinetianum:* Oʻahu and Molokaʻi mesic forest
  - *Santalum haleakalae:* Maui subalpine; red flowers
  - *Santalum pyrularium:* Kauaʻi
  - *Santalum involutum:* Kauaʻi
- **Family:** Sandalwood (Santalaceae)
- **Ecology:** root hemiparasite. Its roots attach to roots of neighboring plants (often koa) and draw water and nutrients while it also photosynthesizes.
- **Size:** shrubs to trees 10-20 m
- **Wood:** sapwood pale and scentless; heartwood yellow-brown, oily, fine-grained and strongly fragrant from santalol oils
- **Aging:** heartwood forms over decades. Oil is richest in roots and lower trunk of mature trees. Cut heartwood keeps its scent for decades to centuries.
- **Uses:** scent (powdered wood for scenting cloth), carving, fine objects

### 27.7 Lama

- **Scientific names:** *Diospyros sandwicensis* (all main islands); *Diospyros hillebrandii* (Kauaʻi and Oʻahu)
- **Family:** Ebony (Ebenaceae)
- **Habitat:** lowland dry and mesic forest, 0-1,200 m
- **Size:** tree to 12 m or more
- **Leaves:** glossy and leathery; new growth pinkish
- **Fruit:** yellow-orange to red; edible when fully ripe, astringent before
- **Wood:** very hard, dense, fine-grained; dark heartwood
- **Uses:** sacred enclosures and altars, fine tools, firewood, charcoal

### 27.8 Wiliwili

- **Scientific name:** *Erythrina sandwicensis*
- **Family:** Legume (Fabaceae)
- **Distribution:** all main islands
- **Habitat:** lowland dry forest, 0-600 m
- **Size:** 5-15 m; thick trunk; short prickles on branches
- **Leaves:** three leaflets; drops leaves in the dry season
- **Flowers:** claw-shaped; orange, salmon, yellow, red, green or white
- **Seeds:** bean-like; red, orange or yellow
- **Ecology:** fixes nitrogen; a dominant tree of the lowland dry forest
- **Wood:** extremely light (SG about 0.2-0.3); soft; pale
- **Drying:** dries quickly; stains and decays easily if kept damp
- **Durability:** perishable
- **Uses:** outrigger floats, fishing net floats, surfboards, rafts

### 27.9 Kauila

- ***Alphitonia ponderosa:***
  - Distribution: Kauaʻi, Oʻahu, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Maui, Big Island
  - Habitat: dry to mesic forest, about 250-1,000 m
  - Size: tree to 25 m
  - Leaves: dull above, pale and hairy beneath
- ***Colubrina oppositifolia:***
  - Distribution: Oʻahu, Maui, Big Island (North Kona dry forest)
  - Size: tree to 13 m
  - Leaves: opposite pairs
- **Family:** Buckthorn (Rhamnaceae)
- **Wood:** dark reddish brown; extremely hard and heavy; sinks in water
- **Durability:** very durable
- **Uses:** spears, tapa beaters, digging sticks, pegs, tool handles

### 27.10 Uhiuhi

- **Scientific name:** *Mezoneuron kavaiense*

### 27.11 Olopua

### 27.14 ʻAʻaliʻi

- **Scientific name:** *Dodonaea viscosa*
- **Family:** Soapberry (Sapindaceae)
- **Distribution:** all main islands; pantropical
- **Elevation:** coast to about 2,350 m

- **Ecology:** extremely wind and drought tolerant; colonizes lava and cinder
- **Wood:** hard, dense, dark heartwood
- **Durability:** durable
- **Uses:** small posts, house construction, spears, firewood; capsules for dye and decoration

### 27.15 Kōlea

- **Scientific name:** *Myrsine lessertiana* (kōlea lau nui) with about 20 other Hawaiian *Myrsine* species including *M. sandwicensis*, *M. lanaiensis*, *M. knudsenii*, *M. linearifolia* and *M. juddii*

### 27.28 Loulu Palms

- **Scientific name:** *Pritchardia*
- **Family:** Palm (Arecaceae)
- **Form:** fan palms 5-25 m tall
- **Ecology:** pollen records show loulu once formed extensive lowland forests (such as Oʻahu's ʻEwa plain) before seed-eating rats arrived with people. In a human-free Hawaiʻi these palm forests stand intact.
- **Uses:** leaves for thatch, fans and umbrellas; young seeds edible; trunk fiber

### 27.29 Hala

- **Scientific name:** *Pandanus tectorius*
- **Family:** Screwpine (Pandanaceae)
- **Status:** native; pollen predates human arrival
- **Habitat:** coastal and windward lowland
- **Form:** to 10 m; stilt roots; separate male and female trees
- **Fruit:** large compound fruit of wedge-shaped keys; fleshy bases edible
- **Leaves:** long, spiny-edged; prime weaving material for mats, sails and baskets
- **Trunk:** hard outer rind around a soft fibrous core

### 27.30 Hāpuʻu (Tree Ferns)

- **Scientific names:**
  - *Cibotium glaucum* (hāpuʻu pulu): all main islands; golden hairs (pulu) at frond bases
  - *Cibotium menziesii* (hāpuʻu ʻiʻi): largest, with dark hairs; trunks 6-10 m
  - *Cibotium chamissoi* (meu): Oʻahu and Molokaʻi
  - *Cibotium nealiae*: Kauaʻi
  - *Cibotium st.-johnii*: rare
- **Family:** Cibotiaceae
- **Habitat:** wet forest understory forming a dense layer 3-6 m tall beneath ʻōhiʻa
- **Growth:** slow; tall trunks represent decades to centuries of growth. Fallen trunks re-root and sprout.
- **Uses:** pulu hair for stuffing and wound dressing; starchy trunk core edible after cooking (famine food); trunks as natural water collectors and nursery logs for seedlings

### 27.31 ʻAmaʻu

- **Scientific name:** *Sadleria* (6 species)
- **Form:** short-trunked ferns to about 2 m; new fronds red
- **Ecology:** among the first plants on new lava

### 27.32 Hala pepe

- **Scientific names:** *Chrysodracon aurea, C. auwahiensis, C. fernaldii, C. forbesii, C. halapepe, C. hawaiiensis*
- **Family:** Asparagus (Asparagaceae)
- **Habitat:** dry and mesic forest
- **Form:** 3-10 m; sword-shaped leaves clustered at branch tips; hanging clusters of pale yellow flowers
- **Wood:** soft


### 27.33 Pāpala

- **Scientific names:** *Charpentiera densiflora, C. elliptica, C. obovata, C. ovata, C. tomentosa*
- **Family:** Amaranth (Amaranthaceae); one of very few trees in this family worldwide
- **Habitat:** mesic and wet forest
- **Size:** trees to 8-10 m
- **Wood:** very light and soft
- **Uses:** dry branches burn throwing showers of sparks. On Kauaʻi, burning pāpala logs were thrown from sea cliffs at night so the wind carried the glowing brands out over the ocean.

### 27.34 Pāpala kēpau

### 27.35 ʻOhe makai

### 27.63 Lobelioid Treelets

Hawaiʻi's lobelioids are the largest plant radiation on any island group, descended from a single ancestor.

- **Hāhā (*Cyanea*, about 75 species):** mostly unbranched palm-like stems topped by a rosette of leaves. Some juvenile plants bear prickles, thought to be a defense against browsing flightless birds. Big Island species include *C. pilosa*, *C. platyphylla*, *C. stictophylla*, *C. tritomantha*, *C. shipmanii* and *C. hamatiflora*.
- **ʻŌhā wai (*Clermontia*, about 22 species):** branched shrubs and small trees with curved tubular flowers. Some, such as *C. peleana*, grow as epiphytes on other trees. Big Island species include *C. clermontioides*, *C. hawaiiensis*, *C. kakeana*, *C. lindseyana*, *C. montis-loa*, *C. parviflora* and *C. pyrularia*.
- **ʻĀlula (*Brighamia*):** succulent stems on sea cliffs; pollinated by hawkmoths
- ***Trematolobelia*:** branched rosettes in wet forest and bogs
- ***Delissea*:** small treelets of dry and mesic forest

### 27.64 Other Small Trees

- **Heau (*Exocarpos*):** sandalwood relatives; shrubs to small trees
- **ʻĀweoweo (*Chenopodium oahuense*):** goosefoot shrub to small tree; coast to 3,000 m
- **ʻŌhelo kau lāʻau (*Vaccinium calycinum*):** blueberry relative; shrub to small tree; red edible berries
- ***Munroidendron racemosum*:** Kauaʻi cliff tree; ginseng family

## 30. Biomes in Detail

### 30.1 Zone Overview (Big Island)

| Zone | Windward elevation | Leeward elevation | Rainfall | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coastal | 0-100 m | 0-100 m | Varies | Salt spray, sand, rock, tide pools |
| Lowland | 0-1,000 m | 0-1,500 m | Dry to very wet | Forest from shore inland |
| Montane | 1,000-2,000 m | 1,500-2,500 m | Mesic to very wet | Cloud forest windward; koa and māmane leeward |
| Subalpine | 2,000-3,000 m | 2,500-3,000 m | Dry | Above the inversion; woodland and shrubland |
| Alpine | 3,000-3,700 m | 3,000-3,700 m | Very dry | Sparse shrubs and grasses |
| Alpine desert | Above 3,700 m | Above 3,700 m | Very dry | Bare rock and cinder |

### 30.2 Coastal Strand

**Location:** entire shoreline within reach of salt spray
**Elevation:** 0 to about 100 m
**Conditions:** wind, salt, sand or bare lava, intense sun

**Structure:**
- Spray zone: low mats and succulent herbs
- Back beach: shrubs 0.5-2 m
- Inland edge: groves of hala, loulu, naio and wiliwili

**Plants:**
- Naupaka kahakai (*Scaevola taccada*): dense shrub hedges along the sand line
- Pōhuehue (beach morning glory): runners across sand
- ʻĀkulikuli (*Sesuvium portulacastrum*): succulent mats near tide line
- ʻAkiʻaki (*Sporobolus virginicus*): salt grass
- Hinahina kahakai (*Heliotropium anomalum*): silver-leaved groundcover
- Pōhinahina (*Vitex rotundifolia*): fragrant silver shrub
- ʻIlima (*Sida fallax*): yellow-orange flowered shrub
- Nehe (*Lipochaeta*): yellow daisy groundcover
- ʻŌhai (*Sesbania tomentosa*): red-orange flowered shrub
- Maʻo (*Gossypium tomentosum*): native cotton
- Maiapilo (*Capparis sandwichiana*): native caper; white flowers open at night
- Nanea (*Vigna marina*): beach pea vine
- Pāʻū o Hiʻiaka (*Jacquemontia sandwicensis*): blue morning glory
- Kīpūkai (*Heliotropium curassavicum*): succulent of salty flats
- Hala groves: dominant on windward shores
- Loulu: on some coastal slopes

**Coastal wetlands and anchialine pools:**
- Makaloa (*Cyperus laevigatus*): weaving sedge
- ʻAhuʻawa (*Cyperus javanicus*): coarse sedge
- Kaluhā (*Schoenoplectus*): bulrush
- ʻAeʻae (*Bacopa monnieri*): creeping herb
- Anchialine pools: brackish ponds in lava fed by groundwater and tides, home to red ʻōpaeʻula shrimp

### 30.3 Lowland Dry Forest

**Location:** leeward coasts and lower slopes (Kohala coast, North and South Kona, Kaʻū lowlands)
**Elevation:** 0 to about 1,000-1,500 m
**Rainfall:** under 50 in (1,270 mm), often under 20 in
**Soil:** rocky lava, thin soils, some ash

**Structure:**
- Canopy 6-15 m, open to closed
- Subcanopy of small trees 3-6 m
- Shrub layer
- Sparse ground layer; many trees drop leaves in the dry season

**Canopy trees:** wiliwili, lama, olopua, kauila (both species), uhiuhi, ʻiliahi (*S. paniculatum*, *S. ellipticum*), naio, mēhamehame, ʻaʻe, ʻālaʻa, ʻāulu, loulu

**Subcanopy and shrubs:** ʻaʻaliʻi, alaheʻe, hala pepe, kokiʻo (*K. drynarioides* in North Kona), maʻo hau hele, ʻohe makai, ʻaiea, pāpala kēpau, hōlei, nānū, hao, ʻakoko, kolomona (*Senna gaudichaudii*), ʻilima

**Vines:** ʻānapanapa (*Colubrina asiatica*), native morning glories

**Notes:**
- In a human-free Hawaiʻi this zone is a true forest rather than today's grassland.
- It holds the greatest concentration of very hard, dense woods.
- Over 90% of it has been lost in the real world.
- Water is scarce; coastal springs and anchialine pools are the main sources.

### 30.4 Lowland Mesic Forest

**Location:** transition belts between dry and wet zones; Kona uplands; Kohala slopes
**Elevation:** 0 to about 1,200 m
**Rainfall:** 50-100 in

**Structure:**
- Canopy 10-25 m
- Rich subcanopy
- Shrub and fern layer

**Canopy trees:** koa, ʻōhiʻa, lama, olopua, ʻālaʻa, kōlea, mēhamehame, ʻiliahi, ʻōhiʻa hā, ʻaʻe

**Subcanopy:** hame, ʻahakea, ʻōlapa, kōpiko, kalia, poʻola, ʻakoko, hao, kaulu, alani, hōʻawa, māmaki, loulu, ʻaʻaka, maua

**Notes:** the most species-rich forest type in Hawaiʻi. Richest mix of timber species.

### 30.5 Lowland Wet Forest

**Location:** windward coasts and lower slopes (Hilo, Puna, Hāmākua)
**Elevation:** 0 to about 1,000 m
**Rainfall:** over 100 in

**Structure:**
- Canopy 15-30 m of ʻōhiʻa
- Tree fern layer 3-6 m
- Subcanopy of small trees
- Shrub layer
- Dense ground layer of ferns and mosses
- Abundant vines and epiphytes

**Canopy:** ʻōhiʻa dominant, loulu palms emergent or mixed in

**Tree fern layer:** hāpuʻu pulu and hāpuʻu ʻiʻi

**Subcanopy:** kōpiko, kōlea, ʻōlapa, hame, pāpala, alani, ʻaiea, pāpala kēpau

**Understory:** hāhā and ʻōhā wai lobelias, haʻiwale, māmaki, ʻōpuhe, kanawao (*Broussaisia arguta*)

**Vines:** ʻieʻie (*Freycinetia arborea*) climbing trunks; maile (*Alyxia stellata*) fragrant vine; hoi kuahiwi (*Smilax*)

**Ferns:** palaʻā (lace fern), ʻamaʻu, uluhe (*Dicranopteris linearis*) forming dense mats on landslides and openings

**Notes:**
- Hala groves near the coast
- Abundant streams and gulches on Hāmākua
- Maximum decay hazard for stored wood

### 30.6 Montane Wet and Cloud Forest

**Location:** windward slopes of Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa above Hilo and Hāmākua; Kohala uplands
**Elevation:** about 1,000-2,000 m
**Rainfall:** up to 200-300 in plus fog drip
**Temperature:** cool, 12-18°C

**Structure:**
- Canopy 15-30 m of ʻōhiʻa and koa
- Dense hāpuʻu layer
- Subcanopy
- Shrubs
- Thick moss and fern ground layer
- Trunks and branches covered in mosses, liverworts and epiphytic ferns

**Canopy:** ʻōhiʻa and koa
**Subcanopy:** ʻōlapa, kōlea, kāwaʻu, pilo, ānini, alani
**Shrubs:** ʻōhelo, pūkiawe (*Leptecophylla tameiameiae*), kanawao, lobelias
**Vines:** ʻōlonā (*Touchardia latifolia*), whose bark yields one of the strongest natural plant fibers known

**Notes:** prime habitat for the richest honeycreeper populations. Air drying of wood stalls here.

### 30.7 Montane Bogs

**Location:** Kohala summit plateau; poorly drained windward flats
**Elevation:** about 1,200-1,700 m on Kohala
**Conditions:** waterlogged, acidic, nutrient-poor peat

**Plants:**
- Dwarf ʻōhiʻa under 1 m
- Sedges (*Oreobolus*, *Carex*)
- Clubmosses
- Sphagnum moss
- ʻŌhelo
- Native violets
- Lobelias

### 30.8 Montane Mesic Forest

**Location:** upper Kona, upslope of Kīlauea, Kaʻū uplands
**Elevation:** about 1,200-2,000 m
**Rainfall:** 50-100 in

**Canopy:** koa dominant with ʻōhiʻa
**Associated trees:** māmane, naio, kōlea, ʻiliahi, pilo, ʻaʻe (soapberry), alani, hōʻawa
**Notes:** the finest koa grows here. Kīpuka of this forest type surrounded by younger lava hold some of the richest tree diversity on the island.

### 30.9 Montane Dry Forest

**Location:** leeward Hualālai, Puʻu Waʻawaʻa, northern Mauna Loa slopes, western Mauna Kea
**Elevation:** about 1,500-2,500 m
**Rainfall:** 20-50 in

**Canopy:** māmane and naio, with koa and ʻōhiʻa lower down
**Associated:** ʻaʻaliʻi, ʻiliahi, kōlea, ʻakoko (*E. olowaluana*), pūkiawe, ʻōhelo
**Ground:** native bunchgrasses

### 30.10 Subalpine Woodland and Shrubland

**Location:** Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa and Hualālai above the inversion
**Elevation:** about 2,000-3,000 m
**Conditions:** dry, cold nights, frost, intense sun, low humidity

**Structure:** open woodland thinning upslope to scattered shrubs

**Trees:** māmane and naio (Mauna Kea's māmane-naio woodland is the signature community)
**Shrubs:** pūkiawe, ʻōhelo, ʻaʻaliʻi, naʻenaʻe, native geranium (*Geranium cuneatum*)
**Grasses:** tussock grasses (*Deschampsia nubigena*, *Trisetum glomeratum*)

**Notes:** the palila lives here on māmane seeds. Fastest natural wood-drying conditions on the island.

### 30.11 Alpine

**Location:** upper slopes of Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa
**Elevation:** about 3,000-3,700 m

**Plants:**
- ʻĀhinahina (Mauna Kea silversword, *Argyroxiphium sandwicense* subsp. *sandwicense*): silver rosettes that bloom once and die
- Kaʻū silversword (*Argyroxiphium kauense*) on Mauna Loa
- Sparse pūkiawe, naʻenaʻe and grasses (*Trisetum*, *Agrostis sandwicensis*)
- Lichens and mosses on rock

### 30.12 Alpine Desert

**Location:** summit areas above about 3,700 m
**Plants:** almost none; lichens and mosses in sheltered crevices
**Animals:** the wēkiu bug, which scavenges insects blown up from below

### 30.13 Riparian and Stream Corridors

**Location:** windward gulches and valleys
**Plants:** ʻōhiʻa (including the streamside variety *newellii*), māmaki, ʻōlonā, haʻiwale, hāpuʻu, ferns, mosses
**Water life:** native gobies (ʻoʻopu), shrimp (ʻōpae) and snails (hīhīwai) that migrate between stream and sea

### 30.14 Lava Barrens

**Location:** recent flows on Kīlauea and Mauna Loa; the Kaʻū Desert
**Plants:** lichens, mosses, ʻamaʻu, sword ferns, ʻōhiʻa seedlings and pūkiawe in cracks
**Notes:** succession stage depends on flow age and rainfall (see Section 32).

### 30.15 Lava Tubes and Caves

- Twilight zones near entrances hold ferns and mosses.
- ʻŌhiʻa roots hang from cave ceilings, feeding blind cave insects and spiders.
- Dry tubes preserve organic remains for long periods.

### 30.16 Cliffs and Gulch Walls

- Windward cliffs carry hanging gardens of ferns, lobelias and shrubs.
- Sea cliffs hold salt-tolerant shrubs and nesting seabirds.

## 31. Big Island Sector by Sector

### 31.1 Hāmākua (Northeast)

- Coast: sea cliffs 100-200 m tall cut by gulches; hala and loulu on slopes
- Lowland: wet ʻōhiʻa-hāpuʻu forest, deep ash soils
- Upland: koa-ʻōhiʻa cloud forest to about 2,000 m
- Above: māmane-naio subalpine woodland on Mauna Kea
- Water: many perennial streams
- Timber: excellent koa and ʻōhiʻa; drying requires transport leeward or heated sheds

### 31.2 Hilo (East)

- Coast: Hilo Bay; hala and loulu; Wailuku River
- Lowland: wet ʻōhiʻa forest with tree ferns; rainfall about 126 in at the coast
- Upland: extremely wet forest at 600-1,000 m (200-300 in)
- Above: cloud forest, then subalpine

### 31.3 Puna (Southeast)

- Young lava throughout; Kīlauea's east rift zone
- Coast: black sand beaches, anchialine pools, hala groves
- Lowland: ʻōhiʻa forest of varying ages on lava; lava tree molds
- Fresh flows in every succession stage

### 31.4 Kīlauea and Volcano Uplands

- Summit area around 1,200 m: wet ʻōhiʻa-hāpuʻu forest to the east, dry Kaʻū Desert to the southwest
- Kīpuka with koa, soapberry and rich mixed forest
- Upslope on Mauna Loa: koa-ʻōhiʻa montane mesic forest

### 31.5 Kaʻū (South)

- Coast: dry to moderate rainfall, rocky shore
- Kaʻū Desert: southwest of Kīlauea; sparse plants on ash and lava, made harsher by volcanic gas and acid rain
- Upper slopes: wet ʻōhiʻa and koa forest
- Southern tip (Ka Lae): windswept grassland and shrubland

### 31.6 South Kona (Southwest)

- Coast: dry with anchialine pools
- Lower slopes: lowland dry forest of wiliwili, lama and kauila
- Middle slopes: Kona cloud belt with rich mesic forest at 600-1,800 m
- Upper slopes: koa-ʻōhiʻa, then māmane, then Mauna Loa subalpine

### 31.7 North Kona (West)

- Coast: very dry lava coastline with anchialine pools and springs
- Lower slopes: lowland dry forest, one of the richest in Hawaiʻi (kokiʻo, uhiuhi, kauila, ʻiliahi, wiliwili, lama, olopua)
- Hualālai slopes: mesic forest belt, then montane dry forest of māmane and naio
- Puʻu Waʻawaʻa: cinder cone with exceptional dry forest diversity

### 31.8 Kohala Coast and Waikoloa (Northwest)

- Driest area of the island; Kawaihae receives about 8-10 in
- Lowland dry forest and shrubland: wiliwili, ʻaʻaliʻi, naio, ʻilima, loulu in gulches
- Best natural drying yard on the island; wood seasoning conditions excellent

### 31.9 Kohala Mountain (North)

- Oldest volcano with deeply weathered soils
- Windward valleys (Waipiʻo, Waimanu, Pololū): wet forest, waterfalls, streams
- Summit: wet forest and bogs
- Leeward slopes: mesic forest grading to dry

### 31.10 Waimea Plateau (North-Central)

- Gap between Kohala and Mauna Kea where trade winds pour through
- Wet on the east, dry on the west within a few miles
- Natural vegetation: koa-ʻōhiʻa forest on the wet side, mesic and dry forest on the west

### 31.11 The Saddle (Central)

- High plateau between Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa, about 1,800-2,100 m
- Dry māmane-naio woodland on Mauna Kea's flank
- Vast young lava fields from Mauna Loa with sparse shrubs
- Cold nights, frost, dry air
- Fast natural wood drying with heavy checking risk

### 31.12 The Summits

- Mauna Kea: subalpine woodland, alpine shrubland, alpine desert, Lake Waiau, glacial features, winter snow
- Mauna Loa: mostly bare lava above 3,000 m; Kaʻū silversword on upper slopes


## 32. Lava Succession

### 32.1 Wet Side (over 100 in rain)

| Years after flow | Vegetation |
|---|---|
| 0-1 | Bare rock; cyanobacteria and algae films |
| 1-5 | Lichens (*Stereocaulon*, which fixes nitrogen); mosses; ʻamaʻu and sword ferns in cracks |
| 5-50 | ʻŌhiʻa seedlings; pūkiawe; ʻōhelo; uluhe fern mats spread |
| 50-200 | Open ʻōhiʻa woodland with dense fern understory |
| 200-400+ | Closed ʻōhiʻa forest with tree ferns |
| 1,000+ | Mature mixed forest; koa and many subcanopy species on deeper soils |
| 10,000+ | Deep soils; full species-rich forest |

### 32.2 Dry Side (under 50 in rain)

| Years after flow | Vegetation |
|---|---|
| 0-50 | Nearly bare; lichens only |
| 50-200 | Scattered ferns and shrubs in cracks |
| 200-1,000 | Open shrubland with scattered small trees |
| 1,000-3,000+ | Dry forest on flows that have gathered soil |

### 32.3 Lava Type Effects

- ʻAʻā clinker traps moisture, dust and seeds in its many gaps. It is colonized faster than smooth pāhoehoe.
- Pāhoehoe is colonized first along cracks and pressure ridges.
- Ash and cinder deposits are colonized faster than solid flows.

### 32.4 Kīpuka

Islands of older forest surrounded by younger lava act as seed sources and refuges. Each kīpuka is a time capsule of the forest that stood before the flow.

## 33. Native Animals That Shape the Plants

### 33.1 Birds (Full Pre-Human Assemblage)

**Honeycreepers (main pollinators and insect hunters):**
- ʻIʻiwi: scarlet, curved bill; feeds on lobelia and ʻōhiʻa nectar
- ʻApapane: crimson; ʻōhiʻa nectar specialist
- ʻAmakihi: yellow-green generalist
- ʻAkiapōlāʻau: chisels into koa bark for insects
- ʻAkepa: crossbill-like bill for prying buds
- Palila: māmane seed specialist
- Hawaiʻi creeper, ʻōʻū and many others

**Honeyeaters:** Hawaiʻi ʻōʻō with black plumage and yellow tufts; important pollinators

**Seed dispersers:**
- ʻŌmaʻo (Hawaiian thrush): spreads fleshy-fruited plants
- ʻAlalā (Hawaiian crow): spreads large-fruited trees and loulu

**Predators:** ʻio (Hawaiian hawk), pueo (short-eared owl)

**Waterbirds:** nēnē, koloa (Hawaiian duck), ʻalae keʻokeʻo (coot), ʻalae ʻula (gallinule), ʻaukuʻu (night heron), aeʻo (stilt)

**Flightless birds of the Big Island:** Hawaiian rails and a giant goose. Other islands held the moa-nalo, large flightless ducks that browsed like grazing mammals.

**Seabirds nesting inland:** ʻuaʻu (Hawaiian petrel) in burrows up to high elevations; ʻaʻo (Newell's shearwater); band-rumped storm petrel. Their colonies carry ocean nutrients inland as guano.

### 33.2 Mammals

- ʻŌpeʻapeʻa (Hawaiian hoary bat): the only native land mammal
- ʻĪlio holo i ka uaua (Hawaiian monk seal): on beaches

### 33.3 Reptiles and Amphibians

No native land reptiles or amphibians. Honu (green sea turtles) and honuʻea (hawksbills) nest on beaches.

### 33.4 Insects and Invertebrates

- Over 500 species of Hawaiian picture-wing and other Drosophila flies
- Hylaeus (yellow-faced bees): about 60 native species; key pollinators of many native plants
- Kamehameha butterfly; Blackburn's sphinx moth
- Happy face spider
- Wood-boring longhorn beetles
- Kāhuli (tree snails) with hundreds of species
- Wēkiu bug on Mauna Kea's summit
- No mosquitoes, so no avian malaria; native birds thrive from coast to treeline

### 33.5 Effects on the Plant Landscape

- Tubular curved flowers across many genera match honeycreeper bills.
- Many plants lost thorns, toxins and stinging hairs in the absence of mammal browsers.
- Fleshy fruits rely on ʻōmaʻo and ʻalalā for dispersal.
- No rats means palm and hardwood seeds survive, sustaining loulu forests and dense dry forests.
- Seabird guano enriches high-elevation soils.

## 34. Wood Selection Guide for Native Species

### 34.1 By Use

| Use | Best species | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Posts in ground | Māmane, kauila, naio, ʻaʻaliʻi (small sizes) | Use heartwood; char the buried section |
| Beams and framing | ʻŌhiʻa, kōlea, ʻālaʻa, naio, lama | Season fully before load |
| Planks, boards, furniture | Koa | Most stable and workable |
| Canoe hulls | Koa | Large old trees from montane forest |
| Canoe parts (gunwales) | ʻAhakea | Yellow, durable |
| Floats, rafts | Wiliwili | Keep dry between uses |
| Spears | Kauila, uhiuhi, olopua, koaiʻa, māmane | Fire-harden tips |
| Digging sticks | Alaheʻe, māmane, kauila | |
| Tool and adze handles | Olopua, koaiʻa, ʻōhiʻa, kauila | Straight-grained pieces |
| Mallets and anvils | Kōpiko, hame, kōlea | Fine, tough grain |
| Bowls and containers | Koa | Rough-turn green, dry slowly |
| Carving | Koa, ʻōhiʻa, naio | Green for roughing; dry for finish |
| Aromatic objects | ʻIliahi, naio | Use old heartwood |
| Instruments | Koa | Improves acoustically with age |
| Firewood | ʻŌhiʻa, māmane, naio, lama, ʻaʻaliʻi | Season 6-24 months |
| Charcoal | ʻŌhiʻa, lama | Dense, long-burning |
| Fire-plow hand stick | Olomea | Hard |
| Fire-plow base | Pāpala, wiliwili, ʻohe makai | Soft, light |

### 34.2 Non-Wood Materials From Native Trees

| Material | Source |
|---|---|
| Thatch | Loulu leaves; hala leaves |
| Weaving | Hala leaves; makaloa sedge |
| Cordage | ʻŌlonā (strongest); māmaki; ʻōpuhe; kalia; ʻākia |
| Stuffing and dressing | Hāpuʻu pulu |
| Soap | ʻAʻe (soapberry) fruit |
| Bird glue | Pāpala kēpau seeds |
| Fish stunning | ʻĀkia bark and roots |
| Red dye | Kōlea bark; kokiʻo bark |
| Yellow dye | ʻAhakea bark; hōlei bark and roots; nānū fruit |
| Blue-black dye | ʻŌlapa fruit |
| Purple dye | Hame fruit |
| Scent | ʻIliahi heartwood; naio wood; maile vine |
| Edible fruit | Lama, ʻōhelo, ʻōhiʻa hā, nīoi, māmaki, hala keys, loulu seeds |
| Edible starch | Hāpuʻu trunk core (cooked) |

### 34.3 Seasoning Plan by Species Group

| Group | Species | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Light woods | Wiliwili, pāpala, ʻohe makai, ʻaiea, ʻōlapa | Dry fast in airy shade; keep off ground; use within years unless kept dry |
| Medium woods | Koa, kāwaʻu, ʻaʻe | Sticker and weight; shade figured stock; 6-12 months per 25 mm |
| Heavy woods | ʻŌhiʻa, māmane, naio, kōlea, ʻālaʻa | Seal ends; shade; heavy weight; 12-18 months per 25 mm; split rather than saw where possible |
| Very heavy woods | Kauila, uhiuhi, lama, olopua, mēhamehame | Small dimensions only; seal ends; very slow drying in deep shade; 18-24 months or more |
| Aromatic woods | ʻIliahi, naio | Dry slowly in shade; store covered to hold scent |

### 34.4 Long-Term Aging Plan

1. Season to local EMC.
2. Store under cover in the driest available zone, off the ground, with airflow.
3. For maximum strength and stability, use wood aged several decades to a few centuries.
4. For tools that take shock (spears, handles, mallets), use well-seasoned but not ancient wood, since toughness falls steadily with age.
5. For instruments, longer aging improves tone.
6. Keep wood in upland stores (cooler) to slow aging chemistry; keep it in warm lowland stores to speed it.
