## Foundations: Why Hawaii Has So Many Biomes

### Geology and Island Age

The Hawaiian Islands are the exposed peaks of a volcanic chain built as the Pacific Plate moves northwest over a stationary hotspot. The result is an age gradient running from southeast to northwest:

| Island | Approximate age of oldest exposed rock | Highest point |
|---|---|---|
| Kauaʻi | 5.1 million years | Kawaikini, 5,243 ft |
| Oʻahu | 3.7 to 2.6 million years (Waiʻanae and Koʻolau volcanoes) | Kaʻala, 4,025 ft |
| Molokaʻi | about 1.9 million years | Kamakou, 4,961 ft |
| Maui | about 1.3 million years (West Maui) | Haleakalā, 10,023 ft |
| Hawaiʻi Island | under 1 million years; parts are days old | Mauna Kea, 13,803 ft |

This gradient matters enormously for ecology. Older islands are lower and more eroded with deep valleys, thick weathered soils and fewer high-elevation habitats. Younger islands are tall and steep with thin soils, active lava and the only true alpine zones in the state. Kauaʻi has the most species and the highest local endemism because life has had the longest time to diversify there. Hawaiʻi Island has the widest range of environments because it alone spans sea level to nearly 14,000 feet.

A new volcano, Kamaʻehuakanaloa (formerly called Lōʻihi), is currently building on the seafloor southeast of Hawaiʻi Island and remains about 3,000 feet below the surface.

### Climate Engines

**Trade winds.** Northeast trade winds blow most of the year. Moist air hits the windward (northeast-facing) slopes and rises, cools and drops its rain. The leeward (southwest-facing) slopes sit in a rain shadow. Rainfall gradients are among the steepest on Earth. On Kauaʻi the summit region near Waiʻaleʻale receives long-term averages reported between roughly 370 and 460 inches per year depending on the period measured. Less than 15 miles away the Mānā coastal plain receives around 20 inches. On Hawaiʻi Island the Kawaihae coast receives under 10 inches while windward Hilo receives about 130 inches.

**The trade wind inversion.** A layer of warm dry air typically sits around 6,000 to 7,000 feet. It acts as a lid on cloud formation. Below it windward slopes are wet; above it conditions abruptly become dry, sunny and prone to frost. This single atmospheric feature creates the sharp boundary between rainforest and subalpine shrubland on Haleakalā, Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa.

**Elevation and temperature.** Temperature drops about 3.5 °F per 1,000 feet. Sea level averages in the mid 70s to low 80s °F. The summit of Mauna Kea sees nightly freezing and winter snow.

**Kona storms and seasonality.** Winter brings occasional Kona storms from the southwest that deliver much of the annual rain to leeward areas. Summer is drier across most of the state.

Taken together these factors give the islands most of the world's major climate types within a land area smaller than New Jersey.

### Isolation and Evolution

Hawaii lies about 2,400 miles from the nearest continent. Very few organisms ever arrived. Botanists estimate that the entire native flowering plant flora of roughly 1,000 to 1,100 species descends from only about 270 to 290 successful colonists. Those arrivals were carried by wind, by floating on ocean currents or by birds (in feathers, mud on feet or in guts).

Successful colonists diversified spectacularly. This process is called adaptive radiation. Key Hawaiian examples:

- **Hawaiian honeycreepers:** more than 50 known species descended from a single rosefinch ancestor that arrived roughly 6 to 7 million years ago.
- **Hawaiian lobelioids:** more than 125 species from a single colonist that arrived about 13 million years ago on older islands now eroded below sea level. This is the largest plant radiation from one colonist known on any island system.
- **Silversword alliance:** about 30 species in three genera (*Argyroxiphium*, *Dubautia*, *Wilkesia*) descended from a California tarweed ancestor about 5 million years ago.
- **Hawaiian Drosophilidae:** an estimated 1,000 species of fruit flies.
- **Land snails:** more than 750 described species historically.

Endemism rates are among the highest anywhere. About 90 percent of native flowering plants are endemic. Native insect endemism approaches 98 percent. Many groups common elsewhere never arrived naturally at all: there were no native ants, no native mosquitoes, no native reptiles or amphibians on land, no snakes and only one native land mammal (the Hawaiian hoary bat).

This isolation produced plants that lost defenses (thorns, bitter chemicals, strong scents) because no browsing mammals existed. That loss left them extremely vulnerable once goats, cattle, pigs and deer arrived.

### Human Arrival and Transformation

Polynesian voyagers settled the islands roughly 1000 to 1200 CE according to most recent radiocarbon studies. They brought about two dozen "canoe plants" (kalo, ʻulu, kō, maiʻa, niu, kukui, kī and others) along with pigs, dogs, chickens and the Polynesian rat. Lowland forests were cleared for agriculture and fire was used to maintain pili grass. Dozens of bird species went extinct in this period, including flightless ducks (moa-nalo), flightless ibises, a long-legged owl and several rails.

Hawaiians organized land into **ahupuaʻa**, wedge-shaped divisions running from mountain to sea. Each ahupuaʻa ideally contained a full sequence of biomes so its people could access forest resources, farmland, streams and reef. Forest zones were named by function: the **wao akua** (upper forest, realm of the gods, rarely entered) and the **wao kanaka** (lower zone used by people).

Western contact in 1778 accelerated change. Goats, cattle, sheep, horses, deer, mongooses, mosquitoes, avian disease and thousands of nonnative plants arrived over the following two centuries. Sugar and pineapple plantations converted much of the lowlands. Hawaii now has more federally listed threatened and endangered species than any other state, accounting for a large share of all listed plants in the United States despite covering a tiny fraction of its land area.

### How Ecologists Classify Hawaiian Ecosystems

The standard framework (Gagné and Cuddihy, 1990) uses two axes:

- **Elevation zone:** coastal, lowland, montane, subalpine, alpine.
- **Moisture regime:** dry (under about 50 inches per year), mesic (about 50 to 100 inches) and wet (over about 100 inches).

Each combination is further divided by vegetation structure (forest, shrubland, grassland, herbland) and dominant species. The fourteen biomes in this guide follow that logic with additional sections for habitats the framework treats separately (reefs, anchialine pools, caves, lava flows, freshwater).

## 1. Coral Reefs

### Overview

Hawaiian reefs are geologically young, relatively low in coral species and extraordinarily high in endemism. They make up a large majority of the coral reef area under United States jurisdiction once the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands are included.

### Distribution and Structure

- **Fringing reefs** grow directly against the shorelines of the main islands. Most are narrow because the islands are steep and young and because high wave energy limits reef buildup.
- **Kāneʻohe Bay** on windward Oʻahu holds the only true barrier reef in the main islands, separated from shore by a lagoon.
- **Atolls** dominate the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands. Kure Atoll is the northernmost coral atoll in the world. It sits near the "Darwin Point," the latitude beyond which coral growth cannot keep pace with island subsidence, so reefs there are close to drowning.
- **Mesophotic reefs** occur at roughly 100 to 500 feet in dim light. ʻAuʻau Channel between Maui and Lānaʻi holds some of the most extensive mesophotic coral beds known anywhere.

Reef development is shaped by wave exposure. North and east shores face large winter swells and support wave-resistant encrusting corals. Leeward Kona and Kohala coasts are calmer and hold more delicate finger coral.

### Corals

Hawaii has only around 60 species of reef-building stony corals compared with 500 or more in the Coral Triangle. A notable share are endemic. Dominant species:

- **Lobe coral** (*Porites lobata*): massive mounding colonies, the main reef builder statewide.
- **Finger coral** (*Porites compressa*): dense thickets in calm water; dominant in Kāneʻohe Bay and sheltered Kona reefs.
- **Cauliflower coral** (*Pocillopora meandrina*, koʻa): branching heads on exposed shallow reef.
- **Rice coral** (*Montipora capitata*): plating and branching forms, often the first to bleach.
- **Blue rice coral** (*Montipora flabellata*): endemic, bright blue, found in high-energy shallow water.
- **Corrugated coral** (*Pavona varians*) and **crust coral** (*Leptastrea*): encrusting species on rocky surfaces.

Coral growth in Hawaii is slower than in most tropical regions because water temperatures are relatively cool for corals (roughly 73 to 81 °F annually).

The Kumulipo, the Hawaiian creation chant, begins with the birth of the coral polyp ('uku koʻakoʻa). Coral is literally the first living thing in the Hawaiian cosmological sequence.

### Algae

More than 500 species of marine algae (limu) occur in Hawaii. Crustose coralline algae cement reefs together. Limu was a staple food; species such as limu kohu (*Asparagopsis taxiformis*) and limu manauea (*Gracilaria coronopifolia*) remain culturally important.

### Fish

About a quarter of Hawaiian shallow reef fish species are endemic, one of the highest rates of any tropical marine region. At mesophotic depths in Papahānaumokuākea, surveys have found fish communities that are almost entirely endemic.

Notable reef fish:

- **Humuhumunukunukuāpuaʻa** (reef triggerfish, *Rhinecanthus rectangulus*): the state fish.
- **Hawaiian cleaner wrasse** (*Labroides phthirophagus*): endemic; runs cleaning stations where larger fish queue for parasite removal.
- **Saddle wrasse** (hīnālea lauwili): endemic and abundant.
- **Yellow tang** (lauʻīpala): a major target of the aquarium trade, which was restricted by court rulings after 2017.
- **Potter's angelfish**, **bandit angelfish** and **Hawaiian morwong**: endemic.
- **Uhu** (parrotfish): crucial grazers that keep algae in check and grind coral into sand. A single large parrotfish can produce hundreds of pounds of sand a year.
- **Manini** (convict tang), **kala** (unicornfish) and **ʻāweoweo** (bigeye).

### Other Fauna

- **Honu** (green sea turtle): most nest at Lalo (French Frigate Shoals) in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands. Hurricane Walaka in 2018 washed away East Island, the most important nesting beach.
- **Hawaiian monk seal** (ʻīlio holo i ka uaua): endemic, about 1,600 individuals, one of the most endangered marine mammals.
- **Spinner dolphins** (naiʻa): rest in shallow bays by day and feed offshore at night.
- **Manta rays** (hāhālua): famous night aggregations off Kona.
- **Humpback whales** (koholā): winter breeding visitors, protected by a national marine sanctuary.
- **Invertebrates:** slate pencil urchins (hāʻukeʻuke), collector urchins, octopus (heʻe), spiny lobster (ula), Spanish dancer nudibranchs and crown-of-thorns starfish.

### Human History

Hawaiians managed reefs through the konohiki system. Local land managers set kapu (restrictions) on species or areas by season. **Loko iʻa** (fishponds) were built along reef flats with stone walls and mākāhā (sluice gates) that let small fish enter and trapped them once grown. Hundreds existed before contact; several are now being restored, including Heʻeia Fishpond on Oʻahu.

### Threats

- **Marine heat waves and bleaching:** the 2014 to 2015 events caused roughly half of coral cover on west Hawaiʻi Island reefs to die. Bleaching recurred in 2019.
- **Land-based pollution:** sediment from erosion and runoff smothers corals. Hawaii has roughly 80,000 or more cesspools that leak nutrients into groundwater and nearshore waters.
- **Overfishing** of herbivores leads to algal overgrowth.
- **Invasive algae:** *Gracilaria salicornia*, *Kappaphycus* and *Acanthophora spicifera* smother reef flats. Oʻahu uses underwater vacuum barges ("super suckers") to remove them from Kāneʻohe Bay.
- **Sunscreen chemicals:** Hawaii banned sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate effective 2021.
- **Ocean acidification** slows coral calcification.

### Protection

- **Papahānaumokuākea:** established as a marine national monument in 2006, expanded in 2016 to about 582,000 square miles, inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2010 and designated a national marine sanctuary in January 2025. It is one of the largest protected areas on Earth.
- **Marine Life Conservation Districts:** Hanauma Bay (the first, 1967), Molokini, Kealakekua Bay, Pūpūkea and others.
- **Community-Based Subsistence Fishing Areas:** Hāʻena on Kauaʻi (2015) was the first, codifying traditional rules into state law.

## 2. Coastal Strand

### Overview

The coastal strand is the narrow band between the high tide line and the start of lowland vegetation. It is shaped by salt spray, wind, sand movement and storm waves. Unlike most Hawaiian biomes many plants here are indigenous rather than endemic. Their seeds float and are spread across the Pacific by ocean currents, so Hawaiian populations stay genetically connected to populations elsewhere.

### Habitat Types

- **Sandy beaches:** white sand is biogenic, built from coral fragments, coralline algae, mollusk shells and foraminifera. Black sand forms when lava hits the ocean and shatters (Punaluʻu). Papakōlea on Hawaiʻi Island is one of only a few green sand beaches in the world; its color comes from olivine crystals eroded from a cinder cone.
- **Dunes:** the largest dune systems are at Moʻomomi on Molokaʻi, Polihale and Mānā on Kauaʻi and Kaʻena Point on Oʻahu. Some Moʻomomi dunes are lithified (cemented into dune rock) and contain fossil bird bones.
- **Rocky shores and sea cliffs:** basalt benches with tide pools; cliff faces hold salt-tolerant shrubs and nesting seabirds.
- **Coastal salt marshes and flats:** small areas with pickleweed and sedges.

### Plants

Plants here share adaptations: succulent or hairy leaves, low prostrate growth, deep roots and tolerance for salt.

- **Naupaka kahakai** (*Scaevola taccada*): thick succulent leaves and white "half flowers." In legend the flower was split between separated lovers; the mountain naupaka holds the other half.
- **Pōhuehue** (beach morning glory, *Ipomoea pes-caprae*): long runners that stabilize sand.
- **ʻAkulikuli** (*Sesuvium portulacastrum*): succulent mat former.
- **ʻAkiʻaki** (*Sporobolus virginicus*): salt-tolerant grass on sand.
- **Hinahina kū kahakai** (*Heliotropium anomalum*): silvery groundcover; Kahoʻolawe's official lei material.
- **ʻIlima** (*Sida fallax*): yellow-orange flowers used in lei; the official flower of Oʻahu.
- **Pāʻū o Hiʻiaka** (*Jacquemontia sandwicensis*): endemic blue-flowered vine.
- **Nanea** (*Vigna marina*): yellow-flowered beach pea.
- **Kaunaʻoa** (*Cuscuta sandwichiana*): an endemic parasitic dodder; orange threadlike stems; Lānaʻi's official lei.
- **Maʻo** (Hawaiian cotton, *Gossypium tomentosum*): endemic with brown lint-free seeds.
- **ʻOhai** (*Sesbania tomentosa*): endangered endemic with salmon-red flowers.
- **Nehe** (*Lipochaeta* / *Melanthera*): yellow daisies.
- **Hala** (*Pandanus tectorius*): stilt-rooted trees whose leaves (lauhala) were woven into mats, sails and baskets.
- **Kou** (*Cordia subcordata*) and **milo** (*Thespesia populnea*): shade trees probably brought or spread by Polynesians, prized for carving bowls.
- **Makaloa** (*Cyperus laevigatus*): sedge used for Niʻihau's famously fine mats.

### Fauna

**Seabirds** are the defining animals of the strand. Predator-free islets and fenced headlands hold large colonies.

- **Mōlī** (Laysan albatross): nests at Kaʻena Point and on Kauaʻi's north shore as well as in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands.
- **ʻUaʻu kani** (wedge-tailed shearwater): burrows in sandy soil; known for eerie moaning calls at night.
- **Koaʻe kea** (white-tailed tropicbird): nests on cliffs.
- **ʻIwa** (great frigatebird): steals food from other seabirds in flight; traditionally a sign of approaching weather.
- **ʻĀ** (boobies): brown, red-footed and masked boobies.
- **Noio** (black noddy) and **manu-o-Kū** (white tern, the official bird of Honolulu, which lays its single egg directly on a bare branch).

**Shorebirds:** the **kōlea** (Pacific golden plover) migrates about 3,000 miles nonstop from Alaska each fall and returns to the same lawn or beach year after year. Hawaiians admired its navigation.

**Other animals:**

- Hawaiian monk seals haul out to rest and pup.
- Green sea turtles bask on beaches, a behavior uncommon elsewhere.
- **ʻŌhiki** (ghost crabs) dig burrows above the tide line.
- **Hawaiian yellow-faced bees** (*Hylaeus*): seven species were listed as endangered in 2016; several depend on coastal plants such as naupaka and ʻilima.

### Ecological Processes

Salt spray prunes vegetation into wind-sculpted forms, so plants closest to the water are lowest. Strand vegetation stabilizes sand and buffers inland areas from storm surge. Seabird guano historically enriched coastal soils with nitrogen and phosphorus; this nutrient subsidy disappeared wherever colonies were lost.

### Human History

Coastal zones were the most densely settled areas in pre-contact Hawaii. Burials were commonly placed in dunes, which is why dune development remains culturally sensitive. Salt was harvested in shallow pans; the salt beds at Hanapēpē on Kauaʻi are still worked by families today.

### Threats

- **Development** of hotels, homes and seawalls. Seawalls cause beaches to narrow and disappear.
- **Sea level rise:** state planning uses projections of about 3 feet by 2100; much of the strand has nowhere to retreat.
- **Invasive plants:** **kiawe** (*Prosopis pallida*), first planted in 1828 from a single seed at the Catholic mission in Honolulu, now dominates leeward coasts with thorny thickets. Other invaders include ironwood, tree heliotrope, buffelgrass and fountain grass.
- **Predators:** the small Indian mongoose (introduced in 1883 to control rats in cane fields) along with feral cats, dogs and rats devastate ground-nesting seabirds. Mongooses never established widely on Kauaʻi, which partly explains why Kauaʻi retains more ground-nesting birds.
- **Off-road vehicles** crush plants and burrows.

### Conservation Highlights

- **Kaʻena Point Natural Area Reserve** (Oʻahu): a predator-proof fence completed in 2011 allowed albatross and shearwater numbers to rise sharply.
- **Moʻomomi Preserve** (Molokaʻi): one of the best-preserved dune systems in the main islands.
- **Kīlauea Point National Wildlife Refuge** (Kauaʻi): large seabird colonies.

## 3. Anchialine Pools

### Overview

Anchialine pools are landlocked bodies of brackish water with no surface connection to the sea. They connect to the ocean through underground cracks in porous lava or limestone. Water levels rise and fall with the tides while fresh groundwater flowing downslope floats on denser seawater beneath. The result is a pool with a salinity gradient that changes by depth and by distance from shore.

### Distribution

Hawaii holds one of the greatest concentrations of anchialine pools on Earth, estimated at several hundred. The large majority are on Hawaiʻi Island along the young lava coasts of Kona and Kohala. Smaller numbers occur on Maui (ʻĀhihi-Kīnaʻu and La Perouse Bay), in limestone sinkholes at Kalaeloa on Oʻahu and on Molokaʻi and Kahoʻolawe.

Pools are typically shallow (a few inches to several feet), range in size from small cracks to ponds of an acre or more and age naturally: pools gradually fill with sediment and organic material and become marshes, while new lava flows create new pools.

### Fauna

- **ʻŌpaeʻula** (*Halocaridina rubra*): a tiny endemic red shrimp, usually under half an inch long. It grazes bacterial and algal films. It moves between pools through the underground water table and can retreat into the subterranean network. Laboratory animals have lived more than 20 years, extraordinary for a shrimp this size. It is famous in the aquarium hobby for surviving in sealed "ecospheres."
- **Metabetaeus lohena:** a predatory endemic shrimp that hunts ʻōpaeʻula.
- **Rare shrimp:** *Procaris hawaiana*, *Calliasmata pholidota* and *Vetericaris chaceorum*. The last was discovered in 1985 at a single pit crater site, Lua o Palahemo near South Point, and represents an ancient lineage.
- **Snails:** small neritid snails graze pool margins.
- **Orangeblack Hawaiian damselfly** (*Megalagrion xanthomelas*): an endemic damselfly that breeds in some anchialine pools.
- **Microbial mats:** cyanobacteria and diatoms form the base of the food web and give pools green, orange or red tints. Pool colors shift as shrimp grazing pressure changes.

### Ecology

Without fish, ʻōpaeʻula graze at the surface and keep algae short. When fish are introduced the shrimp retreat underground, algae explodes and the pool begins to fill with organic sludge. Pools with healthy shrimp populations stay open and clear for far longer. ʻŌpaeʻula are therefore a keystone species for pool persistence.

### Human History

Hawaiians used pools as sources of brackish drinking water, for bathing and as places to raise or hold fish. ʻŌpaeʻula were collected as bait for ʻōpelu (mackerel scad) fishing: the shrimp were chummed into the water to attract schools. Many pools were enlarged and modified into fishponds; Kalahuipuaʻa at Mauna Lani and the ʻAnaehoʻomalu ponds at Waikoloa are examples. Some pools have names, legends and associated ʻaumakua (family guardians).

### Threats

- **Introduced fish:** tilapia, guppies, mosquitofish and mollies are the single greatest threat. They eat or displace shrimp and trigger the algal collapse cycle described above.
- **Development:** resort and residential construction along the Kona coast filled or bulldozed many pools.
- **Groundwater contamination:** fertilizer and golf course runoff along with cesspool and wastewater seepage.
- **Groundwater withdrawal:** pumping reduces freshwater flow and changes salinity.
- **Lava:** the 2018 Kīlauea eruption buried the Waiʻōpae (Kapoho) tide pools and boiled away Green Lake (Ka Wai o Pele), the largest natural freshwater lake on Hawaiʻi Island.

### Where to See Them

- **Kīholo Bay** (Hawaiʻi Island), including the Queen's Bath lava tube pool.
- **Waikoloa Anchialine Pond Preservation Area.**
- **ʻĀhihi-Kīnaʻu Natural Area Reserve** (Maui).

Visitors should never swim in, step in or apply sunscreen near pools.

## 4. Lowland Dry Forest and Shrubland

### Overview

Lowland dry forest once covered much of the leeward lowlands of every main island. It is now one of the most endangered ecosystems in the United States. Estimates of loss generally exceed 90 percent. What remains is mostly scattered trees in a sea of nonnative grass.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** sea level to about 3,000 feet on leeward slopes.
- **Rainfall:** under about 50 inches per year, sometimes under 20. Most falls in winter; summers can be rainless for months.
- **Substrate:** often rocky young lava (Kona, Kohala) or older weathered soils (Waiʻanae Mountains, West Maui).

Major remnants: North Kona and South Kohala on Hawaiʻi Island (Kaʻūpūlehu, Puʻu Waʻawaʻa, Waikoloa), Auwahi and Kanaio on leeward Haleakalā, Kānepuʻu on Lānaʻi, the Waiʻanae Mountains on Oʻahu and parts of Kauaʻi's leeward valleys.

### Plants

Historically Hawaiian dry forests were surprisingly diverse with numerous tree species, many with dense, heavy wood.

- **Wiliwili** (*Erythrina sandwicensis*): drought-deciduous, dropping leaves in summer. Flowers range from orange to yellow, green and white. Its very light wood was used for surfboards, canoe outriggers and fishing floats. Bright red-orange seeds were strung into lei.
- **Lama** (*Diospyros sandwicensis*): native ebony. The name means "light" or "enlightenment," and lama wood was placed on hula altars dedicated to Laka.
- **ʻAlaheʻe** (*Psydrax odorata*): fragrant white flowers; hard wood for digging sticks (ʻōʻō).
- **ʻIliahi** (sandalwood, *Santalum* species): the sandalwood trade of roughly 1790 to 1840 sent enormous volumes of fragrant heartwood to China. Commoners were forced to harvest it and the trade nearly eliminated accessible stands.
- **Uhiuhi** (*Mezoneuron kavaiense*): endangered legume with extremely hard, dark wood used for spears and kapa beaters.
- **Kauila** (*Alphitonia ponderosa* and *Colubrina oppositifolia*): among the densest native woods; used for spears and tools. Kauila is the name of a female deity associated with the tree.
- **Maʻo hau hele** (*Hibiscus brackenridgei*): endangered yellow hibiscus, the state flower.
- **Kokiʻo** (*Kokia* species): hibiscus relatives with large red flowers. *Kokia cookei* of Molokaʻi survived for decades only as grafts onto a related species after its last wild trees died. *Kokia drynarioides* survives in a handful of trees in North Kona.
- **Hala pepe** (*Dracaena*): yucca-like trees reserved for hula altars.
- **Olopua** (*Nestegis sandwicensis*): native olive; dominant with lama at Kānepuʻu.
- **ʻAʻaliʻi** (*Dodonaea viscosa*): hardy shrub with papery red seed capsules used in lei. A proverb says "I am the ʻaʻaliʻi, no wind can push me over."
- **Naio** (*Myoporum sandwicense*), **ʻūlei** (*Osteomeles anthyllidifolia*), **ʻohe makai** (*Polyscias sandwicensis*) and **ʻaiea** (*Nothocestrum*).
- **Pili grass** (*Heteropogon contortus*): used to thatch houses. Pili grasslands were partly maintained by Hawaiian burning.

### Fauna

- **Blackburn's sphinx moth** (*Manduca blackburni*): the largest native Hawaiian insect, with a wingspan around 5 inches. It was listed as endangered in 2000. Its caterpillars feed on native ʻaiea and on introduced tree tobacco.
- **Hawaiian yellow-faced bees** pollinate many dry forest plants.
- **Hawaiian hoary bat** forages here.
- **Birds:** native bird life is now thin. Historically dry forests held the Kona grosbeak (extinct, with a massive bill for cracking naio seeds), the ʻalalā (Hawaiian crow, extinct in the wild since 2002) and many flightless birds known only from fossils.

### Ecological Processes

Dry forest trees evolved without fire. Today the dominant ecological process is a **grass-fire cycle**: invasive grasses such as fountain grass, buffelgrass and guinea grass grow quickly after rain, cure into dry fuel and carry fire into forest remnants. Each fire kills native trees and favors more grass. Many dry forest trees also lost their seed dispersers (flightless birds and the ʻalalā) and pollinators, so even surviving trees often fail to reproduce.

### Human History

These forests were heavily used by Hawaiians for timber, tools and medicine, then hit by cattle ranching. Cattle introduced in 1793 were placed under a kapu by Kamehameha I and multiplied into wild herds that grazed lowland forests to bare ground. Goats were introduced in 1778, and axis deer arrived on Molokaʻi in 1867 (later Lānaʻi and Maui). Sandalwood harvest and later plantation agriculture finished much of the remainder.

### Threats

- Fire. The August 2023 Maui wildfires burned largely through fallow plantation land overgrown with nonnative grasses, illustrating the danger statewide.
- Feral goats, deer, cattle and pigs.
- Invasive grasses and trees (kiawe, koa haole, Christmas berry).
- Seed predation by rats.
- Loss of pollinators and dispersers.
- The **wiliwili gall wasp** (*Quadrastichus erythrinae*), which arrived in 2005 and deformed wiliwili across the state; a biocontrol wasp released in 2008 has largely suppressed it.

### Conservation Highlights

- **Auwahi** (Maui): beginning in 1997, fencing, removal of kikuyu grass and planting of fast-growing native shrubs as nurse plants allowed native canopy trees to return. Auwahi is now a model for dry forest restoration worldwide.
- **Kaʻūpūlehu Dry Forest** (Hawaiʻi Island): fenced since the 1950s with community-led restoration.
- **Kānepuʻu Preserve** (Lānaʻi): the largest remaining olopua and lama forest.
- **Waikoloa Dry Forest Preserve** and **Puʻu Waʻawaʻa Forest Reserve.**

## 5. Lowland Mesic Forest

### Overview

Mesic forests occupy the middle ground between dry and wet. Historically they held the highest tree diversity of any Hawaiian ecosystem, with dozens of tree species sometimes found in a single valley. They are now fragmented and invaded, but remaining stands on Kauaʻi and Oʻahu still hold extraordinary numbers of rare plants.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** roughly 1,000 to 4,000 feet, sometimes lower in sheltered valleys.
- **Rainfall:** about 50 to 100 inches per year with a noticeable dry season.
- **Locations:** Kōkeʻe and the rim of Waimea Canyon (Kauaʻi), the Waiʻanae Mountains (Oʻahu), parts of West Maui, leeward East Maui and Molokaʻi. On Hawaiʻi Island mesic forest grows in Kona's upland coffee belt and in Kaʻū.

### Plants

- **Koa** (*Acacia koa*): the largest native tree, reaching 100 feet. Mature koa produce flattened leaf stalks (phyllodes) instead of true leaves, an adaptation to drier conditions. Koa was used for the largest voyaging and fishing canoes and remains the premier Hawaiian hardwood.
- **ʻŌhiʻa lehua** (*Metrosideros polymorpha*): codominant with koa in many stands.
- **Hame** (*Antidesma*), **papala kēpau** (*Pisonia*), **kōpiko** (*Psychotria*), **hōʻawa** (*Pittosporum*), **ʻāulu** (*Sapindus oahuensis*), **kauila**, **lama**, **olopua** and **hōlei** (*Ochrosia*).
- **Kokiʻo keʻokeʻo** (*Hibiscus arnottianus* and relatives): white hibiscus, one of the few fragrant hibiscus species in the world.
- **Mokihana** (*Melicope anisata*): anise-scented berries strung into Kauaʻi's official lei.
- **Iliau** (*Wilkesia gymnoxiphium*): a silversword relative endemic to Kauaʻi's western slopes. It grows as a tall single-stemmed rosette and in some cases flowers once and dies.
- **Native lobelioids:** *Cyanea*, *Clermontia*, *Delissea* and *Lobelia*, often with curved tubular flowers that match the bills of nectar-feeding honeycreepers.
- **Schiedea:** a genus of about 30 endemic species, many critically endangered, showing remarkable variation in breeding systems.

### Fauna

- **ʻElepaio** (*Chasiempis*): monarch flycatchers on Kauaʻi, Oʻahu and Hawaiʻi Island. The Oʻahu ʻelepaio is endangered. In legend the ʻelepaio guided canoe makers: if it pecked a fallen koa log, the wood was rotten and unfit.
- **ʻAmakihi** and **ʻapapane:** the most disease-tolerant surviving honeycreepers.
- **Kahuli** (Oʻahu tree snails, *Achatinella*): about 40 species, all federally endangered and many already extinct. Their shells come in an astonishing range of colors and banding. Hawaiian lore says they sang in the forest. They were collected by the millions in the 1800s and later decimated by the predatory rosy wolf snail (*Euglandina rosea*), introduced in 1955 in a failed attempt to control the giant African snail.
- **Partulina** and other tree snails on Molokaʻi, Maui and Lānaʻi.
- **Happy face spider** (*Theridion grallator*): a tiny translucent spider whose abdomen often shows a smiley-face pattern, which varies by island and individual.
- **Megalagrion** damselflies along stream corridors.

### Ecological Processes

Mesic forests are where wet-adapted and dry-adapted species overlap, which explains the high diversity. They are also prone to occasional drought and fire, making them sensitive to invasive grasses at their dry edge. Leaf litter from koa adds nitrogen to soils through symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria in koa roots.

### Threats

- **Invasive plants:** strawberry guava, Christmas berry, Koster's curse (*Clidemia hirta*), lantana, silk oak and molasses grass.
- **Feral ungulates:** pigs and goats.
- **Predators:** rats eat seeds, snails and birds; Jackson's chameleons (introduced in the 1970s) eat native snails and insects.
- **Fire:** the Mākua Valley in the Waiʻanae Mountains has been repeatedly burned by fires linked to military training and roadsides.
- **Koa wilt** (a *Fusarium* fungal disease) and timber harvest.

### Conservation Highlights

The Oʻahu Army Natural Resources Program manages large areas of the Waiʻanae Mountains to protect dozens of endangered species, including captive rearing of tree snails in predator-proof enclosures. Kōkeʻe State Park and the Kuia Natural Area Reserve hold some of Kauaʻi's best mesic stands.

## 6. Lowland Wet Forest

### Overview

Lowland wet forest once blanketed windward slopes from near sea level into the mountains. Below about 1,000 feet very little native forest remains; what does is concentrated in Puna on Hawaiʻi Island, on steep windward cliffs and in remote valleys. Lowland wet forest is notable for its near absence of native forest birds, a consequence of disease rather than habitat loss.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** near sea level to about 3,000 feet on windward slopes.
- **Rainfall:** more than 100 inches per year, spread fairly evenly through the year.
- **Locations:** Puna and Hāmākua (Hawaiʻi Island), Keʻanae and Kīpahulu (Maui), Pelekunu and Wailau valleys on Molokaʻi's north shore, windward Koʻolau valleys (Oʻahu), Hanalei and Wainiha (Kauaʻi).

### Plants

- **ʻŌhiʻa lehua:** the dominant canopy tree on young lava in Puna, where it forms pure stands over ʻuluhe fern.
- **ʻUluhe** (*Dicranopteris linearis*): false staghorn fern that forms dense, almost impenetrable thickets on landslide scars and disturbed slopes. It repeatedly forks its fronds and can climb over other vegetation.
- **Hāpuʻu** (*Cibotium* tree ferns): tall ferns whose soft fibrous hairs (pulu) were exported as mattress and pillow stuffing in the mid 1800s.
- **ʻIeʻie** (*Freycinetia arborea*): a climbing vine with orange-red bracts. Its aerial roots were woven into fish traps and the frameworks of feathered helmets and images.
- **Loulu** (*Pritchardia*): about two dozen endemic fan palm species, the only native palms in Hawaii.
- **Haʻiwale** (*Cyrtandra*): about 60 Hawaiian species, mostly understory shrubs with white flowers and fleshy berries.
- **Māmaki** (*Pipturus albidus*): a small tree whose leaves are brewed as tea. It is the host plant for the **Kamehameha butterfly** (*Vanessa tameamea*), the state insect.
- **Kōpiko**, **hame**, **ʻōlapa** (*Cheirodendron*, whose leaves flutter like hula dancers) and **kanawao** (*Broussaisia*).
- **ʻOlonā** (*Touchardia latifolia*): produced one of the strongest natural plant fibers known, used for fishing line and the netting base of featherwork.

**Canoe plant zones:** in valley bottoms, Polynesian-introduced plants naturalized into a distinct forest: **kukui** (candlenut, the state tree, whose pale silvery canopy marks gulches across the islands), **ʻōhiʻa ʻai** (mountain apple), **kī** (ti), **ʻawa**, **maiʻa** (banana) and **hau**.

### Soils and Nutrient Gradients

Lowland wet forests on Hawaiʻi Island and Kauaʻi are a famous natural laboratory. Ecologist Peter Vitousek and colleagues studied a sequence of sites with similar climate but substrates ranging from a few hundred years to about 4 million years old. Young sites are limited by **nitrogen**, since fresh lava contains almost none. Middle-aged sites around 20,000 to 150,000 years are the most fertile. Very old sites on Kauaʻi become limited by **phosphorus** as weathering leaches it away. Over millions of years, phosphorus increasingly arrives as dust blown from central Asia.

### Fauna

- **Native forest birds** are largely absent below about 3,000 to 4,000 feet. The cause is **avian malaria** (*Plasmodium relictum*) and **avian pox**, carried by the southern house mosquito (*Culex quinquefasciatus*). The mosquito arrived in 1826 at Lahaina aboard the ship *Wellington*. Hawaiian birds evolved without these diseases and most species die quickly when infected. Mosquitoes breed abundantly in warm lowlands.
- **Exception:** the Hawaiʻi ʻamakihi has evolved some disease tolerance and now lives in lowland Puna forests, an example of rapid evolution in real time.
- **Hawaiian hoary bat**, Kamehameha butterfly, native damselflies, spiders and many native moths.

### Threats

- **Miconia** (*Miconia calvescens*): introduced in the early 1960s as an ornamental. It has huge purple-backed leaves, shades out everything below and can produce millions of seeds per plant per year. It devastated Tahiti and is a top target of Hawaii's invasive species committees.
- **Strawberry guava** (*Psidium cattleianum*): forms dense thickets and is spread by pigs and birds. A biocontrol scale insect was released in 2012.
- **Albizia** (*Falcataria moluccana*): an enormously fast-growing nitrogen-fixing tree that changes soil chemistry and drops branches during storms.
- **Kāhili ginger**, **African tulip tree**, **octopus tree** and **Koster's curse.**
- **Coqui frog** (*Eleutherodactylus coqui*): arrived in the late 1980s via nursery plants from Puerto Rico. Densities on Hawaiʻi Island have reached tens of thousands per acre in some areas. The frogs eat enormous numbers of native insects.
- **Little fire ant** (*Wasmannia auropunctata*): established in Puna; its stings can blind pets and wildlife.
- **Rapid ʻŌhiʻa Death:** two fungal pathogens (*Ceratocystis lukuohia* and *C. huliohia*) first noticed around 2010 in Puna. They have killed over a million ʻōhiʻa on Hawaiʻi Island and have been detected on Kauaʻi, Oʻahu and Maui.
- **Feral pigs:** uproot the understory, spread weed seeds and create wallows where mosquitoes breed.

### Conservation Highlights

- **Wao Kele o Puna:** one of the largest remaining lowland wet forests, now owned by the Office of Hawaiian Affairs.
- **Kīpahulu District** of Haleakalā National Park.
- **Pelekunu Preserve** on Molokaʻi: a remote valley with one of the most intact stream and forest systems in the state.

## 7. Montane Wet Forest and Cloud Forest

### Overview

Montane wet forest is the most intact and most celebrated Hawaiian biome. It is the last stronghold of native forest birds, the primary watershed for most of Hawaii's fresh water and home to the greatest concentration of surviving endemic plants and insects. Upper portions bathed in persistent cloud are true cloud forests, draped in mosses, liverworts and ferns.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** about 3,000 to 6,500 feet, capped by the trade wind inversion.
- **Rainfall:** typically 100 to 300 or more inches per year. Fog drip (cloud water captured by leaves and mosses) can add substantial extra water.
- **Temperatures:** cool, roughly 50 to 70 °F, with frequent mist.
- **Locations:** windward Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa (Hakalau, Kūlani, ʻŌlaʻa), Kīlauea's upper slopes, the Kohala Mountains, windward Haleakalā (Waikamoi, Hanawī, Koʻolau Forest Reserve), West Maui's Puʻu Kukui, Molokaʻi's Kamakou, the Koʻolau summit on Oʻahu and Kauaʻi's Alakaʻi Plateau.

### Forest Structure

- **Canopy:** ʻōhiʻa lehua dominates nearly everywhere, joined by koa on many slopes.
- **Subcanopy:** hāpuʻu tree ferns often form a continuous second layer 10 to 25 feet tall. Seedlings of ʻōhiʻa frequently germinate on fallen tree fern trunks, a key regeneration pathway.
- **Understory:** shrubs, lobelioids, mints, ferns and herbs.
- **Ground layer:** mosses, liverworts, ferns, sedges and leaf litter.
- **Epiphytes:** mosses, liverworts, filmy ferns, peperomias (ʻalaʻala wai nui) and occasionally one of the only three native orchids.

### Plants

**ʻŌhiʻa lehua** (*Metrosideros polymorpha*) is the keystone of Hawaiian forests. It is the most variable tree known in Hawaii: it grows as a sprawling shrub inches tall in bogs, as a 100-foot tree in rich forest and as a pioneer on bare lava. Leaves range from smooth to densely hairy. Its pom-pom flowers (lehua) are usually red but also occur in yellow, orange, salmon and white. It is a major nectar source for honeycreepers.

Other important plants:

- **Koa**, often forming a tall overstory above ʻōhiʻa.
- **Kāwaʻu** (*Ilex anomala*), **kōlea** (*Myrsine*), **ʻōlapa**, **pilo** (*Coprosma*), **naʻenaʻe** (*Dubautia*), **ʻōhelo** (*Vaccinium*), **pūkiawe** and **kanawao.**
- **ʻĀkala** (*Rubus hawaiensis*): the Hawaiian raspberry, which has nearly lost its thorns.
- **Lobelioids:** ʻōhāwai (*Clermontia*), *Cyanea* and *Trematolobelia*. Many have curved flowers matching honeycreeper bills. Several *Cyanea* species have prickles only on juvenile leaves, likely a holdover defense against extinct flightless browsing birds.
- **Native mints** (*Stenogyne*, *Phyllostegia*): most have lost their minty scent and are non-aromatic.
- **Native orchids:** only three species are native (*Anoectochilus sandvicensis*, *Liparis hawaiensis*, *Platanthera holochila*), versus more than 100 naturalized nonnative orchids.

### Birds

The montane wet forest is home to most of the surviving Hawaiian honeycreepers (Drepanidinae). Roughly 17 species survive of more than 50 known. Bill shapes evolved for every niche: nectar sipping, seed cracking, insect probing and bark prying.

**Hawaiʻi Island**
- **ʻIʻiwi** (*Drepanis coccinea*): scarlet with a long curved salmon-colored bill. Its feathers were used extensively in featherwork. Listed as threatened in 2017.
- **ʻApapane:** crimson with a black bill; the most abundant honeycreeper.
- **ʻAkiapōlāʻau:** endangered. It uses its short straight lower mandible like a woodpecker to chisel bark and its long curved upper mandible to extract larvae, a unique arrangement.
- **Hawaiʻi ʻākepa:** small and orange; nests in tree cavities of old ʻōhiʻa and koa. Its bill tips cross slightly to pry open leaf buds.
- **Hawaiʻi creeper** (ʻalawī) and **Hawaiʻi ʻamakihi.**
- **ʻŌmaʻo** (Hawaiʻi thrush): a native thrush, the last surviving of several.
- **Hawaiʻi ʻelepaio** and **ʻio** (Hawaiian hawk, endemic to Hawaiʻi Island, delisted in 2020).

**Maui**
- **Kiwikiu** (Maui parrotbill): critically endangered with a heavy hooked bill for tearing into bark and branches. A translocation attempt in 2019 failed due to avian malaria.
- **ʻĀkohekohe** (crested honeycreeper): endangered with a white forward-curling crest.
- **Maui ʻalauahio** (Maui creeper).
- The **poʻouli**, discovered in 1973, was last seen in 2004 and was formally declared extinct in 2023 alongside seven other Hawaiian birds.

**Kauaʻi**
- **ʻAkikiki:** a small gray creeper. By 2024 the wild population had collapsed to near zero, with remaining birds held in captive breeding facilities.
- **ʻAkekeʻe:** endangered, with slightly crossed bill tips.
- **ʻAnianiau:** a small yellow honeycreeper found only on Kauaʻi.
- **Puaiohi:** a small thrush numbering a few hundred.
- **Kauaʻi ʻelepaio.**
- The **Kauaʻi ʻōʻō**, whose last known individual was recorded singing in 1987, was among those declared extinct in 2023.

**Seabirds** also nest inland here. The **ʻaʻo** (Newell's shearwater) burrows on steep, fern-covered slopes on Kauaʻi. Fledglings are attracted and disoriented by artificial lights on their first flight to sea ("fallout"), and the state runs a rescue program each fall.

### Invertebrates

- **Picture-wing Drosophila:** about 100 or more species with patterned wings. Males perform elaborate courtship displays on leks. Many species breed in specific decaying plant tissues.
- **Carnivorous caterpillars** (*Eupithecia*): unique to Hawaii. They mimic twigs and snatch passing insects with grasping front legs, the only known ambush-predator caterpillars.
- **Amphibious caterpillars** (*Hyposmocoma*): some species can live both underwater in streams and on land.
- **Happy face spiders**, **Blackburn's blue butterfly** (*Udara blackburni*) and the **Kamehameha butterfly.**
- **Land snails** (Achatinellinae, Succineidae, Amastridae): once among the richest land snail faunas on Earth, now mostly extinct.

### Mammals

The **ʻōpeʻapeʻa** (Hawaiian hoary bat, *Lasiurus semotus*) is the only native land mammal. It roosts solitarily in foliage and was designated the state land mammal in 2015.

### Watershed Function

These forests act as giant sponges. Mosses and leaf litter capture rain and fog, slow runoff and recharge aquifers. The East Maui watershed supplies a large share of Maui's water. The Koʻolau Mountains recharge the aquifer that provides most of Honolulu's drinking water. Losing forest to pigs and invasive plants directly reduces fresh water supply.

### Cultural Significance

ʻŌhiʻa lehua is sacred to Pele and to Laka, the goddess of hula. In a well-known legend Pele turned the young man ʻŌhiʻa into a tree after he rejected her, and the other gods turned his beloved Lehua into the flower so they would never be parted. Picking lehua blossoms on the way to the volcano is said to bring rain (the lovers' tears). Feathers from ʻiʻiwi, ʻōʻō, mamo and other birds were collected by specialist bird catchers (kia manu) for capes (ʻahu ʻula), helmets (mahiole) and lei. The upper forest was the wao akua, entered only for specific purposes and with protocol.

### Threats

- **Avian disease and warming:** as temperatures rise, mosquitoes move upslope and shrink the disease-free refuge. Kauaʻi's birds have collapsed fastest because its mountains are the lowest.
- **Rapid ʻŌhiʻa Death.**
- **Feral pigs.**
- **Invasive plants:** kāhili ginger, strawberry guava, Himalayan raspberry, banana poka (*Passiflora tarminiana*), Australian tree fern and miconia.
- **Rats and cats** prey on nesting birds; rats eat seeds and snails.

### Conservation Highlights

- **Hakalau Forest National Wildlife Refuge** (Hawaiʻi Island): established in 1985, more than 32,000 acres. Former pastures have been reforested with koa, and native birds now use the restored forest.
- **Waikamoi Preserve** and **Hanawī Natural Area Reserve** (Maui).
- **Alakaʻi Wilderness Preserve** (Kauaʻi).
- **Kamakou Preserve** (Molokaʻi).
- **Puʻu Kukui Watershed Preserve** (West Maui).
- **Birds, Not Mosquitoes:** a multi-agency program that began releasing male mosquitoes carrying an incompatible strain of *Wolbachia* bacteria on Maui in late 2023 and on Kauaʻi in 2024. Females that mate with these males lay eggs that do not hatch, suppressing mosquito populations without pesticides.
- **Watershed partnerships** on every island fence and remove ungulates across hundreds of thousands of acres.

## 8. Montane Bogs

### Overview

Hawaiian bogs are among the strangest landscapes in the islands: open, waterlogged meadows atop rain-soaked plateaus where trees grow only inches tall. They are concentrated on older islands where flat summit plateaus and impermeable clay layers trap water.

### Formation

Bogs form where rainfall is extreme, the land surface is relatively flat and drainage is blocked by a dense layer of weathered clay or ash. Water saturates the soil year round. Oxygen is depleted, decomposition slows and **peat** accumulates, sometimes many feet deep. The soil becomes acidic and nutrient-poor.

### Distribution

- **Alakaʻi Swamp** (Kauaʻi): a plateau of roughly 10 square miles at about 4,000 to 4,500 feet. It is a mosaic of bogs, stunted forest and wet forest.
- **Waiʻaleʻale summit bogs** (Kauaʻi): at about 5,100 feet, one of the rainiest spots on Earth.
- **Puʻu Kukui** (West Maui): summit around 5,788 feet with rainfall comparable to Waiʻaleʻale.
- **Big Bog** (East Maui): at about 5,400 feet on Haleakalā's windward slope; the Rainfall Atlas of Hawaiʻi lists a mean of roughly 400 inches per year.
- **Pepeʻōpae Bog** (Molokaʻi): in the Kamakou Preserve, accessible by boardwalk.
- **Koʻolau summit** (Oʻahu): small bog patches.
- **Kohala Mountains** (Hawaiʻi Island): the only significant bogs on the youngest island, since the other volcanoes are too young and too porous.

### Plants

Bog plants are dwarfed by waterlogged, acidic, nutrient-poor soil and constant wind.

- **Dwarf ʻōhiʻa:** the same species that grows 100 feet tall elsewhere may flower here at a height of under a foot.
- **Sedges and grasses:** *Oreobolus furcatus* forms dense cushions; *Carex*, *Rhynchospora* and native bentgrasses are also common.
- **Greensword** (*Argyroxiphium grayanum*): a green-leaved relative of the silversword found in Maui bogs.
- **Sundew** (*Drosera anglica*): a carnivorous plant native to the Alakaʻi. It is otherwise found in cool northern regions, making the Kauaʻi population a striking long-distance disjunction.
- **Bog lobelias** (*Lobelia kauaensis*, *Lobelia villosa*).
- **Native violets** (*Viola*), **nohoanu** (native geraniums), *Dubautia* species and **ʻōhelo.**
- **Mosses:** including *Sphagnum*.

The flora mixes species with relatives in boreal, Andean and Austral regions, reflecting the random long-distance origins of Hawaiian plants.

### Fauna

- **Kauaʻi birds** in the Alakaʻi: puaiohi, ʻakekeʻe, ʻakikiki (historically), ʻanianiau and Kauaʻi ʻelepaio. The Alakaʻi was the final refuge of the Kauaʻi ʻōʻō, kāmaʻo (large Kauaʻi thrush) and Kauaʻi nukupuʻu, all now extinct.
- **Damselflies**, picture-wing flies and native snails.

### Cultural Significance

Waiʻaleʻale means roughly "rippling or overflowing water." A stone shrine at the summit was associated with Kāne, the god of fresh water and life. The bogs feed the headwaters of Kauaʻi's major rivers, including the Wailua, Waimea and Hanalei.

### Threats

- **Feral pigs:** bogs are extremely sensitive. Pig rooting destroys the thin plant cover, exposes peat and opens space for invasive plants. Damage can persist for decades.
- **Invasive plants:** Australian tree fern, kāhili ginger and strawberry guava along bog margins.
- **Climate change:** shifts in rainfall and cloud cover could alter bog hydrology.

### Conservation Highlights

Fencing and pig removal at Pepeʻōpae, Puʻu Kukui, Big Bog (within Haleakalā National Park's Kīpahulu backcountry and Hanawī) and parts of the Alakaʻi have allowed bog vegetation to recover. Puʻu Kukui is considered one of the most pristine bog systems in the state after decades of management.

## 9. Montane Dry Forest

### Overview

Montane dry forest occupies the leeward upper slopes of Hawaiʻi Island's great volcanoes and leeward Haleakalā. It is a cool, dry, open forest dominated by māmane and naio. It is the only home of the palila and the setting for one of the most important court cases in endangered species law.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** roughly 5,000 feet to treeline near 9,500 to 10,000 feet.
- **Rainfall:** about 15 to 40 inches per year with cloud and fog contributing moisture on some slopes.
- **Temperature:** cool days, frequent winter frost and large daily swings.
- **Locations:** the western and southern slopes of Mauna Kea (the palila's core range), Hualālai, upper North Kona, Mauna Loa's leeward flanks, Pōhakuloa (the saddle between Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa) and leeward Haleakalā (Kahikinui, Kanaio).

### Plants

- **Māmane** (*Sophora chrysophylla*): an endemic legume with bright yellow flowers and nitrogen-fixing roots. Its green seed pods are the main food of the palila. Its hard wood was used for tools and sled runners (papa hōlua).
- **Naio** (*Myoporum sandwicense*): called "false sandalwood" because its wood is faintly fragrant; was sometimes passed off as ʻiliahi during the sandalwood trade.
- **ʻIliahi** (sandalwood) species adapted to higher elevations.
- **ʻĀweoweo** (*Chenopodium oahuense*), **ʻaʻaliʻi**, **pūkiawe**, **ʻākia**, **ʻōhelo** and **naʻenaʻe.**
- **Koa and ʻōhiʻa** mix in at wetter edges in upper Kona.
- **Rare Pōhakuloa plants:** the Pōhakuloa Training Area holds endangered species such as *Haplostachys haplostachya* (a mint), *Silene hawaiiensis* and *Tetramolopium arenarium*.

### Fauna

- **Palila** (*Loxioides bailleui*): a finch-billed honeycreeper with a golden-yellow head. It depends almost entirely on māmane seeds, which are toxic to most animals. Its population has declined to the low thousands or fewer, concentrated on Mauna Kea's western slope.
- **Hawaiʻi ʻamakihi** and **Hawaiʻi ʻelepaio.**
- **Pueo** (Hawaiian short-eared owl): hunts by day over open country. Some families regard pueo as ʻaumakua.
- **ʻIo** (Hawaiian hawk).
- **Nēnē** in open areas.
- **Introduced game birds:** chukar, Erckel's francolin, kalij pheasant and wild turkey.
- **Native insects:** Hawaiian yellow-faced bees, endemic moths and seed-feeding caterpillars.

### Palila v. Hawaii Department of Land and Natural Resources

Feral sheep and goats (and later introduced mouflon sheep, released on Mauna Kea in 1962 for sport hunting) browsed māmane seedlings and saplings, preventing forest regeneration. In 1978 environmental groups sued the state with the palila itself named as plaintiff. In 1979 a federal court ruled that maintaining feral ungulates in critical habitat constituted "harm" under the Endangered Species Act and ordered their removal. The decision was upheld on appeal in 1981. It established that habitat degradation can count as harming a listed species, a precedent that shaped endangered species law nationally.

### Threats

- **Ungulates:** feral sheep, goats and mouflon hybrids persist despite decades of removal efforts.
- **Fire:** invasive fountain grass carries fire into māmane forest. The 2021 Mana Road Fire burned roughly 40,000 acres on Mauna Kea's northern slopes.
- **Drought:** prolonged droughts reduce māmane seed crops and palila breeding.
- **Invasive grasses** and weeds such as fireweed and mullein.
- **Predators:** feral cats and rats prey on nesting palila.

### Conservation Highlights

- **Mauna Kea Forest Restoration Project:** a fence of more than 50 miles around the mountain's palila habitat and large-scale planting of māmane and naio.
- **Puʻu Waʻawaʻa Forest Reserve** and the **Kaʻohe Game Management Area.**
- **Palila translocation:** a population was established on Mauna Kea's northern slope in the 2000s.

## 10. Subalpine Shrubland and Grassland

### Overview

Above the trade wind inversion the climate changes abruptly. Clouds stop and dry, sunny conditions take over, with intense radiation and frequent frost. Vegetation becomes low, open shrubland and grassland on cinder and lava. This zone exists only on Hawaiʻi Island and Maui.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** roughly 6,500 to 10,000 feet (the inversion varies, so the lower boundary shifts).
- **Rainfall:** about 15 to 40 inches per year, with drought common.
- **Temperature:** daytime highs can be warm in strong sun; nights are frequently below freezing.
- **Ultraviolet radiation:** among the highest measured anywhere, due to altitude, latitude and clear skies.
- **Locations:** Haleakalā's summit depression (often called the crater, though it was carved by erosion rather than collapse), upper Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa and Hualālai (summit 8,271 feet).

### Plants

- **Pūkiawe** (*Leptecophylla tameiameiae*): a small-leaved shrub with white, pink or red berries. Its smoke was used in a ritual to release a chief from kapu so he could mingle with commoners.
- **ʻŌhelo** (*Vaccinium reticulatum*): low shrub with red or yellow berries. ʻŌhelo is sacred to Pele, and traditionally the first berries were offered to her before any were eaten.
- **Naʻenaʻe** (*Dubautia menziesii* and others): fragrant shrubs in the silversword alliance.
- **Hinahina** (*Geranium cuneatum*): silver-leaved native geranium.
- **Native grasses:** *Deschampsia nubigena* (hairgrass), *Trisetum glomeratum* and *Agrostis sandwicensis*.
- **ʻAʻaliʻi**, **ʻākia**, **māmane** at the lower boundary and several rare *Tetramolopium* daisies.
- **ʻĀhinahina** (silverswords): see below.

### The Silverswords

The **Haleakalā silversword** (*Argyroxiphium sandwicense* subsp. *macrocephalum*) grows only on the cinder slopes of Haleakalā, roughly 7,000 to 10,000 feet. Its rosette of sword-shaped leaves is covered in fine silver hairs that reflect intense sunlight, reduce water loss and protect against frost. The leaves curve inward like a parabolic dish. A plant can live for decades (often cited as 20 to 90 years) as a rosette, then sends up a single flowering stalk up to about 6 feet tall bearing hundreds of maroon flower heads. After flowering, it dies.

By the 1920s the silversword had been nearly wiped out by goats and by visitors who uprooted plants as souvenirs or rolled them down slopes. Protection and ungulate removal brought numbers back to tens of thousands. Recent decades have seen renewed decline linked to warmer, drier conditions.

Relatives:
- **Mauna Kea silversword** (*A. sandwicense* subsp. *sandwicense*): reduced to a few wild individuals and rebuilt through outplanting.
- **Kaʻū silversword** (*A. kauense*): grows on Mauna Loa at lower, wetter elevations.

The silversword alliance as a whole includes trees, shrubs, cushion plants, vines and rosettes, all descended from a small California tarweed. It is one of the most cited examples of adaptive radiation in plants.

### Fauna

- **Nēnē** (Hawaiian goose, *Branta sandvicensis*): the state bird, descended from Canada geese that arrived perhaps half a million years ago. It has reduced webbing on its feet for walking on lava. By the 1950s only about 30 remained. Captive breeding (including birds raised at Slimbridge in England) and releases restored the population to more than 3,000. It was downlisted from endangered to threatened in 2019.
- **ʻUaʻu** (Hawaiian petrel, *Pterodroma sandwichensis*): endangered seabird that nests in burrows high on Haleakalā, roughly 7,500 to 10,000 feet. Adults forage far out at sea and return to burrows only at night. Haleakalā's colony is protected by fencing and predator control.
- **Pueo**, chukar and introduced pheasants.
- **Native insects:** yellow-faced bees (Haleakalā hosts several species that pollinate silverswords), endemic moths and native flies.

### Cultural Significance

**Haleakalā** means "house of the sun." In legend, the demigod Māui climbed the mountain and lassoed the sun to slow its passage so his mother Hina could dry her kapa. **Mauna Kea** (also written Maunakea) is considered the piko (navel) of Hawaiʻi Island and the realm of Wākea, the sky father, and of Poliʻahu, the snow goddess.

### Threats

- **Climate change:** warming and drying are linked to silversword decline; a higher or more frequent inversion could shift the whole zone.
- **Argentine ants** at Haleakalā prey on native insects, including silversword pollinators.
- **Invasive plants:** mullein (*Verbascum thapsus*) on Mauna Kea, pines escaping from plantations on Haleakalā, telegraph weed and fireweed.
- **Ungulates and predators:** goats, pigs, mongooses, cats and rats.

### Conservation Highlights

Haleakalā National Park completed a boundary fence in the 1980s and removed goats and pigs from its summit area, one of the earliest large ungulate exclusions in Hawaii. Mauna Kea's silversword recovery relies on outplanting into fenced areas.

## 11. Alpine Desert and the Aeolian Zone

### Overview

The summits of Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa form a true alpine desert: cinder, rock, intense sun, freezing nights and almost no vascular plants. Life here depends largely on food blown up from below. Biologists call this an **aeolian ecosystem**, meaning one fueled by wind-delivered material.

### Distribution and Climate

- **Elevation:** above about 10,000 feet to the summits. Mauna Kea reaches 13,803 feet (4,207 m); Mauna Loa reaches 13,679 feet (4,169 m). Haleakalā's summit at 10,023 feet reaches the margins of this zone.
- **Precipitation:** about 10 to 20 inches per year, much of it as winter snow.
- **Temperature:** below freezing most nights year round; daytime often 30s to 50s °F.
- **Atmosphere:** air pressure at the summit of Mauna Kea is about 60 percent of sea level.
- **Wind:** frequently strong.

### Geologic Features

- **Glaciation:** Mauna Kea held ice caps during the Pleistocene. Moraines, glacial striations and meltwater channels are still visible. It is one of very few tropical places with clear glacial landforms.
- **Permafrost:** small bodies of permanently frozen ground exist inside cinder cones near the summit, including Puʻu Wēkiu.
- **Lake Waiau:** at about 13,020 feet, one of the highest lakes in the United States. It is fed by snowmelt and perched on impermeable glacial and volcanic deposits. During the drought of 2010 to 2014 it shrank to a small fraction of its usual size before refilling.
- **Keanakākoʻi:** the largest known basalt adze quarry in Polynesia, located near 12,000 feet. Lava that erupted under glacial ice cooled into exceptionally dense, fine-grained rock ideal for stone tools. Hawaiians lived seasonally at the quarry, leaving shrines, shelters and workshops.

### Plants and Lichens

Vascular plants are nearly absent at the summits. Lichens dominate, with many species crusting rocks. Mosses (such as *Grimmia*) hide in sheltered crevices. A few ferns (including *Asplenium* and *Polystichum* species) and grasses persist in pockets near the lower edge of the zone. Outplanted Mauna Kea silverswords reach into the lower alpine.

### Fauna

- **Wēkiu bug** (*Nysius wekiuicola*): a small flightless-adapted seed bug discovered around 1980 in the cinders of Mauna Kea's summit cones. Its relatives eat seeds, but the wēkiu bug became a scavenger and predator, feeding on insects that are carried up by wind and die or become stunned in the cold. It stays active at near-freezing temperatures thanks to antifreeze compounds in its body fluids and shelters in loose cinder. It lives only near the summit of Mauna Kea; a related species occurs on Mauna Loa.
- **Wolf spiders** (*Lycosa*) hunt among the rocks.
- **Linyphiid spiders**, **mites**, **springtails**, **centipedes** and a few **moths** whose caterpillars feed on lichens.

These animals form a food web almost entirely supported by wind-blown insects, pollen and organic debris.

### Human Use

- **Astronomy:** Maunakea is one of the world's premier astronomical sites because of its dry, stable, dark skies above the inversion. More than a dozen observatories stand near the summit. A master plan calls for decommissioning some older facilities. Proposed construction of the Thirty Meter Telescope prompted extended protests in 2019. In 2022 the state created the Maunakea Stewardship and Oversight Authority, which includes Native Hawaiian representation, to govern the summit lands.
- **Mauna Loa Observatory:** at about 11,141 feet, it has measured atmospheric carbon dioxide since 1958, producing the Keeling Curve. The 2022 Mauna Loa eruption cut its road and power line, and measurements were temporarily moved to Maunakea.
- **Cultural sites:** shrines (ahu), burials and the quarry make the summit a highly sacred and protected landscape.

### Threats

- **Climate change:** reduced snowfall, warming and potential changes in wind patterns that deliver food.
- **Physical disturbance** of cinder cones, which are the wēkiu bug's only habitat.
- **Invasive arthropods** carried up in vehicles and construction materials.
- **Foot traffic** and off-trail activity.

## 12. New Lava Flows

### Overview

Hawaiʻi Island's active volcanoes continuously create new land. Fresh lava is completely sterile. Watching life colonize it is one of the best opportunities anywhere to observe **primary succession**, the process by which ecosystems assemble from nothing.

### Lava Types

- **Pāhoehoe:** smooth, ropy or billowy lava. Its surface cracks and fractures as it cools, creating crevices.
- **ʻAʻā:** rough, clinkery lava made of jagged fragments. Hawaiian words for these two types are now standard terms in geology worldwide.
- **Cinder and tephra:** fragments ejected from vents.

Moisture and wind-blown material collect in cracks and between ʻaʻā clinkers, so colonization begins there.

### Recent Activity

- **Puʻu ʻŌʻō eruption** (1983 to 2018): Kīlauea's longest eruption in recorded history, covering large areas of the east rift zone and adding new land along the coast.
- **2018 lower East Rift Zone eruption:** covered about 13.7 square miles, destroyed more than 700 structures, buried Kapoho Bay and added roughly 875 acres of new land. Kīlauea's summit collapsed dramatically during the same period.
- **2022 Mauna Loa eruption:** Mauna Loa's first eruption since 1984, lasting about two weeks.
- **Kīlauea summit eruptions:** multiple episodes since 2020, including a long series of episodic summit lava fountaining events beginning in December 2024.

### The Sequence of Colonization

The speed of succession depends overwhelmingly on rainfall. On wet windward Kīlauea, ʻōhiʻa seedlings can appear within a few years and young forest within decades. In dry areas colonization can take centuries.

1. **Microbes:** cyanobacteria and other microbes colonize within months, fixing nitrogen.
2. **Lichens:** *Stereocaulon vulcani* forms silvery-white crusts and is one of the first visible colonists. It fixes nitrogen and begins chemically weathering rock.
3. **Mosses:** such as *Campylopus* species, which trap moisture and dust.
4. **Ferns:** **kupukupu** (*Nephrolepis*) and **ʻamaʻu** (*Sadleria cyatheoides*), whose new fronds emerge bright red. ʻAmaʻu is often the most visible early plant on recent pāhoehoe.
5. **Shrubs and trees:** **ʻōhiʻa lehua** arrives early because its tiny wind-blown seeds travel far. It is soon joined by **ʻōhelo**, **pūkiawe**, **ʻaʻaliʻi** and **naʻenaʻe**.
6. **Forest:** over centuries a closed ʻōhiʻa forest with tree ferns develops on wet sites.

### Fauna of Barren Lava

- **Lava cricket** (*Caconemobius fori*): an endemic cricket that colonizes new lava flows within months, often before any plant. It lives in cracks and emerges at night to scavenge wind-blown organic debris. When vegetation arrives the lava cricket disappears from that flow.
- **Wolf spiders** and **other predators** that feed on wind-blown insects.
- These are another expression of the aeolian ecosystem described in the alpine section.

### Kīpuka

A **kīpuka** is a patch of older land surrounded by younger lava, an island of vegetation in a sea of rock. Kīpuka serve as natural laboratories for studying isolation, dispersal and genetics. Hampton Carson's research on Drosophila populations in kīpuka helped shape modern understanding of how new species form. Kīpuka Puaulu ("Bird Park") in Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park is a well-known example with rich soil and old koa.

### The Kaʻū Desert

Southwest of Kīlauea's summit lies the **Kaʻū Desert**. It is not a desert because of low rainfall alone; it sits in a rain shadow and is also bathed in acidic precipitation and volcanic gas (vog) from the summit. Its surface includes ash from the 1790 explosive eruption. Footprints preserved in that ash have been associated with people caught in the eruption, traditionally linked to warriors of Keōua, Kamehameha's rival. Vegetation remains extremely sparse.

### Volcanic Gases

Sulfur dioxide emitted by Kīlauea can reach thousands of tonnes per day during high activity. Downwind of the vents, vog damages vegetation, acidifies rain and creates zones where only the hardiest lichens and ferns survive.

### Cultural Significance

Pele, goddess of volcanoes, is said to live in Halemaʻumaʻu crater at Kīlauea's summit. New lava is understood as Pele creating land. Lava flows are not seen as purely destructive; cultural practitioners often offer chants and ʻōhelo or other gifts at eruptions. Petroglyph fields such as Puʻuloa on Kīlauea's south flank are carved into older pāhoehoe.

## 13. Lava Tubes and Caves

### Overview

Hawaii's caves contain an entire hidden biome of animals adapted to permanent darkness. Until 1971 biologists assumed that young volcanic islands were too new to have evolved cave-adapted species. Entomologist Frank Howarth disproved this by discovering blind cave animals in Hawaiian lava tubes, and later work showed some had evolved on flows only thousands of years old.

### Formation

Lava tubes form when the surface of a pāhoehoe flow cools and crusts over while molten lava continues flowing beneath. When the eruption ends, the lava drains out and leaves a hollow tube. Tubes can branch, stack in multiple levels and stretch for miles.

- **Kazumura Cave** (Hawaiʻi Island): about 40 miles (65 km) long, the longest known lava tube in the world, formed by a flow from Kīlauea roughly 500 to 600 years ago.
- **Nāhuku** (Thurston Lava Tube) in Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park: the most visited.
- **Limestone caves:** less common, found on older islands. **Makauwahi Cave** on Kauaʻi is the largest limestone cave in Hawaii.

### Cave Zones

1. **Entrance zone:** light and surface vegetation; surface animals mix with cave dwellers.
2. **Twilight zone:** dim light; fewer plants.
3. **Dark zone:** total darkness, constant temperature and humidity near 100 percent. True cave-adapted species (troglobites) live here.
4. **Stagnant air zone:** deep passages with elevated carbon dioxide, preferred by some troglobites because humidity is especially stable.

### Food Web

Darkness means no photosynthesis. Energy enters through:

- **Tree roots:** ʻōhiʻa roots penetrate cave ceilings and hang in curtains. Many cave animals feed directly on these roots.
- **Organic matter** washed or blown in.
- **Animals** that wander in and die.

Because surface forest provides the roots, cutting forest above a cave starves the cave ecosystem below.

### Fauna

- **Cave planthopper** (*Oliarus polyphemus*): eyeless, pale and wingless; sucks sap from ʻōhiʻa roots. Different cave systems hold distinct populations.
- **Cave crickets** (*Caconemobius* species): close relatives of the lava cricket, adapted to darkness.
- **Kauaʻi cave wolf spider** (*Adelocosa anops*): known locally as the "no-eyed big-eyed wolf spider" because its relatives have huge eyes while it has none. Females carry egg sacs and young on their backs. Found only in a few caves in the Kōloa and Poʻipū area. Listed as endangered in 2000.
- **Kauaʻi cave amphipod** (*Spelaeorchestia koloana*): a blind terrestrial amphipod and the spider's main prey. Also endangered.
- **Hawaiʻi Island cave wolf spider** (*Lycosa howarthi*): with reduced eyes.
- **Cave moths** whose caterpillars feed on roots.
- **Thread-legged bugs** (*Nesidiolestes*), predatory and highly modified.
- **Cave water treaders** (*Cavaticovelia aaa*): related to water striders but living on wet cave rock.
- **Cave earwigs** (*Anisolabis howarthi*), **millipedes**, **pseudoscorpions**, **springtails** and **cave beetles.**

Typical troglobite traits include loss of eyes and pigment, reduced wings, long legs and antennae and slow metabolism.

### Paleontological Value

Caves and sinkholes preserve fossils. **Makauwahi Cave**, studied by David and Lida Burney, holds roughly 10,000 years of sediment, pollen and bones. It recorded the lowland forest that existed before human arrival and the bones of extinct species such as flightless ducks, a long-legged owl (*Grallistrix*), rails and many land snails. Lava tubes on Maui and Hawaiʻi Island have produced similar fossil records.

### Human History

Hawaiians used caves for shelter, refuge, water collection and especially burial (ana kūpapaʻu). Many caves contain iwi kūpuna (ancestral remains) and are among the most sacred and legally protected places in the islands. Water dripping in some tubes was captured in containers. Petroglyphs and cultural deposits occur in many caves.

### Threats

- **Development** over caves, including sealing entrances and paving.
- **Surface forest loss**, cutting off root food sources.
- **Pollution:** sewage, cesspools, pesticides and trash.
- **Invasive species:** cockroaches, rats, nonnative spiders and invasive predatory arthropods.
- **Human visitation:** trampling, vandalism and looting of cultural sites.

Locations of sensitive caves are typically not publicized.

## 14. Streams and Wetlands

### Overview

Hawaiian freshwater ecosystems are short, steep and dynamic. Native stream animals are few in species but remarkable in their life histories: almost all spend part of their lives in the ocean. Wetlands, from coastal marshes to taro paddies, support a group of endemic waterbirds.

### Streams

**Physical character.** The 1990 Hawaii Stream Assessment identified 376 perennial streams. Most run steeply from mountain to sea over waterfalls. Flow is "flashy": heavy rain produces floods within minutes, then levels drop quickly. Older islands have the longest and most deeply incised streams (Kauaʻi's Wailua and Waimea rivers, Molokaʻi's Pelekunu and Wailau). Young Hawaiʻi Island has few permanent streams outside the windward slopes because porous lava absorbs rainfall.

**Amphidromy.** Nearly all native stream animals are amphidromous. Adults live and spawn in streams. Newly hatched larvae drift downstream to the ocean, spend weeks to months at sea as plankton, then return to a stream (not necessarily the one they came from) as postlarvae and migrate upstream. This explains how stream animals colonized the islands and why every stream depends on a connection to the sea.

**Native fish (ʻoʻopu).** Five native gobioid fishes:

- **ʻOʻopu ʻalamoʻo** (*Lentipes concolor*): a champion climber found far upstream above waterfalls hundreds of feet tall. It climbs using a suction disk formed by fused pelvic fins plus its mouth, inching up wet rock faces.
- **ʻOʻopu nōpili** (*Sicyopterus stimpsoni*): also an excellent climber; grazes algae from rocks.
- **ʻOʻopu nākea** (*Awaous stamineus*): the largest native stream fish, up to about a foot. It spawns in mass downstream migrations during fall floods.
- **ʻOʻopu naniha** (*Stenogobius hawaiiensis*): lives in lower reaches and estuaries; cannot climb.
- **ʻOʻopu akupa** (*Eleotris sandwicensis*): a predatory sleeper in lower reaches.

**Other native stream animals:**

- **Āholehole** (*Kuhlia xenura*): an endemic flagtail of estuaries and lower streams.
- **ʻŌpae kalaʻole** (*Atyoida bisulcata*): an endemic shrimp that climbs to headwaters and filters food with fan-like claws.
- **ʻŌpae ʻoehaʻa** (*Macrobrachium grandimanus*): an endemic prawn in lower reaches.
- **Hīhīwai** (*Neritina granosa*): an endemic snail that climbs waterfalls and was a traditional food.
- **Hapawai** (*Neritina vespertina*): a brackish-water snail.
- **Megalagrion damselflies:** a radiation of about two dozen endemic species. Some breed in streams, some in seeps, some in plant leaf axils and one in moist leaf litter. Many are endangered.
- **Pinao** (*Anax strenuus*): the Hawaiian giant darner, the largest dragonfly in the United States.

**Water use and law.** Hawaiians built ʻauwai (irrigation channels) to divert water into loʻi kalo (taro paddies) and then return it to the stream, sustaining both. Plantations built massive ditch systems that captured entire streams and carried water across watersheds; East Maui's system alone moved water from dozens of streams. In 2000 the Hawaiʻi Supreme Court's Waiāhole Ditch decision affirmed that water resources are held in public trust, with protection of stream life and traditional practices among the trust purposes. Subsequent decisions restored flow to streams in Nā Wai ʻEhā (West Maui) and East Maui, and native fish and snails returned to restored reaches.

**Threats to streams:**

- Diversions that leave streams dry and break the connection to the sea.
- Concrete channelization in urban areas (Ala Wai Canal tributaries on Oʻahu).
- **Introduced species:** tilapia, swordtails, guppies, mosquitofish (introduced in 1905 for mosquito control), smallmouth bass, Tahitian prawns, crayfish and bullfrogs. They prey on and compete with natives and spread parasites.
- Sedimentation, pollution and invasive plants such as California grass.

**Best intact streams:** Pelekunu and Waikolu (Molokaʻi), Hanalei and Wainiha (Kauaʻi), Waipiʻo and Waimanu (Hawaiʻi Island), ʻĪao and parts of East Maui.

### Wetlands

**Types:**

- **Coastal marshes and ponds:** Kawainui Marsh on Oʻahu (the largest remaining emergent wetland in the state; part of a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance designated in 2005), Kanahā Pond and Keālia Pond on Maui, ʻAimakapā fishpond in Kaloko-Honokōhau National Historical Park.
- **Loʻi kalo:** taro paddies function as artificial wetlands and are prime habitat for endangered waterbirds. Hanalei National Wildlife Refuge on Kauaʻi is managed partly through active taro farming.
- **Loko iʻa:** fishponds create brackish wetland habitat.
- **Montane pools and lakes:** Lake Waiau and small upland ponds.
- **Hypersaline lakes:** in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands (Laysan).

**Endemic waterbirds:**

- **Aeʻo** (Hawaiian stilt, *Himantopus mexicanus knudseni*): long pink legs and black-and-white plumage; feeds in shallow mudflats.
- **ʻAlae keʻokeʻo** (Hawaiian coot, *Fulica alai*): white frontal shield. In legend it is associated with fire, since it was said to have brought fire to people.
- **ʻAlae ʻula** (Hawaiian gallinule): with a red frontal shield. In legend it brought fire to Māui, scorching its forehead red. Now found mainly on Kauaʻi and Oʻahu.
- **Koloa maoli** (Hawaiian duck, *Anas wyvilliana*): threatened mainly by hybridization with feral mallards, which has left Kauaʻi as the stronghold for genetically pure birds.
- **Nēnē** frequently use lowland wetlands and taro fields, especially on Kauaʻi.

**Indigenous and migratory birds:**

- **ʻAukuʻu** (black-crowned night heron).
- **Kōlea** (Pacific golden plover), **ʻūlili** (wandering tattler), **ʻakekeke** (ruddy turnstone) and **hunakai** (sanderling).
- **Koloa mapu** (northern pintail) and **koloa moha** (northern shoveler).

**Threats to wetlands:**

- Filling and development; Hawaii lost a large share of its lowland wetlands to urbanization and agriculture.
- **Red mangrove**, introduced to Molokaʻi in 1902, now clogs coastal wetlands and fishpond walls on several islands.
- Invasive plants: California grass, water hyacinth and pickleweed.
- Predators: mongooses, cats, dogs, rats and bullfrogs prey on eggs and chicks.
- **Avian botulism** outbreaks in shallow, warm water.
- Sea level rise and saltwater intrusion.

**Conservation highlights:** Hanalei, Huleʻia, Kealia Pond, James Campbell and Pearl Harbor National Wildlife Refuges; community restoration at Kawainui Marsh and Heʻeia; predator control and water level management for stilt and coot nesting.

## Bonus: The Northwestern Hawaiian Islands on Land

The small islands and atolls northwest of Kauaʻi (Nihoa, Mokumanamana, Lalo, Laysan, Lisianski, Pearl and Hermes, Midway and Kure) host terrestrial ecosystems found nowhere else.

- **Nihoa:** a steep remnant volcano with its own endemic **loulu palm** (*Pritchardia remota*), the **Nihoa finch** and the **Nihoa millerbird**. Nihoa and Mokumanamana also hold extensive Hawaiian archaeological sites, including many stone shrines on Mokumanamana.
- **Laysan:** a low coral island ringed by a **hypersaline lake** where brine flies support the endemic **Laysan duck** and **Laysan finch**. Rabbits introduced in 1903 stripped the island of vegetation, causing the extinction of the Laysan millerbird, Laysan rail and Laysan ʻapapane. After rabbits were removed in 1923 vegetation recovered. Nihoa millerbirds were translocated to Laysan in 2011 and 2012 to fill the empty niche.
- **Midway Atoll:** holds the largest Laysan albatross colony in the world and large numbers of black-footed albatross. It is home to **Wisdom**, a Laysan albatross banded in 1956 and the oldest known wild bird, who was still returning to nest and laying eggs into her 70s. Laysan ducks were translocated to Midway in 2004 and 2005 and to Kure in 2014.
- **Seabird populations:** the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands support some 14 million seabirds of about 22 species.

Threats include sea level rise (which could submerge low islands), storm overwash, marine debris and plastic ingestion by albatross chicks, invasive ants and introduced house mice that have been documented attacking nesting albatross on Midway.

|---|---|---|
| Feral pigs | Wet forests, bogs, streams | Spread weeds, create mosquito habitat, destroy understory |
| Goats, sheep, deer, cattle | Dry forests, subalpine, mesic forests | Prevent regeneration; caused the palila lawsuit |
| Rats | All terrestrial | Eat seeds, snails, eggs and chicks |
| Mongooses and cats | Coastal, wetlands, subalpine | Devastate ground-nesting birds |
| Mosquitoes and avian disease | All forests below about 5,000 ft | Primary cause of honeycreeper declines |
| Invasive plants | All terrestrial | Miconia, strawberry guava, fountain grass, kāhili ginger and many more |
| Fire and the grass-fire cycle | Dry forest, montane dry, subalpine | Native systems lack fire adaptation |
| Rapid ʻŌhiʻa Death | All ʻōhiʻa-dominated systems | Threatens the keystone tree statewide |
| Climate change | All, especially high-elevation and reefs | Upslope mosquito spread, drought, bleaching, sea level rise |
| Development | Coastal, lowland, caves, anchialine pools | Permanent habitat loss |
| Water diversion | Streams, wetlands | Breaks amphidromous life cycles |

Biosecurity is critical because many of the worst potential invaders are not yet established. The brown tree snake, which wiped out most native forest birds on Guam, is the most feared. Hawaii's agricultural inspection system and dog teams at airports and harbors are designed to stop it.
