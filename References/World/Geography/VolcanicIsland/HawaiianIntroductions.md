## 7. Polynesian Voyaging Canoes (about 1000 to 1200 AD)

Polynesians from the Marquesas and Society Islands sailed double-hulled canoes about 2,500 miles. They carried a deliberate kit of animals and plants needed to start a colony.

### Animals carried on purpose

- **Pig (pua'a):** a small Polynesian breed. Raised for food, offerings and ceremony. Some escaped and went feral
- **Dog ('īlio):** the Hawaiian poi dog. Small, raised mostly for food and fed on poi. The original breed is extinct after mixing with Western dogs
- **Chicken (moa):** descended from red junglefowl. Used for food, feathers and cockfighting. Feral descendants mixed with modern chickens and are now everywhere on Kaua'i

### Stowaways

- **Pacific rat ('iole):** smaller than European rats. Ate seeds, seedlings, eggs and nesting birds. Possibly the single largest cause of pre-contact extinctions, especially of the seed-producing loulu palm forests on O'ahu's plains
- **Geckos:** mourning gecko, stump-toed gecko, Indo-Pacific tree gecko
- **Skinks:** moth skink, snake-eyed skink, azure-tailed skink (now likely gone from the main islands)
- **Small land snails**
- **Insects and weeds:** carried in soil, seeds and plant material

### Canoe plants

Roughly two dozen species. These supplied food, fiber, medicine, tools, dyes and building material.

**Staple starches**
- **Kalo (taro):** the most important plant in Hawaiian culture. Hawaiians developed over 300 varieties. Grown in flooded lo'i terraces. Pounded into poi. In legend, the elder brother of the first Hawaiian
- **'Uala (sweet potato):** originally from South America. Its presence proves Polynesians reached the Americas before Columbus. Grown in dry areas where kalo could not grow
- **'Ulu (breadfruit):** a tree producing large starchy fruit. One tree can feed a family
- **Uhi (yam):** large tubers. Stored well on long voyages
- **Hoi (bitter yam):** poisonous raw. A famine food after careful preparation
- **Pia (Polynesian arrowroot):** tubers made into a starch for puddings such as haupia
- **'Ape:** a giant taro relative. Famine food

**Fruits and nuts**
- **Niu (coconut):** food, drinking water, oil, cordage from husk fiber (sennit), leaves for thatch and weaving, shells for containers. Coconuts can float but Hawaii's coconut groves came by canoe
- **Mai'a (banana):** dozens of varieties. Some were kapu (forbidden) to women
- **'Ōhi'a 'ai (mountain apple):** red fruit. Wood used for carving
- **Kukui (candlenut):** oily nuts burned for light, roasted into a relish ('inamona), used for dye, varnish and medicine. State tree

**Sweeteners, drinks and medicine**
- **Kō (sugarcane):** chewed for sweetness, used in medicine and planted as windbreaks
- **'Awa (kava):** roots made into a ceremonial sedative drink
- **Noni:** pungent fruit used for medicine. Bark and roots used for dye
- **'Ōlena (turmeric):** medicine and yellow dye
- **'Awapuhi kuahiwi (shampoo ginger):** sap used as shampoo

**Fiber, tools and containers**
- **Wauke (paper mulberry):** bark pounded into kapa cloth for clothing and bedding
- **Kī (ti):** leaves for wrapping food, rain capes, sandals, thatch and spiritual protection. Roots baked as a sweet treat or later distilled into okolehao liquor
- **'Ohe (bamboo):** knives, nose flutes, stamps for decorating kapa
- **Ipu (bottle gourd):** containers, bowls and percussion instruments for hula

**Timber trees**
- **Kamani:** hard wood for bowls and canoe parts
- **Kou:** prized wood for calabashes and bowls
- **Milo:** wood for bowls and calabashes

**Disputed or possibly native**
- Hau (beach hibiscus)
- Hala (pandanus): pollen records show it was here before people

### Impact of Polynesian settlement

- Roughly half of Hawaii's native bird species disappeared before Captain Cook arrived. This includes every flightless bird
- Lowland forests were cleared by fire for farming. Loulu palm forests on O'ahu's lowland plains vanished
- Hawaiians built a sophisticated land management system (ahupua'a) running from mountain to sea, with fishponds (loko i'a) and kapu rules restricting overharvest
- Upper forests stayed mostly intact

## 8. Western Ships (1778 Onward)

### Livestock and game mammals

- **Goats (1778, Captain Cook):** left on Ni'ihau. Spread everywhere. Ate native plants down to bare rock on steep slopes and wiped out much of the dry forest
- **European pigs (1778 onward):** crossbred with Polynesian pigs and became much larger. Root up forest floors, create mud wallows where mosquitoes breed, spread invasive seeds and destroy bogs
- **Cattle (1793, George Vancouver):** King Kamehameha placed a kapu protecting them. Herds grew wild for decades and stripped forests. Led to Parker Ranch and the paniolo (Hawaiian cowboy) tradition
- **Sheep (1790s):** feral herds on Mauna Kea ate silverswords and māmane trees, threatening the palila
- **Horses (1803)**
- **Donkeys:** feral "Kona nightingales" once roamed Kona coffee country
- **Axis deer (1867):** a gift to King Kamehameha V. Spread to Moloka'i, Lāna'i and Maui. Populations exploded during recent droughts
- **Black-tailed deer (1961):** Kaua'i
- **Mouflon sheep (1950s to 1960s):** Lāna'i and the Big Island
- **Pronghorn:** introduced to Lāna'i and died out

### Predators and pests

- **Black rat and Norway rat (1800s):** the black rat climbs trees and raids bird nests. Also eats snails and seeds
- **House mouse:** eats seeds and insects. Kills nesting albatross at Midway by eating them alive on the nest
- **Feral cats:** kill seabirds, forest birds and nēnē. Spread toxoplasmosis, which kills monk seals and native birds
- **Small Indian mongoose (1883):** brought from Jamaica to sugar plantations in Hilo to kill rats. Rats are nocturnal and mongooses hunt by day, so it failed at rat control. Instead it eats ground-nesting birds, eggs and turtle hatchlings. Absent from Kaua'i
- **Brush-tailed rock wallaby (1916):** escaped from a private zoo on O'ahu. A small population survives in Kalihi Valley

### Birds

**Game birds**
- Ring-necked pheasant
- Kalij pheasant
- Wild turkey
- California quail, Japanese quail
- Chukar
- Erckel's francolin, gray francolin, black francolin
- Domestic chickens (mixed with Polynesian moa)

**Pest control releases**
- Common myna (1865): brought from India to eat armyworm caterpillars in sugarcane
- Barn owl (late 1950s): brought to hunt rats in cane fields. Also kills seabirds
- Cattle egret (1959): brought to eat flies around cattle. Also eats native waterbird chicks

**Songbirds and other introductions**
- Japanese white-eye (1929): now the most common land bird in Hawaii. Competes with native birds and spreads disease
- House sparrow (1871)
- House finch
- Zebra dove, spotted dove, rock pigeon
- Red-vented bulbul, red-whiskered bulbul
- Northern cardinal
- Red-crested cardinal
- Yellow-billed cardinal
- Java sparrow
- Nutmeg mannikin, chestnut munia
- African silverbill
- Yellow-fronted canary
- Saffron finch
- Japanese bush warbler
- White-rumped shama
- Red-billed leiothrix
- Melodious laughingthrush
- Rose-ringed parakeet: a growing problem on Kaua'i crops
- Mallard: hybridizes with koloa

### Reptiles and amphibians

**Frogs and toads**
- Coquí frog (1980s): arrived in nursery plants from Puerto Rico. Calls reach 90 decibels and populations hit densities several times higher than in Puerto Rico because nothing eats them
- Greenhouse frog
- Cane toad (1932): released to eat sugarcane pests. Toxic to dogs
- American bullfrog
- Green and black poison dart frog (1932): released on O'ahu to eat mosquitoes

**Lizards**
- Jackson's chameleon (1970s): released from a pet shop in Kāne'ohe. Eats native insects and snails
- Green iguana
- Brown anole, green anole
- Gold dust day gecko
- Common house gecko: displaced the Polynesian geckos
- Tokay gecko

**Snakes and turtles**
- Brahminy blind snake (1930): tiny, worm-like and harmless. The only established snake
- Brown tree snake: has been caught arriving at airports several times. Establishment would devastate the remaining birds, as it did on Guam
- Red-eared slider
- Chinese softshell turtle

### Insects and other invertebrates

**Disease carriers and stingers**
- Southern house mosquito (1826): arrived at Lahaina, Maui in a ship's water casks. Spread avian malaria and avian pox, the biggest reason native forest birds now survive only in high mountains
- Asian tiger mosquito
- Yellow fever mosquito: carries dengue
- Western yellowjacket: eats native insects in high-elevation forests
- Large centipede and scorpions

**Ants** (Hawaii had no native ants)
- Big-headed ant (1879)
- Argentine ant (1940): threatens the native bees and the plants they pollinate on Haleakalā
- Little fire ant (1999): painful sting. Blinds pets and wildlife
- Yellow crazy ant: attacks seabird chicks

**Agricultural pests**
- Formosan subterranean termite
- Coffee berry borer (2010)
- Coconut rhinoceros beetle (2013): kills coconut palms
- Two-lined spittlebug (2016): destroys Big Island pastures
- Mediterranean, oriental and melon fruit flies
- Varroa mite (2007): damages honeybee hives

**Honeybee (1857):** pollinates crops. Competes with native bees

**Snails, slugs and worms**
- Giant African snail (1936)
- Rosy wolf snail (1955): released to control the giant African snail. Instead ate native tree snails and drove dozens of species to extinction
- Semi-slug: carries rat lungworm, which infects humans
- Apple snail: eats taro in lo'i
- New Guinea flatworm: eats native snails

### Fish and marine life

**Freshwater**
- Mosquitofish (1905): released to control mosquitoes. Eats native damselfly larvae
- Tilapia: several species
- Guppies, swordtails, mollies
- Largemouth bass, smallmouth bass
- Peacock bass
- Rainbow trout: Kōke'e on Kaua'i
- Chinese catfish, channel catfish
- Pond loach
- Armored catfish: burrows into stream banks

**Marine fish (1950s)** introduced to boost fisheries
- Roi (peacock grouper): eats native reef fish. Carries ciguatera toxin
- Ta'ape (bluestripe snapper)
- To'au (blacktail snapper)

**Marine invertebrates and algae**
- Snowflake coral
- Upside-down jellyfish
- Invasive algae: gorilla ogo, spiny seaweed, Kappaphycus (smothers reefs in Kāne'ohe Bay)

### Diseases and pathogens

- **Avian malaria:** kills native honeycreepers in days. Arrived with mosquitoes
- **Avian pox:** causes tumor-like lesions
- **Rapid 'Ōhi'a Death:** a fungus discovered in 2014. Killing 'ōhi'a, the dominant native tree across most Hawaiian forests
- **Myrtle rust (2005)**
- **Toxoplasmosis:** spread by cats

- Coffee: Kona coffee
- Macadamia (1881)
- Papaya

- Avocado
- Lychee, longan, rambutan
- Citrus

**Ornamentals**

- Orchids

**Timber and reforestation**
- Eucalyptus

- Norfolk Island pine, Cook pine
- Silky oak
- Tropical ash
- Paperbark
- Albizia: fast-growing. Its brittle limbs crash onto roads and power lines

**Worst invasive plants**
- Strawberry guava: forms dense thickets in native forest. Uses more water than native trees
- Miconia (1960s): arrived as an ornamental. Huge purple-backed leaves shade out everything below. Called the "purple plague" after overtaking much of Tahiti
- Kiawe (1828): reportedly grew from a single seed planted in Honolulu. Now dominates dry lowlands
- Koa haole: covers dry lowlands
- Christmas berry
- Lantana: thorny thickets in dry areas
- Clidemia (Koster's curse): wet forest understory
- Banana poka: a vine that smothers forest canopy
- Kāhili ginger: carpets wet forest floors
- Australian tree fern: outcompetes native tree ferns
- Cane tibouchina
- Octopus tree
- Gorse: spiny shrub on Maui and the Big Island
- Blackberry
- Fireweed: toxic to livestock
- Ivy gourd
- Red mangrove (1902): planted on Moloka'i. Now clogs coastal fishponds and wetlands

**Invasive grasses** (these brought wildfire to Hawaii)
- Fountain grass: Big Island dry slopes
- Guinea grass
- Molasses grass
- Buffelgrass
- Kikuyu grass

These grasses grow fast, dry out and burn hot. Native Hawaiian plants did not evolve with frequent fire and cannot recover from it, while the grasses regrow quickly. Each fire expands the grassland. The 2023 Lahaina fire spread through abandoned plantation land overgrown with these grasses.

**Water weeds**
- Water hyacinth
- Salvinia

### Impact of Western contact

- Hawaii holds more federally listed endangered species than any other state, despite being one of the smallest states
- Over 90% of native dry forest is gone
- More than 70% of bird extinctions in the United States occurred in Hawaii
- More species have been introduced to Hawaii (over 5,000 plants alone) than exist natively
