## 1. Size of Hawaii

- **Total land area:** about 6,420 sq mi (16,600 km²), between Connecticut and New Jersey in size
- **Islands:** 8 main islands plus about 130 smaller islets, atolls and shoals strung across roughly 1,500 miles of ocean
- **Big Island:** about 4,030 sq mi, larger than all the other islands combined
- **Elevation:** sea level to 13,803 ft at Mauna Kea. A single island runs from tropical beach to alpine desert with seasonal snow
- **Isolation:** about 2,400 miles from the nearest continent, the most isolated major island group on Earth

### What makes an island robust

- **Area:** larger islands hold larger populations that survive bad years
- **Elevation:** high mountains catch rain and create many habitat bands. Species can shift uphill or downhill as climate changes
- **Rainfall:** wet islands support more biomass and recover faster from disturbance
- **Age:** older islands have more endemic species but are eroding and sinking. Younger islands have fewer endemics but more land and height
- **Volcanic activity:** lava destroys habitat but also creates new land
- **Connections:** islands that were once joined can exchange species

### Hawai'i (Big Island)

**Robustness: Highest**

- Covers 63% of the state's land
- Five volcanoes: Kohala (extinct), Mauna Kea (dormant), Hualālai (dormant), Mauna Loa (active, largest volcano on Earth by volume) and Kīlauea (one of the most active volcanoes on Earth)
- The only island with the full range of biomes, including true alpine desert on two summits
- Huge unbroken forest belts on the slopes of Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa and Hualālai
- **Strengths:**
 - Sheer size means no single disaster can wipe out a species
 - Elevation range of nearly 14,000 ft gives every species room to shift with climate
 - New land is constantly forming, so pioneer species always have room
 - Kīpuka (patches of old forest left standing between lava flows) act as seed banks and refuges that recolonize fresh lava
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Lava regularly destroys large areas. About 90% of Kīlauea's surface is less than 1,100 years old
 - Volcanic gas (vog) stresses plants downwind
 - Being young, it has fewer endemic species of its own than older islands
- **No-human outlook:** the most stable island long term. Lava destruction is offset by constant new land and huge refuges

### Maui

**Robustness: High**

- Two volcanoes joined by a low isthmus
 - West Maui Mountains: old, eroded, extremely wet. Pu'u Kukui gets around 350 inches of rain a year
 - Haleakalā: massive and young. Last erupted roughly 400 to 500 years ago
- Contains alpine habitat on Haleakalā's summit, home to the Haleakalā silversword and the 'ua'u (Hawaiian petrel)
- Large wet forests on Haleakalā's north and east slopes
- **Maui Nui:** Maui, Moloka'i, Lāna'i and Kaho'olawe were joined as one island called Maui Nui during periods of low sea level. At its largest, about 1.2 million years ago, Maui Nui was roughly 5,600 sq mi, bigger than today's Big Island. Species moved freely across it, so the four islands share many endemics
- **Strengths:**
 - Second-highest elevation range
 - Two separate mountain systems act as independent refuges
 - Shares species with neighboring islands
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Leeward Haleakalā is dry and slow to recover from drought
 - Alpine and subalpine zones are small and fragile
- **No-human outlook:** very stable. Haleakalā's eastern rainforest is one of the largest intact forest blocks in the state

### O'ahu

**Robustness: Moderate to high**

- Two eroded volcanic ranges
 - Ko'olau Range: windward, wet, dramatic fluted cliffs
 - Wai'anae Range: leeward, drier, holds the highest point
- The 'Ewa Plain was once covered in loulu palm forest and lowland dry forest
- Home to the richest radiation of tree snails (Achatinella) in Hawaii
- **Strengths:**
 - Two separate mountain ranges with different climates
 - Large lowland plains with rich dry forest
 - Many streams with strong native fish populations
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Highest point is only about 4,000 ft. No alpine or subalpine zone
 - No high-elevation refuge if conditions warm
 - Middle-aged and eroding
- **No-human outlook:** stable and biologically rich. The lack of high mountains matters little without introduced disease. With mosquitoes present, this is why O'ahu lost more native birds than higher islands

### Kaua'i

**Robustness: Moderate to high**

- The oldest of the large main islands, with the most deeply eroded landscape
- Mount Wai'ale'ale averages around 450 inches of rain a year, one of the wettest places on Earth
- **Alaka'i Swamp:** a high plateau of montane bog and wet forest around 4,000 ft. The last stronghold of several rare Kaua'i birds
- **Waimea Canyon:** about 10 miles long and 3,600 ft deep
- **Nā Pali Coast:** towering sea cliffs with isolated hanging valleys
- **Strengths:**
 - Highest rate of single-island endemism in Hawaii. Millions of years of isolation produced species found only here
 - No active volcanism. Habitats have been stable for millions of years
 - Very high rainfall feeds dozens of permanent streams
 - Deep canyons and cliffs shelter rare plants
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Many species live in tiny ranges, sometimes a single valley
 - The island is sinking and eroding. High-elevation habitat shrinks over geologic time
 - Sits in the most common hurricane path. Hurricane 'Iwa (1982) and Hurricane 'Iniki (1992) both hit Kaua'i directly
 - Old, heavily leached soils are low in phosphorus
- **No-human outlook:** stable but with many localized species. A single bad storm or landslide could eliminate a species restricted to one valley

### Moloka'i

**Robustness: Moderate**

- Two volcanoes
 - East Moloka'i: wet. Holds Kamakou and the Pēpē'ōpae bog
 - West Moloka'i: low and dry
- The north coast has some of the tallest sea cliffs on Earth, reaching about 3,600 to 3,900 ft
- Kalaupapa Peninsula: a flat lava shelf at the base of the cliffs
- **Strengths:**
 - Wet eastern mountains with intact bogs and forest
 - Sea cliffs are nearly inaccessible refuges for rare plants and seabirds
 - Shares species with the rest of Maui Nui
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Small area
 - Western half is dry with limited habitat diversity
 - No alpine zone
- **No-human outlook:** stable in the east and fragile in the west

### Lāna'i

**Robustness: Low to moderate**

- One eroded volcano
- Sits in the rain shadow of West Maui. Lowlands get about 10 inches of rain a year
- The summit ridge, Lāna'ihale, catches moisture from passing clouds. Fog drip on leaves supplies much of the island's water
- Kānepu'u preserve holds a remnant of native dry forest with olopua and lama trees
- **Strengths:**
 - Summit fog belt supports a small wet forest
 - Shares species with Maui Nui
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Small and dry
 - No permanent streams
 - Heavily dependent on the fog belt. Loss of summit vegetation means loss of water capture
 - Prolonged drought hits the whole island at once
- **No-human outlook:** a dry forest island. Stable in normal years but vulnerable to long droughts

### Ni'ihau

**Robustness: Low to moderate**

- Low, flat and old
- Sits in the rain shadow of Kaua'i
- Holds the largest natural lakes in Hawaii. They are shallow and seasonal, filling in wet years and drying in dry years
- Important haul-out and pupping area for Hawaiian monk seals
- Major waterbird and seabird habitat
- **Strengths:**
 - Large undisturbed coastline for seals, turtles and seabirds
 - Wetlands support native waterbirds
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Low elevation and little rain limit habitat types
 - No forest belt or mountain refuge
 - Low species diversity
- **No-human outlook:** a coastal and wetland island. Excellent for seabirds, seals and waterbirds. Poor for forest species

### Kaho'olawe

**Robustness: Lowest of the main islands**

- The smallest and driest main island
- Sits in the rain shadow of Haleakalā. Gets about 10 to 25 inches of rain a year
- No permanent streams
- Pollen records show it once held native dry forest and shrubland
- **Strengths:**
 - Coastlines suitable for seabird colonies and turtles
 - Shares species with Maui Nui
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Very low rainfall
 - Thin soils that erode quickly once vegetation is lost
 - Small area and low elevation
- **No-human outlook:** a fragile dry forest and shrubland island. Stable only as long as its plant cover holds the soil in place

### Northwestern Hawaiian Islands

**Robustness: Low for land habitats, very high for seabirds and marine life**

A chain of tiny islands, atolls and reefs northwest of Kaua'i. These are the eroded remains of islands that were once as large as Kaua'i. Combined land area is only about 3 sq mi.

| Island | Approx. land area | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nihoa | 171 acres | Rocky island about 900 ft high. Home to the Nihoa finch, Nihoa millerbird and a native loulu palm |
| Mokumanamana (Necker) | 46 acres | Rocky. Many ancient Hawaiian stone shrines |
| French Frigate Shoals | about 60 acres of sandy islets | Main nesting site for green sea turtles. East Island was washed away by Hurricane Walaka in 2018 |
| Gardner Pinnacles | about 6 acres | Two rock pinnacles |
| Laysan | about 1,000 acres | Has a hypersaline central lake. Home to the Laysan finch and Laysan duck |
| Lisianski | about 400 acres | Sandy island |
| Pearl and Hermes Atoll | about 80 acres | Low sandy islets on a large reef |
| Midway Atoll | about 1,500 acres | Largest Laysan albatross colony on Earth |
| Kure Atoll | about 200 acres | The northernmost coral atoll on Earth |

- **Strengths:**
 - Extremely isolated, so very few new species ever arrive
 - Massive seabird colonies with no natural land predators
 - Coral reefs with very high rates of endemic fish
- **Weaknesses:**
 - Most land is only a few feet above sea level
 - Storms and sea level rise can erase whole islets
 - Tiny populations of land birds vulnerable to a single disaster
- **No-human outlook:** stable seabird and seal sanctuaries. Land bird populations stay small and risky

### Robustness ranking

1. **Hawai'i (Big Island):** size, height and new land outweigh volcanic destruction
2. **Maui:** two mountain systems and alpine habitat
3. **Kaua'i:** wettest and most stable geologically but with many tiny-range species
4. **O'ahu:** two ranges and rich lowlands but no high-elevation refuge
5. **Moloka'i:** strong wet east and fragile dry west
6. **Lāna'i:** dry and dependent on summit fog
7. **Ni'ihau:** low, dry, coastal and wetland habitat only
8. **Kaho'olawe:** smallest and driest
9. **Northwestern Hawaiian Islands:** fragile land but thriving seabird and reef ecosystems

### Long-term geology

The Pacific Plate carries the islands northwest about 3 to 4 inches a year away from the volcanic hotspot under the Big Island. Each island grows, stops erupting, erodes, sinks and eventually becomes a low atoll and then a submerged seamount. Kaua'i will eventually look like Ni'ihau. Ni'ihau will eventually look like Nihoa. A new island, Kama'ehuakanaloa (formerly Lō'ihi), is growing underwater southeast of the Big Island and will reach the surface in tens of thousands of years. Species colonize each new island from older ones, which is how the native biota has persisted for millions of years even though each individual island is temporary.

## 3. What the Ecosystem Would Look Like

Without Polynesian or European arrival, Hawaii would keep the biota it had for millions of years. The only native land mammal is the Hawaiian hoary bat. There would be no rats, pigs, goats, cattle, cats, mongooses, mosquitoes or ants. Native reptiles and amphibians would also be absent.

Birds would fill the roles mammals hold elsewhere:

- **Large grazers:** moa-nalo, goose-sized flightless ducks with tortoise-like beaks, along with several large nēnē-type geese
- **Predators:** a Hawaiian eagle and long-legged stilt-owls that hunted birds on the forest floor
- **Ground dwellers:** flightless rails and ibises
- **Songbirds:** more than 50 species of honeycreepers, with bills adapted for nectar, seeds, insects or wood-boring

The forests would be dense and rich, with huge lobelia radiations, tree ferns and koa-'ōhi'a canopy. Millions of seabirds would nest on the main islands rather than only on remote rat-free islets. Their guano would carry ocean nutrients into the forest. Monk seals and green turtles would crowd the beaches. Insects would include around 1,000 species of fruit flies, carnivorous caterpillars and flightless crickets.

---

## 4. Overall Robustness

The system is robust against the forces it evolved with: lava flows, eruptions, hurricanes, tsunamis, flash floods and drought. 'Ōhi'a trees colonize fresh lava within years. Rainforest rebuilds in centuries. Steep elevation gradients let species shift uphill or downhill as climate swings.

It is extremely fragile against anything new. Plants lost thorns and toxins because nothing grazed them heavily. Birds never learned to fear ground predators. Native birds had no resistance to bird malaria. With almost no people, new species would arrive only by wind, ocean drift or bird transport. That pace averages roughly one successful arrival every tens of thousands of years, slow enough that the system would stay stable for a very long time.

## 5. Biomes and Their Robustness

| Biome | Where | Robustness |
|---|---|---|
| Coral reef | Shallow coast | High against storms. Vulnerable to warming ocean temperatures from global climate change |
| Coastal strand | Beaches, dunes | High. Recovers fast after storms and tsunamis |
| Lowland dry forest | Leeward slopes | Moderate. Most tree diversity of any Hawaiian forest. Fire is rare without invasive grasses |
| Lowland wet forest | Windward slopes | High. Constant rain and fast regrowth |
| Montane wet forest | 2,000 to 6,000 ft | High. Core habitat for honeycreepers |
| Montane bogs | Rainy summits | Low. Easily damaged by any rooting or trampling animal |
| Subalpine shrubland | 6,000 to 9,500 ft | Moderate. Slow growth and long recovery |
| Alpine desert | Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa, Haleakalā summits | Low. Silverswords grow for decades and few species live there |
| Streams | Island-wide | High. Native gobies and shrimp are flood-adapted and recolonize from the ocean |
| Lava tubes and caves | Young volcanic islands | Low. Tiny populations of blind insects found nowhere else |
| Anchialine pools | Coastal lava | Moderate. Small but self-contained |
