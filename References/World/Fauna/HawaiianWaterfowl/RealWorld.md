## A. Egg-Laying Rates

| Species | Eggs per clutch | Incubation | Breeding season | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nēnē (Hawaiian goose) | 2 to 5, average 3, rarely 6 | ~30 days | Peak October to March | One clutch per year; re-nests if eggs are lost |
| Koloa maoli (Hawaiian duck) | 2 to 10; Kauaʻi study mean 5.8 (range 4 to 9) | ~28 days | Year-round, peak December to May | Hidden ground nests near water |
| Laysan duck | ~4 on Laysan Island | ~28 days | Variable | Larger clutches on Midway where food is richer |

- Nēnē: female incubates; male guards. Goslings are flightless for 10 to 14 weeks.
- Koloa: female incubates alone. Ducklings feed themselves from hatching.

## B. Current Populations

| Species | Population | Historic low |
|---|---|---|
| Nēnē | 3,862 statewide (2022 survey) | About 30 birds in the 1950s |
| Koloa maoli | Estimates range from under 1,000 to about 2,200 pure birds; true numbers uncertain because of hybrids | Gone from several islands by the mid-1900s |

- Over half of all nēnē live on Kauaʻi, which has no established mongoose population.
- Nēnē recovery came from captive breeding starting in 1949 and releases starting in 1960.
- Nēnē were downlisted from endangered to threatened under the federal Endangered Species Act.
- Koloa were common on all main islands except Lānaʻi and Kahoʻolawe before 1900.

## C. Can the Islands Support Them?

- Yes. Both species are far below what the habitat once held.
- The low numbers come from losses, not lack of space or food.
- Eggs laid far outnumber birds that reach adulthood. One Kauaʻi base recorded at least 76 nesting adult nēnē hatching 81 eggs with 35 goslings identified.

## D. How Nature Keeps Numbers in Check

**Before humans (natural checks)**
- **Slow reproduction:** small nēnē clutches, one brood a year, long gosling dependency.
- **Territory:** lifelong pairs defend nest sites, capping how many families fit in an area.
- **Food supply:** clutch size follows food. Laysan ducks lay more eggs where food is richer.
- **Drought:** shrinks wetlands for koloa.
- **Native predators:** ʻio (Hawaiian hawk) and owls take young birds.
- **Molt:** adult nēnē lose flight feathers between March and June, leaving them exposed.

**Today (human-caused checks)**
- Dogs, cats, mongooses, rats and pigs prey on eggs, young and adults.
- Feral cattle, goats, pigs and sheep damage habitat.
- Vehicles kill nēnē on roads.
- Hybridization with feral mallards is the single greatest threat to koloa.
- Predator control in nesting areas is a top conservation priority.

## E. Could Koloa Become Farm Ducks?

**Legal barriers**
- Koloa maoli are federally endangered. Catching, keeping or collecting eggs is illegal without a federal permit.
- Permits go to conservation programs, not farms.

**Biological barriers**
- **Low output:** wild clutches average about 6 eggs. Laying breeds like Khaki Campbells produce around 250 to 300 a year.
- **Small eggs:** adults weigh only about 14 to 26 ounces.
- **Temperament:** secretive birds that live in pairs, not flocks.
- **Time:** mallards took centuries of selective breeding to become domestic breeds. Koloa would need dozens of generations at minimum.

## F. Hawaiʻi Duck Rules

- Muscovy are the only ducklings hatcheries can ship to Hawaiʻi.
- Importing mallard-derived breeds (Pekin, Khaki Campbell, Runner and others) is banned without a special research permit, out of concern they will cross with koloa.
- Muscovy are allowed because Muscovy crossed with koloa or mallards produce sterile offspring.
- The mallard is on the state's List of Restricted Animals for importation.
- Using ducks for pest control is legal. Only importing them is restricted, so ducks already on the islands can be bred locally.
- Taro farmers use ducks to control invasive apple snails.
