## 8. Variant: Reptile Land Predators

This variant adds native land reptiles as ground predators. It replaces the "no ground predators" condition from Sections 1, 2 and 5. Everything else stays the same: no people, no mammals, no mosquitoes.

### 8.1 Why Reptiles Fit Hawaiʻi

- Reptiles reach remote islands by rafting on floating trees and vegetation. Fiji, Vanuatu and New Caledonia once had land crocodiles, giant skinks, giant geckos and boas.
- Reptiles need far less food than mammals of the same size, so they survive on small islands.
- Warmth limits them. Hawaiʻi's lowlands are warm year-round. Highlands above about 2,000 m have cold nights and frost.
- This creates a hard split: warm lowlands are reptile country; cool uplands are bird refuges.
- Older islands have had more time to be colonized. Kauaʻi would hold the most reptile species; the Big Island the fewest.

### 8.2 Reptile Predators

#### Island monitor (*Varanus*-type)
- **Size:** 1.5 to 2.5 m long; 15 to 40 kg.
- **Habitat:** Lowland forest, coast, grassland, lava fields in warm zones.
- **Hunting:** Active forager. Tracks scent with a forked tongue. Digs out buried and hidden nests. Young climb trees.
- **Prey:** Eggs, goslings, ducklings, molting adults, moa-nalo chicks, seabird colonies, turtle eggs, carrion.
- **Reproduction:** 10 to 25 eggs buried in soil; incubation 7 to 8 months.
- **Intelligence:** Among the smartest reptiles. Learns routes, remembers productive nesting grounds, patrols them seasonally.
- **Threat level:** Highest overall. Scent finds even well-hidden nests.

#### Land crocodile (mekosuchine-type)
- **Size:** 1.5 to 2.5 m long; short deep snout; partly terrestrial.
- **Habitat:** Wetland edges, lowland streams, marsh, forest near water.
- **Hunting:** Ambush at water's edge and along trails. Can travel overland.
- **Prey:** Koloa, coots, gallinules, stilts, geese coming to drink, moa-nalo.
- **Reproduction:** 10 to 25 eggs in a mound nest; incubation about 80 to 90 days. Mother guards the nest.
- **Threat level:** Deadly around water. Makes lowland wetlands dangerous for all waterfowl.

#### Giant ground skink
- **Size:** 50 to 70 cm long; 1 to 2 kg.
- **Habitat:** Leaf litter, rock piles, burrows, forest floor.
- **Hunting:** Slow forager. Omnivore.
- **Prey:** Eggs, rail chicks, mole ducks, insects, snails, fallen fruit.
- **Reproduction:** Live birth, 2 to 6 young.
- **Threat level:** Constant low-level nest raider on the forest floor.

#### Giant forest gecko
- **Size:** 40 to 60 cm long.
- **Habitat:** Forest canopy, tree hollows, cliff faces.
- **Hunting:** Nocturnal. Sticky toe pads for climbing.
- **Prey:** Honeycreeper nestlings, roosting small birds, eggs, insects, nectar, fruit.
- **Reproduction:** 2 eggs per clutch; several clutches per year.
- **Threat level:** Major for forest birds. Minor for waterfowl.

#### Pacific ground boa (*Candoia*-type)
- **Size:** 1 to 2 m long.
- **Habitat:** Forest floor, stream banks, low trees, marsh edges.
- **Hunting:** Ambush at trails, nests and roosts. Constricts prey. Climbs well.
- **Prey:** Ducklings, adult koloa, Laysan ducks, rails, eggs, bats.
- **Reproduction:** Live birth, 10 to 30 young.
- **Threat level:** High at night. Hits roosting and nesting birds.

### 8.3 Reptile Distribution by Island

| Island | Reptiles | Raptors | Bird outlook |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kauaʻi (oldest) | All five types | No eagles; stilt-owl present | Heavy ground pressure, light air pressure. Birds most shaped by reptiles. |
| Oʻahu | Monitor, crocodile, skink, gecko, boa | Eagle, harrier, stilt-owl | Pressure from ground and air. Hardest island for waterfowl. |
| Maui Nui | Monitor, skink, gecko, boa; crocodiles limited to lowland streams | Eagle, harrier (Molokaʻi), stilt-owls | High uplands of Haleakalā give strong refuges. |
| Big Island (youngest, highest) | Skinks and geckos in lowlands; few or no large predators | ʻIo | Safest island. Huge cold uplands. Best home for giant geese. |

### 8.4 Effects on Each Bird Group

**Nēnē**
- Nesting shifts to uplands, lava fields, cliff ledges and offshore islets.
- Winter nesting becomes even more important, since reptiles are sluggish in cool months.
- Adults can fight off skinks and geckos. They cannot fight monitors or crocodiles.
- Lowland grazing done in daylight groups with sentinels posted.
- Families stay tighter; goslings never stray from parents.

**Koloa maoli**
- Lowland wetlands become dangerous because of crocodiles.
- Shift to fast mountain streams, high bogs and cold upland ponds.
- Nests move to pond islets, overhanging banks and tree cavities.
- Clutches grow larger and re-nesting becomes faster to replace losses.
- Even more secretive than today.

**Laysan duck**
- Night foraging now meets boas and geckos.
- Survives mainly on atolls and offshore islets without reptiles.
- Rare or absent on main islands.

**Moa-nalo**
- Hardest hit. Flightlessness is risky with ground predators.
- Two possible outcomes:
  - **Fighters:** Grow larger (8 to 15 kg) with heavy beaks and strong kicks. Live in herds. Chicks guarded in the center of the group.
  - **Refugees:** Survive only in cold uplands or on islands without monitors and crocodiles.
- Eggs are the weak point. Both parents guard nests in dense thickets.

**Giant geese and nēnē-nui**
- Same logic as moa-nalo.
- The Big Island giant goose does best because of the cold uplands and lack of large reptiles.

**Kauaʻi mole duck**
- Likely never evolves on a Kauaʻi full of skinks and monitors.
- If it exists, it survives only in the cold, wet high bogs of the Alakaʻi where reptiles cannot stay active.

**Flightless rails and ibises**
- Restricted to cold uplands and reptile-free islets.
- Far fewer species than in the reptile-free scenario.

**Wetland neighbors**
- Coots build floating nests away from banks; safe from monitors, still exposed to crocodiles.
- Stilts nest on open mudflats where approaching reptiles are seen early. Mob monitors aggressively.
- Night herons gain a food source by eating hatchling lizards and young crocodiles.

**Forest birds**
- Geckos and boas raid nests.
- Nests move higher, onto thin branches that cannot hold reptiles, or into hanging and spiny cover.

**Seabirds**
- Monitors raid lowland colonies.
- Colonies pushed to cliffs, high peaks and offshore islets.

### 8.5 Raptors Become Partial Allies

- Eagles, harriers, ʻio and stilt-owls also hunt young reptiles.
- Eagles take young monitors and small crocodiles basking in the open.
- Stilt-owls specialize in geckos and skinks at night.
- With reptiles as alternate prey, raptor pressure on waterfowl drops somewhat.
- Raptor numbers likely rise thanks to extra food.

### 8.6 New Ecological Rules

| Rule | Effect |
|---|---|
| Altitude equals safety | Cold uplands become nurseries. Lowlands are feeding grounds visited in groups. |
| Cold season equals nesting season | Reptiles slow down in cool months and cool nights. |
| Dawn advantage | Reptiles are cold and slow at dawn. Birds feed in lowlands early and retreat by midday when reptiles are warm and active. |
| Water equals danger | Crocodiles make lowland wetland edges deadly. |
| Islets equal nurseries | Offshore rocks and pond islands hold the safest nests. |
| Flight equals survival | Flightless species become rare or restricted. |
| More eggs, more care | Clutch sizes rise to offset nest losses. Parents guard harder. |
| Group living | Flocks, sentinels and shared nesting grounds increase. |
| Lower lowland density | Total waterfowl numbers drop below the reptile-free scenario. Uplands hold the core populations. |

### 8.9 Intelligent Bird Scenario Changes

**Reptiles as the common enemy**
- Reptiles unite all bird species more strongly than raptors did.
- The raptor truce becomes a partnership. Waterfowl report basking reptiles; eagles and owls hunt them.
- Raptors gain status as protectors instead of feared rulers.

**Tactics against cold-blooded predators**
- Strike or move at dawn when reptiles are cold.
- Track female monitors to find buried egg sites, then dig them up.
- Mob a guarding crocodile while others raid the mound nest.
- Plant thorny shrubs in rings around nesting grounds.
- Choose nest sites surrounded by open ground so approaching reptiles are seen early.

**Territory and culture**
- Clans hold the cold uplands as strongholds.
- Lowlands become a dangerous frontier, entered in organized grazing parties.
- Chicks taught reptile scent, tracks and warning signs from hatching.
- Seasons named by reptile activity: cold safe season, warm danger season.
- Kauaʻi birds become ground-defense specialists. Oʻahu birds fight on two fronts. Big Island birds live the most secure lives.

**Roles**
- **Nēnē:** High sentinels, leaders, coordinators.
- **Koloa:** Water watchers; spot crocodiles at banks.
- **Laysan ducks:** Night watch against boas and geckos.
- **Moa-nalo (fighter form):** Heavy defenders; kick and bite monitors.
- **Stilts and coots:** Loud alarms and mob leaders.
- **Herons:** Reptile hatchling hunters; rise in status from outcasts to useful allies.

**Arms race**
- Monitors learn guard schedules and patrol routes.
- Birds rotate sentinels unpredictably and move nest sites year to year.
- Older, experienced monitors become known individuals, possibly named and feared.

### 8.10 A Person as Patron in the Reptile World

**What protection means to the birds**
- Removing monitors and crocodiles from nesting areas.
- Fencing and guarding nests.
- Clearing ambush cover at wetland edges.
- Securing safe water access.
- This kind of protection is concrete and life-changing, so loyalty would be stronger than in the reptile-free scenario.

**What the birds offer**
- Early warning of reptiles approaching.
- Locating reptile nests and dens.
- Scouting safe paths through lowlands.
- Night watch from Laysan ducks; water watch from koloa.

**If the person needs protection**
- Large monitors and crocodiles threaten people too. Monitor bites cause heavy bleeding and infection.
- Birds act as a living alarm system.
- Mobbing flocks distract a monitor or crocodile long enough for escape.
- Fighter moa-nalo and ganders can body-block smaller reptiles.

**Risks**
- Birds gathering at the person's land draw reptiles to it.
- Monitors learn to follow bird traffic to food.
- Dependency weakens the birds' own defenses.
- If the person leaves, a concentrated nesting ground becomes an easy feeding site for reptiles.
- Raptors may resent the person taking over their new protector role.
