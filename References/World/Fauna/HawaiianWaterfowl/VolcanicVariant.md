## 9. Variant: Volcanic Warm Uplands

This variant builds on Section 8. Unusual volcanic activity keeps the upper slopes warm year-round through heated ground, steam vents, hot springs and warm air rising off lava. The altitude refuge from Section 8 no longer exists. Reptiles can live and hunt from coast to summit.

### 9.1 What the Volcanic Presence Creates

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Warm ground | Heated soil across upper slopes; frost-free year-round |
| Steam vents (fumaroles) | Hot, wet vapor; patches of lush moss and fern growth around them |
| Hot springs and warm pools | Warm water at high elevation; rich in algae and invertebrates |
| Crater lakes | Deep, sometimes acidic or mineral-heavy water |
| Gas hollows | Low depressions where heavy carbon dioxide pools at ground level |
| Sulfur plumes | Airborne sulfur gas drifting downwind from vents |
| Active and fresh lava | Hot flows that block movement; barren black rock with no cover |
| Kīpuka | Islands of old forest surrounded by newer lava flows |
| Ash fall | Fertilizes soil; drives fast plant growth after eruptions |

### 9.2 Effects on Reptiles

- Active year-round at every elevation. No cold season slowdown.
- No safe dawn window at high elevation, since the ground stays warm overnight.
- Monitors and crocodiles bury eggs in heated soil. Incubation shortens from 7 to 8 months to about 3 to 4 months.
- Breeding becomes year-round. Reptile numbers rise sharply.
- Body size likely increases with steady warmth and food. Monitors could reach 3 m.
- Crocodiles live in warm upland pools and hot spring outflows. Acidic crater lakes stay crocodile-free.
- Low-slung reptiles are vulnerable to carbon dioxide in gas hollows. Monitors and boas die in them.

### 9.3 Effects on Birds

**Lost advantages**
- No cold upland nurseries.
- No cool-season nesting advantage.
- No safe dawn feeding window in the highlands.

**New advantages**
- Rich food. Ash-fed plants grow fast, feeding nēnē, moa-nalo and giant geese.
- Warm pools full of invertebrates feed koloa and Laysan ducks at high elevation.
- Open lava fields give clear sightlines. Reptiles crossing them are seen from far away.
- Acidic crater lakes offer safe water free of crocodiles.
- Gas hollows kill ground predators. Birds standing tall or flying above are safer than low reptiles.
- Geothermal ground can incubate eggs.

**New dangers**
- Sulfur plumes damage bird lungs. Birds are more sensitive to airborne gas than reptiles.
- Ducklings and goslings walking into gas hollows die.
- Sudden eruptions destroy nesting grounds.
- Lava flows cut off kīpuka, trapping birds with any reptiles inside.

### 9.4 Geothermal Nesting

Real birds already do this. The maleo of Sulawesi and the Niuafoʻou megapode of Tonga bury eggs in volcanically heated soil and leave the heat to incubate them.

**How Hawaiian birds might adapt**
- Bury eggs in warm ground instead of sitting on them.
- Parents freed from incubation to feed and guard.
- Chicks hatch well developed, dig out and run or fly within days.
- Eggs laid one at a time across a long season instead of in a single clutch.
- Season totals rise, since parents do not need to sit.

**The conflict**
- Monitors and crocodiles want the same warm soil for their own eggs.
- Monitors also dig up bird eggs buried there.
- Warm nesting fields become contested ground between birds and reptiles.
- Birds that use them need communal guarding.

**Which species benefit most**
- Moa-nalo and giant geese: large eggs, flightless parents; heated soil frees them to guard in herds.
- Nēnē: may split between ground-nesting and geothermal-burying populations.
- Koloa and Laysan ducks: less likely; stay with hidden or water-surrounded nests.

### 9.5 New Refuges

| Refuge | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Offshore islets and sea stacks | No reptiles; most important nurseries |
| Cliff ledges | Hard for monitors and crocodiles to reach |
| Acidic crater lakes | Crocodiles avoid the water; islands in them become safe nest sites |
| Edges of gas hollows | Reptiles that follow birds in die; birds learn the safe margins |
| Fresh lava fields | No cover for ambush; birds see attackers coming |
| Thin tree branches | Cannot hold heavy reptiles |

### 9.6 Effects on Each Bird Group

**Nēnē**
- Feed well on ash-enriched grasses.
- Nest on cliffs, islets, crater lake islands or bury eggs in guarded geothermal fields.
- Graze open lava fields where predators are visible.
- Strong fliers survive best.

**Koloa maoli**
- Feed in warm upland pools and hot spring runoff.
- Nest on islands in acidic crater lakes.
- Lose the cold-stream refuge; rely on water-surrounded nests.

**Laysan duck**
- Survives on offshore islets and atolls.
- Absent or very rare on the main islands.

**Moa-nalo and giant geese**
- Only the fighter form survives: large, herd-living, aggressive.
- Geothermal egg burial with herd guarding becomes their main strategy.
- Refugee form dies out, since no cold refuge remains.

**Kauaʻi mole duck**
- Does not exist in this variant.

**Flightless rails and ibises**
- Survive only on reptile-free islets.

**Seabirds**
- Restricted to sea cliffs and offshore islets.

### 9.7 Raptors in the Volcanic Variant

- More reptiles mean more food. Raptor numbers rise.
- Eagles hunt basking monitors on open lava.
- Rising warm air off lava fields gives soaring raptors easy lift. They patrol huge areas.
- Raptors become the main check on reptile numbers.
- Raptors sensitive to sulfur plumes avoid downwind zones, leaving reptiles free there.

### 9.8 Updated Ecological Rules

| Rule | Effect |
|---|---|
| Altitude no longer equals safety | Reptiles everywhere. |
| Water and rock equal safety | Islets, cliffs and acidic lakes replace uplands as nurseries. |
| Open ground equals visibility | Lava fields become safe feeding zones. |
| Heat equals contested ground | Birds and reptiles fight over warm nesting soil. |
| Gas equals a double-edged weapon | Ground gas kills reptiles; air gas harms birds. |
| Flight equals survival | Flightless birds survive only as large herd fighters or on islets. |
| Food is abundant | Plant growth is high. Populations limited by predators, not food. |
| Eruptions reset the map | Nesting grounds, kīpuka and refuges shift every few years. |

### 9.9 Updated Island Outlook

| Island | Outlook |
|---|---|
| Kauaʻi | Highest reptile diversity, no eagles. Birds survive mainly on cliffs (Nā Pali) and islets. |
| Oʻahu | Pressure from ground and air. Fewest safe zones. |
| Maui Nui | Crater lakes and lava fields of Haleakalā offer the best mainland refuges. |
| Big Island | Loses its safe status. Most volcanic activity means the most gas hollows, lava fields and geothermal nesting grounds. Becomes the island of extremes. |

### 9.11 Intelligent Bird Scenario Changes

**Volcanic knowledge as power**
- Clans that understand vents, gas, lava and eruption signs hold power.
- Elders read ground tremors, smell, steam changes and ash fall as warnings.
- Eruption history passed down as oral tradition.

**Using the volcano as a weapon**
- Lure monitors into carbon dioxide hollows by placing eggs or decoys at the bottom.
- Use active lava flows as moats around nesting grounds.
- Drive reptiles onto open lava for raptor allies to kill.

**Geothermal nurseries**
- Communal warm-soil nesting fields become fortified sites.
- Rotating guard shifts from all species.
- Moa-nalo herds as the heavy guard; nēnē as sentinels; raptors as air cover.
- Control of nursery fields becomes the main source of conflict and politics.

**Movement and settlement**
- Kīpuka become isolated villages.
- Crater lake islands become strongholds.
- Lava fields serve as open roads where travel is safe in daylight.
- Downwind sulfur zones avoided; birds learn wind patterns.

**Culture**
- Gas hollows treated as sacred or cursed ground.
- Eruptions seen as world-changing events that redraw territory.
- Chicks taught to smell gas, read steam and avoid low hollows.

### 9.12 A Person as Patron in the Volcanic Variant

**What the person provides**
- Guards geothermal nesting fields against monitors and crocodiles.
- Moves eggs from threatened fields during eruptions.
- Builds raised or walled nests reptiles cannot reach.

**What the birds provide**
- Living gas detectors. Birds react to bad air before a person notices it.
- Eruption warnings from elders who read the volcano.
- Safe route knowledge across lava fields and around gas hollows.
- Reptile tracking at all hours.

**Shared risks**
- Gas hollows and plumes threaten the person and the birds alike.
- Eruptions can destroy the person's land and the nesting grounds around it.
- A guarded nursery near the person draws monitors year-round with no cold season break.
- Year-round reptile activity means protection never gets a rest period.
