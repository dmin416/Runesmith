# Hawaiian Waterfowl: Hawaiʻi Without People

> Earth invent loot. Reptile-free no-people premise, species, raptors, intelligent-bird scenario, egg quick reference (sections 1–7). Not Caldris law. Pack: `Index.md`. Reptile variant: `ReptileVariant.md`. Domestication: `Domestication.md`. Island ecosystem: `../../Geography/VolcanicIsland/HawaiianEcosystem.md`.


---

## 1. Core Premise

Hawaiʻi with almost no human presence. No Polynesian or European settlement. No introduced mammals. Lowland forests, coastal plains and wetlands stay intact. Species lost after human arrival survive.

### What is absent
- Rats (Polynesian and ship rats)
- Pigs, dogs, cats, mongooses, goats, cattle, sheep, deer
- Chickens and jungle fowl
- Mallards and other introduced ducks (no hybridization with koloa)
- Mosquitoes (no avian malaria or avian pox)
- Invasive plants and invasive apple snails
- Hunting, land clearing, roads and vehicles

### What is present
- Full native bird community, including extinct flightless waterfowl
- Native birds of prey as the main predators of adult birds
- Native plants with no defenses against mammal grazers (few thorns, little toxicity)
- Ground nesting as a safe strategy, since no ground mammals raid nests

## 2. Major Ecological Considerations

| Factor | Effect on birds |
|---|---|
| No ground predators | Eggs and chicks far safer than today. Flightlessness evolves freely. |
| Raptors | Main cause of adult death. Eagles and harriers hunt from the air; stilt-owls hunt on the ground and in forest. |
| Food supply | Drought and poor fruiting years shrink clutches and chick survival. |
| Territory | Pairs space nests apart. Good nest sites cap population density. |
| Competition | Moa-nalo, giant geese and nēnē share browse and grass; size and diet split the habitat. |
| Volcanism | Lava flows wipe out local populations. Neighboring populations recolonize. |
| Storms and floods | Destroy wetland nests; koloa and coots re-nest quickly. |
| Island isolation | Flightless species cannot move between islands. Each island develops its own species. |
| No disease vectors | Low disease pressure. Populations can become dense without epidemics. |
| Molt | Waterfowl lose flight feathers for weeks each year; vulnerable to raptors during this window. |
| Long lives, low output | Slow-breeding, long-lived birds are favored. Small clutches with heavy parental care. |

## 3. Bird Species

Egg counts for living species come from field data. Counts for extinct species are estimates based on close relatives and body size, since fossil records do not preserve clutch size.

### 3.1 Waterfowl

#### Nēnē (Hawaiian goose), *Branta sandvicensis*
- **Status in scenario:** Common on all main islands from coast to high slopes.
- **Size:** About 1.7 to 2.3 kg; 53 to 66 cm long. Males larger.
- **Flight:** Strong flier; travels between lowland and upland feeding grounds.
- **Feet:** Reduced webbing; built for walking on lava and rough ground.
- **Diet:** Grasses, leaves, seeds, berries (ʻōhelo, pūkiawe, naupaka, hala).
- **Social life:** Lifelong pairs. Families stay together for months. Loose flocks outside nesting season.
- **Breeding:** Nests in winter (October to March). Ground scrape lined with down.
- **Eggs:** 2 to 5, average 3, rarely 6.
- **Incubation:** About 30 days. Female sits; male guards.
- **Young:** Flightless 10 to 14 weeks after hatching.
- **Maturity:** Breeds at 2 to 3 years.
- **Lifespan:** Can exceed 20 years in the wild.
- **Threats in scenario:** Eagles, harriers, ʻio on the Big Island; drought.

#### Koloa maoli (Hawaiian duck), *Anas wyvilliana*
- **Status in scenario:** Common in wetlands, streams and bogs on all main islands except Lānaʻi and Kahoʻolawe.
- **Size:** About 400 to 740 g; 48 to 50 cm long.
- **Flight:** Strong flier; fast vertical takeoff when flushed.
- **Diet:** Snails, insect larvae, worms, crustaceans, seeds, water plants, algae.
- **Social life:** Pairs, not flocks. Secretive and quiet.
- **Breeding:** Year-round, peak December to May. Hidden ground nests near water.
- **Eggs:** 2 to 10, average about 6.
- **Incubation:** About 28 days. Female only.
- **Young:** Ducklings feed themselves from hatching; mother leads and guards.
- **Lifespan:** Estimated 10 or more years.
- **Threats in scenario:** Night herons and stilt-owls take ducklings; harriers and eagles take adults; floods destroy nests.

#### Laysan duck (koloa pōhaka), *Anas laysanensis*
- **Status in scenario:** Present on the main islands as well as the northwestern atolls.
- **Size:** About 450 g; small and compact.
- **Flight:** Weak flier; short distances only.
- **Diet:** Insects, especially brine flies and their larvae; snails; seeds.
- **Activity:** Forages heavily at dusk and night.
- **Social life:** Pairs and small groups.
- **Eggs:** About 4 on poor ground; larger clutches where food is rich.
- **Incubation:** About 28 days.
- **Lifespan:** Estimated 10 or more years.
- **Threats in scenario:** Stilt-owls; competition with koloa where ranges overlap.

#### Moa-nalo (four species)
Large flightless goose-like ducks that filled the role of deer or tortoises.

| Species | Island | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Thambetochen chauliodous* | Maui Nui (Molokaʻi, Maui, Lānaʻi) | Toothlike serrations on bill for shearing leaves |
| *Thambetochen xanion* | Oʻahu | Similar to the Maui Nui species |
| *Ptaiochen pau* | Maui | Small-nosed moa-nalo; possibly shared Maui with *T. chauliodous* |
| *Chelychelynechen quassus* | Kauaʻi | Turtle-jawed moa-nalo; heavy beak like a tortoise |

- **Size:** Estimated 4 to 7 kg; heavy-legged, tiny wings.
- **Flight:** None.
- **Diet:** Ferns, leaves, shrubs; main browsers of lowland and mid-elevation forest.
- **Ecological role:** Shaped forest structure the way deer do on continents. Many native plants likely evolved partly in response to moa-nalo browsing.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 2 to 4 large eggs.
- **Incubation (estimate):** 30 to 35 days.
- **Lifespan (estimate):** 20 or more years.
- **Threats in scenario:** Eagles were the only predator large enough to take adults.

#### Giant Hawaiʻi goose, *Branta rhuax*
- **Island:** Big Island.
- **Size:** Larger and heavier than nēnē.
- **Flight:** Very limited or absent.
- **Diet:** Grazer and browser.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 2 to 4.
- **Incubation (estimate):** 30 to 35 days.

#### Nēnē-nui (giant nēnē), *Branta hylobadistes*
- **Island:** Maui (possibly other Maui Nui islands).
- **Size:** Larger than nēnē.
- **Flight:** Reduced; likely weak or near-flightless.
- **Habitat:** Wetter, wooded areas compared to nēnē.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 2 to 4.
- **Incubation (estimate):** About 30 days.

#### Kauaʻi mole duck, *Talpanas lippa*
- **Island:** Kauaʻi.
- **Size:** Small, compact duck.
- **Flight:** None.
- **Senses:** Tiny eyes; likely nearly blind. Bill packed with touch sensors.
- **Diet:** Invertebrates found by touch in forest floor litter.
- **Activity:** Possibly nocturnal.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 3 to 6.
- **Incubation (estimate):** About 28 days.
- **Threats in scenario:** Stilt-owls.

### 3.2 Birds of Prey

#### Hawaiian eagle, *Haliaeetus* sp.
- **Islands:** Oʻahu, Molokaʻi, Maui.
- **Relation:** Close relative of the white-tailed eagle.
- **Size (estimate):** 4 to 7 kg; wingspan over 2 m.
- **Diet:** Moa-nalo, geese, ducks, seabirds, fish.
- **Role:** Apex predator. The only hunter able to kill adult moa-nalo.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 1 to 3.
- **Incubation (estimate):** About 38 days.
- **Lifespan (estimate):** 20 to 30 years.

#### Wood's harrier, *Circus dossenus*
- **Islands:** Molokaʻi, Oʻahu.
- **Build:** Short wings, long legs; adapted to chasing birds through forest instead of open ground.
- **Diet:** Birds, including ducks and goslings.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 3 to 5.
- **Incubation (estimate):** About 30 to 32 days.

#### Stilt-owls, *Grallistrix* (four species)
| Species | Island |
|---|---|
| *G. auceps* | Kauaʻi |
| *G. orion* | Oʻahu |
| *G. geleches* | Molokaʻi |
| *G. erdmani* | Maui |

- **Build:** Long legs, short wings; walk and hunt on the ground and in dense forest.
- **Diet:** Small birds, ducklings, mole ducks, Laysan ducks.
- **Activity:** Nocturnal or crepuscular.
- **Eggs (estimate):** 2 to 4.
- **Incubation (estimate):** About 30 days.

#### ʻIo (Hawaiian hawk), *Buteo solitarius*
- **Island:** Big Island only.
- **Size:** About 400 to 600 g; females larger.
- **Diet:** Birds, insects, small prey; takes goslings and ducklings.
- **Eggs:** Usually 1, rarely 2.
- **Incubation:** About 38 days.

#### Pueo (Hawaiian short-eared owl), *Asio flammeus sandwichensis*
- **Status in scenario:** Uncertain. Pueo may have colonized Hawaiʻi only after Polynesian arrival brought rats as prey. In a no-people scenario it may be absent.
- **Eggs (if present):** 3 to 6.
- **Incubation:** About 26 to 28 days.

### 3.3 Wetland Neighbors

| Species | Eggs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ʻAlae keʻokeʻo (Hawaiian coot), *Fulica alai* | 3 to 10 | Floating nests; aggressive toward intruders |
| ʻAlae ʻula (Hawaiian gallinule), *Gallinula galeata sandvicensis* | 5 to 8 | Hides in dense marsh plants |

## 4. Other Animals Still Present

- **Mammals:** ʻŌpeʻapeʻa (Hawaiian hoary bat); Hawaiian monk seal on beaches
- **Reptiles:** Green sea turtles nesting on main island beaches (far more common than today)
- **Seabirds:** Huge colonies of shearwaters, petrels, albatrosses, boobies and terns on the main islands, not just offshore islets
- **Freshwater:** ʻOʻopu (native gobies), ʻōpae (native shrimp), hīhīwai (stream snails)
- **Land snails:** Hundreds of species of tree snails (*Achatinella* and others)
- **Insects and spiders:** Native damselflies, crickets, moths, happy-face spiders
- **Forest birds:** Far more honeycreeper species surviving, plus native crows, thrushes and honeyeaters

## 5. Population Balance Without People

- Nēnē and koloa live near carrying capacity on every main island.
- Eggs are safe; adult loss to raptors is the main control.
- Lowlands become the core habitat again, since people cleared lowlands first.
- Each island develops its own balance: Kauaʻi lacks eagles; the Big Island has ʻio and giant geese; Maui Nui has the richest waterfowl community.
- Populations swing with rainfall. Wet years produce booms; drought years produce busts.
- Eagles stabilize moa-nalo numbers, which keeps forests from being overbrowsed.

## 6. Intelligent Bird Scenario

Assumes the birds gain intelligence roughly comparable to crows, parrots or higher: memory, problem solving, communication beyond simple calls, social planning.

### 6.1 Species Temperaments

| Species | Likely temperament if intelligent |
|---|---|
| Nēnē | Loyal, family-centered, organized. Lifelong pairs build clans with long memories. Natural leaders and diplomats. |
| Koloa | Cautious, independent, observant. Information gatherers and scouts. Trust slowly. |
| Laysan duck | Night-active. Natural night watch. Small, adaptable, practical. |
| Moa-nalo | Large, slow, calm. Strong but flightless; rely on allies for warning. Hold territory by size. |
| Giant geese | Proud, territorial, physically dominant among waterfowl. |
| Kauaʻi mole duck | Lives by touch and sound. Specialist knowledge of ground and soil. Detects vibration and approaching walkers. |
| Eagle | Apex power. Feared. Possibly the ruling class or an enemy force. |
| Harrier | Fast, aggressive raider. Hunts in forest. |
| Stilt-owls | Night hunters. Feared by small species. Possible assassins or night rulers. |
| ʻIo | Solitary, territorial, single-chick parent. Careful and long-planning. |
| Coots and stilts | Loud, aggressive defenders. Natural alarm systems and border guards. |
| Night herons | Opportunists. Distrusted by ducks. |

### 6.2 How Waterfowl Might Cooperate

- **Shared alarm network:** Nēnē sentinels on high ground, koloa in marshes, Laysan ducks at night, mole ducks feeling ground vibration. A single warning call system across species.
- **Division of habitat:** Moa-nalo browse forest; nēnē graze open ground; koloa work wetlands. Agreements on feeding grounds reduce conflict.
- **Escorts:** Flying nēnē guide or warn flightless moa-nalo and giant geese of approaching eagles.
- **Mobbing:** Groups of nēnē, coots and stilts gang up on harriers and herons near nests.
- **Nest guarding rotations:** Non-breeding adults guard nesting grounds in shifts.
- **Molt protection:** Flighted birds watch over molting birds during their flightless weeks.
- **Seed planting:** Nēnē already spread seeds. Intelligent nēnē could deliberately plant berry shrubs and grass near home ranges.
- **Wetland management:** Koloa clear plant growth or dam small channels to keep ponds open.
- **Knowledge trade:** Koloa trade scouting reports; mole ducks trade knowledge of underground water and soil; nēnē trade access to grazing land.
- **Inter-island relations:** Flighted species carry news and contact between islands. Flightless species stay isolated and develop distinct cultures.

### 6.3 Conflicts and Politics

- **Raptor pressure:** Eagles, harriers and stilt-owls as an outside threat that unites waterfowl.
- **Possible truces:** Raptors agree to hunt only the old, sick or other prey in exchange for not being mobbed. Similar to a tax or tribute system.
- **Food disputes:** Drought years force competition over grazing and wetlands.
- **Size hierarchy:** Moa-nalo and giant geese dominate through bulk; smaller species rely on numbers and speed.
- **Night versus day:** Day species (nēnē, koloa) and night species (Laysan duck, stilt-owls) living in different social worlds.
- **Distrust of herons:** Night herons eat ducklings; intelligent ducks treat them as criminals or outcasts.
- **Island cultures:** Kauaʻi without eagles develops a more relaxed society; Maui Nui under eagle threat develops a militarized one.

### 6.4 Reproduction Changes with Intelligence

- Smarter animals usually invest more in fewer young. Clutches likely shrink.
- Longer childhoods for teaching. Goslings stay with families for years instead of months.
- Learned knowledge (routes, threats, food sources) passed down through generations.
- Elders become valuable for memory, especially of droughts and eruptions.
- Nests placed in group-guarded areas instead of scattered hidden sites.

### 6.5 Responses to a Person Who Provides Food and Protection

**Nēnē**
- Already approach people readily for food in real life. Intelligent nēnē would form strong, loyal bonds.
- See the person as a clan ally or patron.
- Remember kindness for decades; tell offspring about the person.
- Would bring families to the person's land and defend that land.

**Koloa**
- Watch from cover for a long time before accepting.
- Treat the relationship as a trade: information and pest control in exchange for food and safety.
- Would never fully drop their guard.

**Laysan duck**
- Practical and quick to accept a reliable food source.
- Offer night watch in return.

**Moa-nalo and giant geese**
- Value protection most, since they cannot fly from eagles.
- Likely the most dependent and the most grateful.
- Could act as heavy guards or move brush and debris for the person.

**Kauaʻi mole duck**
- Would sense the person by footsteps first.
- Could serve as a ground sensor, warning of anyone approaching.

**Raptors**
- See the person as a rival predator or a powerful new force.
- Might test the person's strength first.
- Could seek alliance if the person controls food or territory.

**Wetland neighbors (coots, stilts)**
- Join quickly if their wetlands are protected.
- Loud and aggressive guards by nature.

### 6.6 If the Birds Protect a Person

- **Alarm system:** Multi-species warnings day and night.
- **Guard geese:** Ganders already defend nests aggressively. Intelligent ganders could guard a home or sleeping person.
- **Scouting:** Flighted birds survey terrain, report weather, find water and food.
- **Escorts:** Flocks travel with the person, watching sky and ground.
- **Night watch:** Laysan ducks and mole ducks cover the hours when the day species sleep.
- **Group defense:** Mobbing tactics turned against any threat to the person.

### 6.7 Risks and Complications of a Patron Relationship

- **Dependency:** Birds stop foraging and lose skills if fed too much.
- **Population boom:** Steady food and safety lift numbers past what the land can hold once the person is gone.
- **Favoritism:** Species that receive more food or protection may be resented by others.
- **Raptor backlash:** Eagles and owls may target the person or the birds under protection.
- **Obligation:** Birds may expect the person to settle disputes, judge conflicts or lead in wars.
- **Succession:** Clans may compete to be the person's closest ally.
- **Loss of wild balance:** Protection removes the raptor check, shifting the whole ecosystem.
- **Disease risk:** Crowding around a food source spreads illness faster.
