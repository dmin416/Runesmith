# Domesticating Koloa Maoli and Nēnē

Hawaii's two native waterfowl as domesticated birds in a fantasy setting: Koloa maoli for bug and snail control, eggs and meat; Nēnē for grazing, weeding, guarding and prized eggs.

## Koloa Maoli (Hawaiian Duck)

### Name and Origin
- **Name:** Koloa means "duck." Koloa maoli means "native duck," used to set it apart from introduced ducks.
- **Origin:** A distinct species that arose from ancient hybridization between the mallard and the Laysan duck.
- **Range:** Once found on every main island except Lānaʻi and Kahoʻolawe. Lives from sea level up to 3,300 m, most commonly between 150 and 1,220 m.

### Physical Description
- **Weight:** Females 447 to 475 g (about 1 lb). Males 517 to 693 g (about 1.1 to 1.5 lb). Roughly half the weight of a mallard.
- **Wingspan:** 210 to 238 mm wing length.
- **Coloring:** Both sexes are mottled brown with chevron patterns. Males have dark speckled chins and olive bills. Females are lighter with buff chins, buff eyebrows and a dark line through the eye. Females are camouflaged for nesting.
- **Male plumage:** Lacks the green head of a mallard drake.

### Diet
- **Omnivore:** Eats algae, aquatic insects and larvae, crayfish, snails and other mollusks, earthworms, grass seeds and wetland plants.
- **Recorded prey:** Bladder snails, ramshorn snails, mud snails, trumpet snails, dragonfly larvae and large insects.
- **Unusual prey:** Occasionally eats cane toad tadpoles.
- **Feeding style:** A dabbler, not a diver. Feeds in water less than 24 cm (about 9 inches) deep.
- **Crop impact:** Eats rice seed but not enough to hurt yields.

### Breeding and Young
- **Pairing:** Monogamous pairs form around November and last through the breeding season.
- **Courtship flight:** Three males fly straight up to about 30 m and circle. The female picks one, usually the strongest flier, and the other males keep displaying.
- **Season:** Breeds year-round with a peak from November to May.
- **Laying:** One egg per day until the clutch is complete. Clutches average about 6 in the wet season and 4.5 in the dry season, with a range of 2 to 10.
- **Nest:** A shallow bowl about 27 cm across, lined with grass and down, hidden in dense vegetation. Usually within about 25 m of water.
- **Incubation:** About 28 days. The female incubates alone and rarely leaves the nest.
- **Hatching success:** Typically only 3 to 4 eggs in a clutch hatch. Wild nest success runs under 40 percent, mostly lost to predators and flooding.
- **Ducklings:** Weigh about 26.5 g at hatch. Leave the nest the first day and feed themselves. Fly at about 9 weeks. Mature at about 1 year.

### Behavior
- **Temperament:** Very wary and highly vigilant. Usually in pairs, sometimes in loose groups of 5 to 15.
- **Voice:** Soft, higher-pitched quack than a mallard. Females quack louder than males. Quiet during the day and most vocal at dawn and dusk.
- **Flight:** Strong, willing fliers. Has been reported flying between islands.
- **Lifespan:** Unknown in the wild. Closely related ducks live up to about 26 years.

### Fighting Habits and Capability
- **Who fights:** Males defend nesting areas. Females defend the nest itself.
- **Methods:** Hissing, chasing, bill pinches and wing slaps. Courtship flights favor strong fliers, which points to males that can hold territory.
- **Capability:** Low. At about 1 to 1.5 lb, a Koloa can chase off another duck, a small bird or a curious child but cannot hurt an adult human or drive off a cat, dog, mongoose or rat.
- **Real defense:** Vigilance, camouflage (especially females) and flight. As adults they have few natural predators.
- **Fantasy use:** Better as an alarm than a guard. Their constant watchfulness and dawn and dusk calls make a flock an early warning system.

### Threats and Health
- **Nest predators:** Rats, mongooses, feral cats and dogs, night herons, cattle egrets, bullfrogs and largemouth bass.
- **Disease:** Avian botulism is a primary year-round threat to adults on Kauai. It breeds in warm, stagnant water, so domesticated flocks need clean, moving water.
- **Parasites:** Hosts avian malaria, tapeworms and feather lice.

### Domestication
- **Path:** Shares ancestry with the mallard (source of nearly every domestic duck), so domestication would follow the same route. Generations of breeding from the calmest birds produce larger, tamer, more productive strains.
- **Best role:** Snail and slug patrol in taro loʻi, wetland gardens and irrigated fields.
- **Egg potential:** Year-round breeding and one egg a day make a strong base for selecting high-output layers. Removing eggs as they are laid could push hens to keep laying.
- **Challenges:** Wariness and flight. Early keepers would clip wings or pinion birds and hand-raise ducklings from hatch.
- **Historical hook:** Common enough to be a regular food for Native Hawaiians. On Kauai, hunters went into remote regions to catch Koloa while they were flightless during molt. Hunted for sport until about 1939.
- **Lore:** Hawaiian ducks were said to guide the legendary blind warrior king Imaikalani, warning him of enemies and guiding his spears.

## Nēnē (Hawaiian Goose)

### Name and Origin
- **Name:** Comes from its soft call. Hawaii's state bird since 1957. The species name sandvicensis refers to the Sandwich Islands.
- **Origin:** Evolved from the Canada goose about 500,000 years ago.
- **Lost relatives:** The only survivor of six goose species once found in Hawaii. Extinct relatives include the giant Hawaiʻi goose and the nēnē-nui.
- **Population:** Once about 25,000. Fell to about 30 birds by the 1950s from hunting and introduced predators.

### Physical Description
- **Weight:** Females 1.5 to 2.6 kg (3.4 to 5.6 lb). Males 1.7 to 3.05 kg (3.7 to 6.7 lb), about 11 percent larger.
- **Length:** 53 to 71 cm (21 to 28 inches).
- **Coloring:** Black face, crown and hindneck. Cream cheeks. Buff neck with deep black furrows and diagonal stripes. Brown to gray body with white-tipped feathers. Black bill, legs and feet. Sexes look alike.
- **Lava feet:** Longer, stronger legs and much less webbing than other geese, built for walking and running across rough lava.
- **Wings:** About 16 percent smaller than a Canada goose's.

### Diet
- **Strict herbivore:** Leaves, grasses, flowers, berries and seeds. Over 30 native and introduced plants recorded.
- **Picky eater:** Eats the nutrient-rich base of grasses and only the succulent parts of plants it pulls up.
- **Forages on land only.**
- **Seed spreader:** Disperses seeds of the plants it eats.

### Breeding and Young
- **Pairing:** Lifelong monogamous pairs, usually formed at about one year old.
- **Courtship:** The male walks stiffly in front of the female, flashing the white under his tail.
- **Triumph ceremony:** Once accepted, the male aggressively pushes rivals away and calls loudly. The pair then call into each other's ears.
- **Mating:** Done on land, unlike most waterfowl. The pair dip heads and necks to the ground in growing sync before mating. Afterward the male raises his wings, pulls her head back and touches her nape with his bill. Both call together, she flaps and he struts.
- **Season:** August to April, the longest of any goose. Most eggs laid October to January. Not every pair lays every year.
- **Nest:** The female picks the site, usually near where she hatched. A shallow scrape under a bush or tree, lined with plants and down.
- **Clutch:** 1 to 5 large creamy-white eggs, averaging 3 to 4. May re-lay if a clutch is lost or removed.
- **Incubation:** 29 to 32 days. The female sits while the gander stands guard. She leaves about 4 hours a day to eat and rest.
- **Goslings:** Weigh about 90 g at hatch. Feed themselves the first day. Fledge at about 3 months. Stay with parents about a year. Mature at 2 to 3 years.

### Behavior
- **Daily life:** Active by day. Sleeps on the ground with feet tucked beneath.
- **Movement:** Walks and runs swiftly over lava. Weak flier but can fly island to island, usually taking off from land. Can swim.
- **Social structure:** Family units are the core. Flocks reach up to 30 birds. Nests may be as close as 45 m apart.
- **Dominance:** Ranked by family size. Large families outrank small ones, small families outrank pairs and pairs outrank single birds.
- **Voice:** Soft mewing or mooing, loud cackling when alarmed and goose trumpeting. Families murmur while foraging to keep spacing. Goslings have distinct pleasure, distress, sleepy and greeting calls. Calls grow louder near breeding season.
- **Senses:** Can see ultraviolet light.
- **Molt:** Flightless for 4 to 6 weeks after breeding, regaining flight at the same time as their goslings.
- **Clumsy flight:** Flies low and often collides with fences and vehicles.
- **Lifespan:** Up to 28 years in the wild and 42 in captivity.

### Fighting Habits and Capability
- **Who fights:** Ganders defend the territory around the nest and family against other Nēnē and predators. Females sometimes join in.
- **Bent-neck threat:** The most common display. Head and neck point down at the rival while neck feathers vibrate.
- **Forward threat:** The most intense display. Neck feathers stand up, head and neck rise toward the opponent, bill open, calling loudly.
- **Charge:** Displays often lead to one bird charging the other. The lower-ranked bird usually runs.
- **Physical fights:** Rare. The fighter grabs the opponent's neck in its bill, pulls and beats it with its wings.
- **Against predators:** Uses the same threat displays to scare predators off. Against hawks and owls, Nēnē call alarms, huddle together and spread their wings, or fly. Goslings hide behind their parents.
- **Capability:** Moderate. A 2 to 3 kg gander's wing strikes and neck grabs can bruise a human and drive off rats, mongooses and many cats. Dogs and pigs are beyond it.
- **Fantasy use:** A real guard animal. A breeding pair defends ground, raises alarms and intimidates intruders, with ganders at their most dangerous during the August to April breeding season.

### Threats
- **Predators:** Barn owls, short-eared owls, Hawaiian hawks, peregrine falcons, Polynesian and roof rats, feral pigs, mongooses, dogs and cats. Mongooses taking eggs are the biggest threat.
- **Mortality:** Females die more often during breeding season from exposure, stress and predators. In captivity, most deaths come from parasites and disease.

### Domestication
- **Easiest of the two to tame:** Weak flight, a land-based lifestyle and a long captive-breeding record (programs since 1949, plus Sir Peter Scott's success at Slimbridge in England) all point that way.
- **Best roles:** Pasture grazing, orchard and field-edge weeding plus guarding.
- **Not a bug eater:** Pest control belongs to the Koloa.
- **Eggs as a luxury:** Small clutches of large eggs, with possible re-laying after removal. Fits a prized or ceremonial food rather than a staple.
- **Breeding hook:** Lifelong pairing means keepers breed by pairs, not harems. A widowed goose or broken pair can serve as a story beat. Dominance by family size could mirror human clan politics.
- **Historical hook:** Early Hawaiians hunted Nēnē heavily for food. A hunting ban came in 1907. A culture that domesticated them offers a counterpoint to that history.

## Raising Them

### Taming
1. **Start young.** Hatch eggs under a broody hen or by hand. Birds handled daily from hatch bond to the keeper and follow them.
2. **Brood them.** Keep hatchlings warm and dry until feathered. Hawaii's lowland climate often needs only a little added warmth at night. Nēnē nesting at high elevation face cold exposure, so upland flocks need more shelter.
3. **Feed them.** Koloa ducklings need plenty of insects, worms and small snails. Nēnē goslings graze tender greens and grass seed.
4. **Water access.** Koloa need clean water deep enough to submerge the whole head. Nēnē need far less water than other geese.
5. **Train a routine.** A consistent call plus a treat teaches them to come in. Both herd easily with a long stick held out as an arm extension.

### Housing and Safety
- **Night shelter:** Both sleep on the ground, so a dry floor with grass or straw bedding works. Nēnē pairs want their own space during breeding season.
- **Predators:** Both nest on the ground. Lock birds up at night and keep rats and mongooses away from nests.
- **Water:** Keep Koloa water clean and moving to prevent botulism.

### In the Fields
- **Koloa:** Run them through flooded taro loʻi and shallow wetland gardens for snails, slugs and insect larvae. Keep them out of seedling beds.
- **Nēnē:** Graze them in pasture, orchards and field edges to keep grass and weeds down.
- **Best foraging times:** Morning, evening and after rain, when snails and slugs are active.

## Eggs

### Size
- **Koloa egg:** White, 49 to 60 mm long. Estimated 40 to 45 g, a little smaller than a large chicken egg.
- **Nēnē egg:** Creamy white and large. Estimated 130 to 145 g, about two and a half to three chicken eggs.

### Nutrition Notes
- **Per gram:** Nearly identical. Both run about 185 calories, 13 g protein and 13 g fat per 100 g, richer in fat, iron and B12 than chicken eggs.
- **Practical difference:** One Nēnē egg feeds about as well as three Koloa eggs. A Koloa egg is a single serving. A Nēnē egg is a meal.
- **Cholesterol:** Very high in both. A single Nēnē egg carries several days' worth by typical dietary guidelines.

### Taste
- **Koloa eggs:** Rich and creamy with a large yolk. A diet heavy in snails and insects gives deep-colored yolks. The best everyday egg.
- **Nēnē eggs:** Even richer and denser with a heavier flavor. Being grass-fed rather than insect-fed, the yolk is likely milder and paler than a Koloa's. Best for feasts and special occasions.
- **Diet matters:** Clean water plants, snails and insects make clean-tasting eggs. Stagnant water makes off flavors.
- **Cooking:** Both have firm whites that turn rubbery when overcooked, so cook gently.

### Yearly Output (Domesticated Estimate)
- **Koloa:** Wild hens lay one clutch a year. Bred and managed hens with eggs collected daily could plausibly reach dozens of eggs a year, rising over generations of selection.
- **Nēnē:** 3 to 5 eggs per clutch, with a second clutch possible if the first is taken. Expect well under a dozen a year per goose.

## Meat

### Yield (Estimated)
- **Koloa:** Live weight about 1 to 1.5 lb. Dresses out to roughly 10 to 16 oz. One bird is about one serving.
- **Nēnē:** Live weight about 3.4 to 6.7 lb. Dresses out to roughly 2 to 4.5 lb. One bird feeds a family.

### Nutrition per 100 g, Meat Only, Raw (Estimated)

Based on standard wild duck and goose meat. Both birds are lean, active foragers, so their meat runs lean like wild game.

| Nutrient | Koloa | Nēnē |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 123 | 161 |
| Protein | 19.9 g | 22.8 g |
| Fat | 4.3 g | 7.1 g |

### Notes
- **Flavor:** Both are dark-meat birds. Koloa should taste like lean wild duck. Nēnē, as a grass-fed grazer, should taste like lean wild goose with a milder, cleaner flavor than fish- or snail-fed waterfowl.
- **Timing:** Koloa were historically taken during the flightless molt. For Nēnē, surplus young ganders would supply the table while breeding pairs stay.
- **Plucking:** Waterfowl down is waterproof and harder to pluck than chicken feathers. Scald in hot water, then strip the down and pin feathers. Butchering between molts avoids a mess of pin feathers.

## Pairing Them Together

Koloa work the wet taro fields for snails and insects. Nēnē graze dry pasture and lava-edge land. Together they cover pest control and weeding. Koloa supply steady small eggs, small meat birds and an early warning system. Nēnē supply grazing, guarding and rare, large, high-value eggs.
