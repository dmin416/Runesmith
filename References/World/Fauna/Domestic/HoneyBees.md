## Part 1: Which Bees Make Honey

### True honey bees (genus *Apis*)
| Species | Common name | Range | Nesting | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Apis mellifera* | Western honey bee | Africa, Europe, Middle East; introduced worldwide | Cavities, multiple parallel combs | The bee of Hawaii and nearly all commercial beekeeping |
| *Apis cerana* | Eastern honey bee | South and East Asia | Cavities | Smaller colonies, strong defenses against hornets and mites |
| *Apis dorsata* | Giant honey bee | South and Southeast Asia | Single huge open comb on cliffs or tall trees | Extremely defensive, migratory |
| *Apis laboriosa* | Himalayan cliff bee | Himalayas | Open combs on cliffs at altitude | Largest honey bee, source of "mad honey" |
| *Apis florea* | Red dwarf honey bee | South Asia to Middle East | Single small open comb on branches | Tiny colonies |
| *Apis andreniformis* | Black dwarf honey bee | Southeast Asia | Open comb | Very small |
| *Apis koschevnikovi*, *A. nigrocincta*, *A. nuluensis* | Regional cavity nesters | Borneo, Sulawesi | Cavities | Close relatives of *A. cerana* |

### Other honey makers
- **Stingless bees (tribe Meliponini):** Over 500 species across the tropics. They store "pot honey" in wax and resin pots rather than hexagonal comb. Their honey is thinner, more sour and often fermented. They cannot sting but bite and some spray caustic secretions.
- **Bumblebees (*Bombus*):** Store small amounts of honey in wax pots for short-term use. Not harvested.
- **Honey wasps (*Brachygastra*, *Polybia*):** Some Latin American social wasps store true honey.

### Western honey bee subspecies and lines
- **Italian (*A. m. ligustica*):** Gentle, prolific, yellow, prone to robbing. Common in Hawaii.
- **Carniolan (*A. m. carnica*):** Gentle, gray, builds up fast, swarms readily.
- **Dark European (*A. m. mellifera*):** Hardy, cold tolerant, more defensive.
- **Caucasian (*A. m. caucasica*):** Very gentle, long tongue, heavy propolis use.
- **African (*A. m. scutellata*):** Highly defensive, swarms and absconds often, fast development. Its hybrids are "Africanized" bees. Not established in Hawaii.
- **Buckfast:** A bred hybrid line selected for gentleness and disease resistance.
- **Russian and VSH lines:** Bred for resistance to Varroa mites.

## Part 2: Anatomy

### Body plan
| Caste | Length | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| Worker | 12 to 15 mm | About 80 to 120 mg |
| Drone | 15 to 17 mm | About 200 to 250 mg |
| Queen | 18 to 22 mm | About 180 to 250 mg (mated, laying) |

### Head
- **Compound eyes:** About 6,900 facets (ommatidia) per eye in workers, about 8,600 in drones. Drone eyes are huge and meet at the top of the head for spotting queens in flight.
- **Ocelli:** Three simple eyes on top of the head. Measure light intensity and help with horizon stability and timing of flight.
- **Antennae:** Packed with thousands of sensory plates and hairs for smell, touch, taste, humidity, temperature, CO2 and sound. Drones have more olfactory plates to detect queen pheromone.
- **Mandibles:** Shape wax, chew, groom, carry debris and bite intruders.
- **Proboscis:** Retractable tongue about 6 to 7 mm long in workers. Laps nectar and water and passes food mouth-to-mouth.
- **Hypopharyngeal glands:** Produce royal jelly and the enzymes invertase and glucose oxidase. Large in nurse bees, shrink in foragers.
- **Mandibular glands:** In workers produce 10-HDA (a fatty acid in royal jelly) and the alarm chemical 2-heptanone. In queens produce queen mandibular pheromone.
- **Brain:** About 1 million neurons in roughly 1 cubic millimeter. Mushroom bodies handle learning and memory.

### Thorax
- **Wings:** Two pairs hooked together in flight by tiny hooks (hamuli). Wingbeat about 230 beats per second.
- **Flight muscles:** Indirect asynchronous muscles that deform the thorax rather than moving wings directly. These same muscles shiver to generate heat with the wings disengaged.
- **Legs:** Six legs, each specialized.
  - Front legs carry the antenna cleaner, a notch that combs debris off the antennae.
  - Middle legs pack pollen and help remove wax scales.
  - Hind legs carry the pollen basket (corbicula), a smooth concave surface fringed with hairs, plus a pollen press and comb.
- **Branched hairs:** Cover the body and trap pollen through both structure and electrostatic charge.

### Abdomen
- **Honey stomach (crop):** Holds about 40 mg of nectar, close to half the bee's body weight. A valve (proventriculus) lets the bee feed itself from the load or keep it separate for the colony.
- **Wax glands:** Four pairs on the underside of abdominal segments 4 through 7. Secrete liquid wax that hardens into flat scales.
- **Nasonov gland:** Near the tip of the abdomen. Releases a lemony orientation pheromone (geraniol, citral, nerolic acid) that guides nestmates home.
- **Sting:** A modified egg-laying tube.
  - Workers have barbed stings that lodge in thick mammal skin. The sting apparatus tears away with its venom sac and keeps pumping. The worker dies.
  - Queens have smooth stings used against rival queens and can sting repeatedly.
  - Drones have no sting.
- **Venom:** Melittin (about half the dry weight, destroys cell membranes and causes pain), apamin (neurotoxin), phospholipase A2 (the main allergen), hyaluronidase (spreads venom through tissue), plus histamine and dopamine. A worker carries about 0.1 to 0.15 mg of venom.
- **Fat body:** Stores protein, fat and glycogen. Produces vitellogenin, a protein linked to immunity, longevity and the switch from nursing to foraging.

### Internal systems
- **Circulation:** Open system. Hemolymph is pumped forward by a tubular heart running along the back.
- **Respiration:** Air enters through spiracles and travels through tubes (tracheae) and air sacs directly to tissues. No lungs. Abdominal pumping ventilates during flight.
- **Excretion:** Malpighian tubules filter waste. Bees hold feces for weeks if needed and defecate outside the hive on cleansing flights.

## Part 3: Castes, Genetics and Development

### Sex determination
Honey bees use haplodiploidy.
- Fertilized eggs (two sets of chromosomes) become females: workers or queens.
- Unfertilized eggs (one set) become males: drones.
- A single gene (csd) controls sex. Two different copies make a female. Two identical copies produce a sterile "diploid drone" that workers eat as a larva. Inbreeding increases these losses and produces spotty brood.
- Because a queen mates with many drones, workers in one colony belong to many half-sister "subfamilies." Full sisters share 75% of their genes. Half sisters share 25%.

### Developmental timeline (days from egg laying)
| Stage | Queen | Worker | Drone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Larva (open cell) | 5.5 | 6 | 6.5 |
| Capped (prepupa and pupa) | 7.5 | 12 | 14.5 |
| **Total to emergence** | **16** | **21** | **24** |

Larvae increase their weight about 1,500 times in under a week. Nurses visit each larva over 1,000 times during development.

### How a queen is made
Caste is set by diet, not genes.
- Queen larvae are fed copious royal jelly for their whole larval life inside a large vertical "queen cell."
- Worker larvae get rich jelly for about 3 days, then a diluted mix with more honey and pollen.
- Plant compounds in pollen and honey (such as p-coumaric acid) suppress ovary development in workers. Queens are spared them.
- Differences in diet change gene methylation and hormone levels (juvenile hormone, insulin signaling), switching on queen traits.
- Any female egg or young larva under about 3 days old can become a queen. This lets a colony replace a lost queen.

### The queen
- Emerges, kills rival queens (often by stinging them in their cells) and makes mating flights 5 to 14 days after emerging.
- Flies to drone congregation areas, specific aerial zones 10 to 40 meters up that drones return to year after year.
- Mates in flight with about 12 to 20 drones, sometimes more.
- Stores about 5 to 7 million sperm in her spermatheca for life. Never mates again.
- Lays up to 1,500 to 2,000 eggs per day at peak, more than her own body weight.
- Lives 2 to 5 years, occasionally longer. Same genes as a worker that lives 6 weeks.
- Her pheromones hold the colony together, suppress worker ovaries and prevent rival queen rearing.

### Drones
- Exist only to mate. Do not forage, build or defend.
- During mating the endophallus everts explosively and breaks off inside the queen. The drone dies within moments.
- Fed by workers. When food runs short, workers drag drones out and let them starve.
- A colony may hold several hundred to a few thousand drones in peak season.

### Workers and age polyethism
Worker tasks follow age, though the schedule is flexible and responds to colony needs.
| Approximate age (days) | Job |
|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Cleaning and polishing cells |
| 3 to 12 | Nursing larvae, feeding the queen (queen retinue) |
| 10 to 18 | Wax building, capping cells, receiving and processing nectar, packing pollen |
| 14 to 21 | Undertaking (removing dead bees), ventilating, guarding the entrance |
| 21+ | Foraging for nectar, pollen, water and propolis |

- Summer workers live about 5 to 7 weeks, mostly limited by wear from flight.
- "Winter bees" in cold climates live 4 to 6 months with swollen fat bodies.
- If a colony loses its foragers, nurses mature early. If it loses nurses, foragers can revert.
- **Laying workers:** In a colony left queenless without young larvae, some workers develop ovaries and lay unfertilized eggs. These produce only drones. The colony dies unless re-queened.
- **Cape honey bee exception:** *A. m. capensis* workers can lay eggs that develop into females without mating (thelytoky) and can act as social parasites in other colonies.

## Part 4: Colony Life

### Size and structure
- 10,000 to 80,000 workers depending on season, stock and forage.
- One queen. Hundreds to thousands of drones in season.
- A nest consists of parallel vertical sheets of comb spaced about 9 to 10 mm apart ("bee space").

### Comb
- Built from beeswax secreted by young workers who hang in festoons to warm their glands.
- Cells start round and become hexagonal as warm wax flows and bees shape the walls. The hexagon uses the least wax for the most storage.
- Worker cells about 5.2 to 5.4 mm across. Drone cells about 6.2 to 6.9 mm.
- Cells tilt slightly upward so liquid stays inside.
- Comb holds brood in the center, pollen in a ring around it and honey above and to the sides.
- Producing 1 kg of wax costs the colony roughly 6 to 8 kg of honey.
- Beeswax is mostly esters of fatty acids and long-chain alcohols. It melts around 62 to 64 °C.

### Climate control
- Brood nest held at 34 to 35 °C (93 to 95 °F), with variation often under 1 °C.
- **Heating:** Bees shiver their flight muscles. "Heater bees" crawl into empty cells among brood and warm the pupae from the side.
- **Cooling:** Bees fan at the entrance in organized lines. Water foragers bring water that is spread across comb and evaporated.
- **Humidity and CO2:** Regulated by fanning.
- Temperature during development affects adult bees. Pupae raised a fraction of a degree too cool become worse learners and dancers.

### Propolis
- Plant resin gathered from buds and wounds, mixed with wax.
- Seals cracks, narrows entrances, coats the cavity in an antimicrobial "propolis envelope," and embalms intruders too heavy to remove (such as mice).
- Contains flavonoids, phenolic acids and terpenes with antibacterial and antifungal properties.

### Reproduction by swarming
The colony itself reproduces by splitting.
1. Crowding and reduced queen pheromone trigger queen cell construction.
2. Workers slim the old queen down by reducing her feed so she can fly.
3. The old queen leaves with about half to two-thirds of the workers. They cluster on a branch.
4. Several hundred scout bees search for cavities. The preferred home is about 40 liters in volume, entrance about 10 to 15 cm2, high off the ground, facing south (in the northern hemisphere), dry.
5. Scouts advertise sites with dances. Weak sites lose support over time. Scouts head-butt rival dancers with "stop signals."
6. When about 20 to 30 scouts gather at one site at once (a quorum), they return and make piping signals that warm the swarm's flight muscles.
7. The swarm flies to the new home, guided by scouts streaking through the cloud.
8. Back home the first new virgin queen emerges, "pipes," kills rivals or leads further "afterswarms."

### Other colony events
- **Supersedure:** Workers quietly raise a replacement for a failing queen without swarming.
- **Emergency queen rearing:** After sudden queen loss, workers convert young worker larvae into queens.
- **Absconding:** The whole colony abandons the nest because of pests, heat or dearth. Common in African and tropical bees.
- **Robbing:** Strong colonies raid weak ones for honey during dearth. Can spread disease.
- **Drifting:** Bees enter the wrong hive, common in tight apiaries.

## Part 5: Foraging and Honey Production

### Foraging range and effort
- Most foraging happens within 1 to 3 km. Bees will travel 6 to 10 km or more when necessary.
- Flight speed about 25 km/h loaded, up to about 30 km/h unloaded.
- A forager makes roughly 10 to 15 trips per day, visiting 50 to 100 flowers per trip.
- Foragers are "flower constant": on one trip they usually stick to one species. This is why bees are effective pollinators.
- A worker's lifetime honey contribution is about 1/12 of a teaspoon.
- One pound of honey represents roughly 2 million flower visits and tens of thousands of flight miles across the colony.

### Nectar
- Sugar water from flowers, usually 10 to 70% sugar (mainly sucrose, glucose, fructose), plus amino acids, minerals and aromatics.
- Bees prefer richer nectar and recruit harder to it.
- Some plants add caffeine or nicotine to nectar in tiny doses, which strengthens bee memory of that flower.

### How nectar becomes honey
1. Forager sucks nectar into the crop. Enzymes begin working in flight.
2. At the hive the forager passes the load to receiver bees by trophallaxis (mouth-to-mouth).
3. Receivers repeatedly extend and retract droplets on their tongues to evaporate water and add enzymes.
   - **Invertase** splits sucrose into glucose and fructose.
   - **Glucose oxidase** converts some glucose into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide.
   - **Diastase (amylase)** breaks down starches.
4. Nectar is spread in thin layers in cells. Fanning bees drive evaporation.
5. At about 17 to 18% water the honey is ripe. Bees cap it with wax.

### Honey composition (typical)
| Component | Approximate share |
|---|---|
| Fructose | 38% |
| Glucose | 31% |
| Water | 17% |
| Maltose and other disaccharides | 7 to 9% |
| Higher sugars | 1 to 2% |
| Acids, minerals, proteins, enzymes, aromatics | 1 to 3% |

- pH about 3.5 to 4.5.
- Honey with high glucose crystallizes quickly. High fructose stays liquid longer.
- Color and flavor come from the floral source and minerals. Dark honeys are usually richer in antioxidants.

### Why honey doesn't spoil
- Low water activity (about 0.5 to 0.6) pulls water out of microbes by osmosis.
- High acidity.
- Hydrogen peroxide from glucose oxidase, activated when honey is diluted.
- Bee-derived antimicrobial peptides such as defensin-1.
- Plant compounds such as methylglyoxal (high in manuka honey).
- Edible honey has been found in Egyptian tombs thousands of years old.

### Honeydew honey
Made from the sugary excretions of sap-sucking insects (aphids, scale insects) rather than nectar. Dark, mineral-rich, slow to crystallize.

### Toxic and unusual honeys
- **"Mad honey":** From rhododendron nectar containing grayanotoxins. Causes dizziness, low blood pressure and hallucination. Produced especially by *A. laboriosa* in Nepal and in the Black Sea region.
- Honey from certain other toxic plants (oleander, some *Datura*, tutu in New Zealand) can poison humans while bees remain unharmed.

### Pollen
- The colony's protein, fat, vitamin and mineral source. Pollen ranges from about 15 to 30% protein depending on the plant.
- Foragers moisten pollen with nectar and pack it into their leg baskets as pellets.
- In the hive it is packed into cells and fermented by lactic acid bacteria and yeasts into "bee bread."
- A colony uses roughly 15 to 55 kg of pollen per year.
- Without pollen, nurses cannot make jelly and brood rearing stops.

### Water and propolis
- Water foragers collect water for cooling and diluting stored honey for larval food.
- Propolis foragers scrape resin and carry it home in leg baskets. Other bees must pull it off them.

### Annual colony budget (temperate, strong colony)
- Total nectar-derived honey used: roughly 60 to 100 kg per year for the colony's own needs.
- Surplus harvestable by beekeepers: typically 10 to 50 kg, sometimes far more in strong flows.

### Hive products
| Product | Source | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| Honey | Processed nectar | Food, medicine, wound dressing, mead |
| Beeswax | Wax glands | Candles, cosmetics, lost-wax casting, waterproofing, polish |
| Propolis | Collected resin | Antimicrobial tinctures, varnish |
| Royal jelly | Nurse glands | Supplements, larval food |
| Bee bread and pollen | Collected pollen | Nutrition |
| Venom | Sting glands | Allergy desensitization, research |
| Brood | Larvae and pupae | Food in many cultures |

## Part 6: Communication

### Dances
- **Waggle dance:** Performed on vertical comb in the dark.
  - The bee runs in a straight line while waggling her abdomen, then circles back and repeats.
  - The angle of the straight run relative to vertical equals the angle of the food source relative to the sun's direction.
  - Duration of the waggle run encodes distance. Roughly 1 second of waggling corresponds to about 750 m to 1 km, varying by population ("dialects").
  - Vigor and number of repetitions encode quality.
  - Bees correct the dance angle over time as the sun moves, even after hours in the dark hive.
  - Followers also learn the flower scent from the dancer's body and from food samples.
- **Round dance:** Used for sources close to the hive (under about 50 to 100 m). Conveys "food nearby" with less direction.
- **Tremble dance:** Signals that too few receivers are available to unload nectar. Recruits more processors.
- **Stop signal:** A brief head-butt and vibration. Used to discourage dancers advertising dangerous or overcrowded sites.
- **Shaking signal (vibration signal):** Activates sluggish bees and readies the colony for activity or swarming.
- **Piping, tooting and quacking:** Vibrational sounds made by queens and workers during swarming and queen emergence.

### Pheromones
| Pheromone | Source | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Queen mandibular pheromone (9-ODA and others) | Queen | Colony cohesion, suppresses worker ovaries, attracts drones, stabilizes swarms |
| Queen footprint pheromone | Queen's feet | Marks comb, inhibits queen cell building at the edges |
| Brood pheromone (ethyl oleate and esters) | Larvae | Stimulates foraging, signals feeding needs |
| E-β-ocimene | Young larvae | Signals hunger |
| Nasonov pheromone | Workers | Orientation, "come here" signal |
| Alarm pheromone (isopentyl acetate) | Sting chamber | Banana smell, triggers mass stinging and marks targets |
| 2-heptanone | Worker mandibles | Repels and anesthetizes small intruders such as wax moth larvae and mites |
| Ethyl oleate (forager) | Foragers | Slows nurse bees from becoming foragers, balancing labor |

### Trophallaxis
Mouth-to-mouth food sharing spreads nutrients, pheromones and information through the whole colony within hours.

## Part 7: Senses and Intelligence

### Vision
- Three color receptors peaking at ultraviolet (about 344 nm), blue (about 436 nm) and green (about 556 nm).
- Blind to red, which appears dark. Red flowers that bees visit usually reflect UV.
- See UV "nectar guides" on flowers invisible to humans.
- Flicker fusion about 200 Hz (human about 60 Hz). Bees see fast motion as distinct frames.
- Detect polarized light with a special dorsal rim of the eye. Can navigate with only a patch of blue sky.
- Use optic flow (image motion) to measure distance flown, an internal odometer.

### Smell and taste
- About 170 odorant receptor genes, far more than flies or mosquitoes.
- Can be trained to detect explosives, drugs and disease odors at parts-per-trillion levels.
- Taste receptors on antennae, mouthparts and feet. Relatively few taste receptor genes.

### Other senses
- **Magnetoreception:** Magnetic particles in the abdomen. Bees can learn magnetic cues and align comb with magnetic fields.
- **Electric field detection:** Flying bees build up positive charge. Flowers carry negative charge. Bees sense floral electric fields through hair deflection and learn them. A visit changes a flower's field, telling later bees the flower was recently emptied.
- **Vibration and sound:** The subgenual organ in the legs detects comb vibrations. Johnston's organ in the antennae detects airborne near-field sound from dancers' wings.
- **Temperature, humidity and CO2:** Sensors on antennae track nest conditions.
- **Time sense:** Strong circadian clock. Bees learn which flowers open at which time and arrive accordingly.

### Cognition
- Learn colors, shapes, scents and routes quickly. Remember them for days to weeks.
- Learn abstract rules such as "same versus different" and "above versus below."
- Count small quantities (up to about 4 or 5) and have shown an understanding of zero as smaller than one.
- Solve mazes, learn landmark sequences and build map-like spatial memories.
- Sleep. Sleep loss reduces dance precision.
- Can be trained in laboratory tasks with sugar rewards (proboscis extension conditioning).
- Bumblebee relatives have shown string pulling, ball rolling and play-like behavior.

## Part 8: The Superorganism

The colony behaves like a single organism made of cells (bees).
- **Distributed decision-making:** No bee knows the whole picture. Choices emerge from local interactions, as in nest selection.
- **Division of labor:** Workers switch roles based on stimuli and need.
- **Homeostasis:** Temperature, food stores and labor balance are controlled like body systems.
- **Social immunity:**
  - Hygienic bees detect and remove diseased or mite-infested brood.
  - Allogrooming removes parasites from nestmates.
  - "Social fever": the colony raises brood temperature to kill heat-sensitive chalkbrood fungus.
  - Propolis coats the nest interior.
  - Sick bees often leave the hive to die alone.
  - Undertaker bees carry corpses away.
- **Reproduction:** The colony, not the individual, reproduces by swarming.

## Part 9: Defense

- **Guard bees** inspect arrivals at the entrance by scent. Each colony has a distinct odor partly from comb wax.
- **Stinging and alarm:** A sting releases alarm pheromone that marks the target and recruits more stingers.
- **Heat-balling:** Eastern honey bees (and to a lesser degree western bees) engulf an attacking hornet in a ball of hundreds of bees and vibrate their flight muscles to about 46 °C plus high CO2, killing the hornet while staying just below their own lethal limit.
- **Shimmering:** Giant honey bees ripple their exposed comb in waves to scare off wasps.
- **Imprisonment:** Some bees corral small hive beetles into propolis "prisons" and guard them.
- **Entrance narrowing:** Propolis walls make entrances easier to defend.
- **Africanized bees:** Respond faster, with more stingers, pursue intruders farther and stay agitated longer.

## Part 11: Honey Bees in Hawaii

### Native versus introduced
- Hawaii has no native honey bees. Native bees are the **yellow-faced bees (*Hylaeus*)**, about 63 endemic species that evolved from a single colonizing ancestor. They are solitary, nest in stems and wood and pollinate native plants such as naupaka, ohia and akoko. In 2016 seven Hawaiian *Hylaeus* species became the first bees listed as endangered in the United States.
- Western honey bees were introduced in the mid-1800s, with the first successful establishment around 1857. They spread across all major islands and now include large feral populations.
- Hawaii has long restricted importing live bees to keep out pests and Africanized genetics.

### Climate effects on bee life
- **No winter:** Colonies brood year-round. There is no long broodless period, which also means mites breed year-round.
- **Rain and wind drive nectar flows:** Windward slopes are wet and lush. Leeward slopes are dry. Heavy rain grounds foragers and dilutes nectar. Trade winds force bees to fly low and limit range on exposed sites.
- **Elevation zones:** From coastal dry forest to wet montane forest to high subalpine shrubland on Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa and Haleakala. Different flowers bloom at different heights, so flows move uphill and downhill through the year.
- **Humidity:** Ripening honey to 17 to 18% water takes more fanning in wet regions. Humidity favors small hive beetles and fungal diseases.
- **Vog:** Volcanic sulfur dioxide haze from Kilauea can stress plants and bees downwind.

### Major Hawaiian forage plants and honeys
| Plant | Status | Honey |
|---|---|---|
| Kiawe (*Prosopis pallida*) | Introduced, leeward coasts | Famous white, creamy, buttery honey that crystallizes quickly. Big Island and Molokai especially |
| Ohia lehua (*Metrosideros polymorpha*) | Native, the most common native tree | Light honey that crystallizes into fine white cream. Threatened by Rapid Ohia Death fungal disease |
| Macadamia | Introduced crop | Mild, nutty honey. Bees also pollinate the orchards |
| Coffee | Introduced crop | Distinct floral honey. Bees improve coffee fruit set in Kona and Kau |
| Christmas berry / wilelaiki (*Schinus terebinthifolius*) | Invasive | Dark, spicy honey in fall. Major flow |
| Eucalyptus | Introduced | Strong-flavored honey |
| Avocado | Introduced crop | Dark, molasses-like honey |
| Lychee, longan | Introduced crops | Fruity honey |
| Citrus | Introduced | Light, fragrant honey |
| Koa (*Acacia koa*) | Native | Pollen source |
| Mamane (*Sophora chrysophylla*) | Native, high elevation | Nectar and pollen |
| Haole koa (*Leucaena*) | Invasive | Pollen and nectar |
| Java plum, mango, coconut, albizia | Introduced | Seasonal nectar and pollen |

Hawaii honey is often sold by single source because flows are distinct and island climates separate them.

### Pests and diseases in Hawaii
- **Varroa mite:** Detected on Oahu in 2007 and on Hawaii Island in 2008. Before that the islands were a rare mite-free population. Kauai, Maui, Molokai and Lanai remained Varroa-free in later surveys, protected by interisland movement restrictions.
- **Varroa and viruses:** The arrival of Varroa in Hawaii became a landmark study. Researchers watched deformed wing virus go from a minor presence to dominating colonies as mites spread. The virus population narrowed to a few virulent strains.
- **Small hive beetle:** Detected on Hawaii Island in 2010 and later on Oahu. The warm, humid climate suits it.
- **Nosema ceranae:** Present.
- **Western yellowjacket (*Vespula pensylvanica*):** Invasive on Maui and Hawaii Island. Preys on bees and native insects and robs hives.
- **Invasive ants:** Little fire ant and big-headed ant attack hives.
- **Cane toads:** Sit at hive entrances at night and eat bees.
- **Wax moths:** Active year-round.
- **No hornets (*Vespa*), no Africanized bees, no Tropilaelaps:** Island isolation and import controls keep these out.

### The Hawaiian queen industry
Hawaii Island (especially the Kona side) is one of the world's major sources of commercially reared queens. Mild year-round weather allows queens to mate in months when mainland drones don't fly. Hawaiian queens are shipped to the US mainland and Canada for early-season colony building.

### Ecological tension
- Honey bees compete with native *Hylaeus* for nectar and pollen.
- Bees pollinate some invasive weeds, helping them spread.
- They also pollinate crops and some native plants. Their net effect on native ecosystems is debated.

## Part 12: Honey Bees in a Magical World

Every magical trait below is built on a real biological trait. Real bees already do most of the "magical" things fantasy imagines: they navigate by invisible light, read electric fields, vote, keep a precise fever and turn flower water into a food that never spoils. Magic makes these existing systems stronger, broader or able to handle mana.

### 12.1 Core principle: bees as mana refiners
Bees already concentrate nectar about four to five times into honey. In a magical world the same process can concentrate ambient or floral mana.
- **Nectar mana:** Flowers absorb mana from soil, sunlight, water or ley lines and release it into nectar.
- **Refinement:** Enzymes split and stabilize mana just as invertase splits sucrose. Fanning removes "impurity" alongside water.
- **Capping:** Wax caps seal the mana in. Capped mana honey stays potent indefinitely, matching honey's real shelf life.
- **Grades by purity:** Real honey is judged by water content. Magical honey can be graded by mana purity and moisture together.

### 12.6 Balancing and limits
- **Energy budget:** Real colonies spend most of their honey just staying alive. Magical colonies burn mana honey to power abilities. A hive that fights constantly starves.
- **Population limits:** One queen lays at most about 2,000 eggs a day. Magical colonies stay limited by her output unless multiple queens or enhanced queens exist.
- **Short worker lives:** Most enhanced bees still live weeks. Lifetime abilities matter less than colony continuity.
- **Toxic honey:** Real "mad honey" proves bees can make honey poisonous to humans while unharmed. Hives near cursed or volatile mana flowers produce dangerous honey.
- **Disease and parasites:** Magical mites, spirit infestations or rot can spread like Varroa and its viruses.
- **Nectar source limits:** Mana quality depends on the plants. Destroying a grove of mana flowers starves the hive.

## Part 14: Flowers and Forage

### 14.1 What makes a good bee flower
- **Colors bees favor:** Blue, violet, purple, yellow and white. Many carry ultraviolet "nectar guides" invisible to humans that point to the nectar.
- **Shape:** Open or shallow-tubed flowers with a landing platform. A worker's tongue reaches about 6 to 7 mm, so deeper tubes are out of reach.
- **Timing:** Open in daylight. Bees fly from roughly sunrise to sunset and rarely forage in heavy rain or strong wind.
- **Nectar:** Bees prefer nectar of about 30 to 50% sugar and abandon thin nectar when richer sources exist.
- **Scent:** Fresh, sweet or spicy scents. Bees memorize flower odors faster than colors.

### 14.2 Flowers bees can't use well
- **Red tubular bird flowers:** Bees see red as dark gray and the tubes are too long. Hawaii's native lobelioids (*Clermontia*, *Cyanea*) have curved tubes shaped to fit honeycreeper bills.
- **Night flowers:** Moth and bat flowers often close by morning. Bees catch some at dawn.
- **Wind-pollinated plants:** Grasses, corn and pines give pollen only, usually low in protein.
- **Deceptive flowers:** Plumeria produces scent and no nectar. It tricks sphinx moths.
- **Nectar robbing:** Bees steal nectar from deep flowers through holes chewed by carpenter bees, skipping pollination.

### 14.3 Pollen quality
- Protein runs from under 10% in some pines and corn to over 30% in some legumes and fruit trees.
- Single-source pollen diets weaken immunity. Bees raised on mixed pollen live longer and fight disease better.
- Sunflower pollen is low in some amino acids yet appears to suppress certain bee gut parasites.

### 14.9 Native Hawaiian yellow-faced bees in depth
- **Name:** *Nalo meli maoli* ("native honey bee" in Hawaiian, though they make no stored honey).
- **Species:** About 63 to 64 endemic *Hylaeus* species, descended from one ancestor. Roughly 10% of the world's yellow-faced bees.
- **Body:** Small, sparsely haired, often black with yellow face markings in males. No pollen baskets. They swallow pollen and carry it internally.
- **Nectar handling:** Concentrate nectar by holding a droplet between the mandibles to evaporate it.
- **Nests:** Hollow stems, beetle borings, rock crevices, sometimes soil.
- **Cuckoo bees:** Five Hawaiian species lay eggs in other *Hylaeus* nests. They are the only cuckoo bees in their entire family worldwide.
- **Specialists:** Some live only on alpine silversword flowers. Some rob nectar from native geranium (nohoanu) with long tongues.
- **Pollination role:** Dominated flower visits (64 to 91%) to native plant communities on Mauna Loa. In degraded coastal areas such as Kaena Point, non-native insects now make most visits.
- **Threats:** Honey bee competition, invasive ants, western yellowjackets, alien bees (including the invasive *Hylaeus strenuus*), habitat loss, fire and drought.
- **Status:** Seven species listed as endangered in 2016, the first bees protected in the United States.

## Part 15: The Tastiest Honeys

### 15.1 How honey flavor works
- **Source:** Each flower's nectar carries its own sugars, acids, amino acids and aromatic compounds.
- **Color grading (Pfund scale, USDA):** Water white, extra white, white, extra light amber, light amber, amber, dark amber. Darker honeys generally hold more minerals and antioxidants.
- **Flavor families:** Floral, fruity, warm (caramel, butter, toffee), woody, spicy, herbal, malty, mineral, bitter.
- **Crystallization:** High glucose honeys (kiawe, lehua, canola) crystallize fast. High fructose honeys (tupelo, black locust) stay liquid. Creamed honey is crystallized under control for a smooth texture.
- **Raw versus processed:** Packers often heat and blend honey into a uniform product. Raw honey keeps enzymes and the flower's character. Heat darkens honey and builds HMF, a breakdown compound used to judge freshness.
- **Moisture:** Above about 18% water, honey can ferment.

### 15.4 History of Hawaiian honey
- First bee colony arrived in **1857** from California after several failed attempts to ship hives around Cape Horn from New England. They were German black bees.
- First commercial honey shipments left Hawaii in **1894**.
- Ranchers spread bees to boost pollination of kiawe trees because kiawe beans were cattle feed.
- The **Sandwich Island Honey Company** once ran 10,000 colonies.
- Before 1920 bees collected huge amounts of honeydew from sugarcane leafhoppers. Biological control of the leafhopper ended that source.
- In **1918** honey exports jumped to over 2.4 million pounds as World War I raised prices.
- In **1930** the Molokai Ranch apiaries produced nearly 500,000 pounds and were reputed to be the largest honey producer in the world that year.
- In **1950** Niihau, the small private island, held 2,200 colonies and outproduced every other island.
- Italian bees gradually replaced the original black bees. In the 1950s black bees still dominated some Hawaii Island locations.

## Part 16: Poison and Strange Honeys

### 16.1 How honey becomes poisonous
- Plants make defensive chemicals. Some of these end up in nectar and pollen.
- Bees often tolerate the chemicals or the toxins get diluted across many flowers.
- When bees work one toxic plant heavily, the honey concentrates the toxin.
- Spring honey from rhododendron is more toxic than honey from other seasons.

| Tropane alkaloids | Datura, angel's trumpet (*Brugmansia*) | Mexico, tropics | Delirium, dilated pupils, dry mouth, racing heart, hallucination | Same family of toxins as belladonna |
| Gelsemine | Yellow jessamine (*Gelsemium*) | Southeastern US | Muscle weakness, breathing trouble | Also toxic to the bees themselves |
| Cardiac glycosides | Oleander, yellow oleander | Warm regions | Heart rhythm disturbances | Reports exist. Bees rarely make pure oleander honey |
| Euphorbia irritants | Spurge (*Euphorbia resinifera*) | Morocco | Burning, peppery throat | Sold locally as a medicinal honey |
| Botulism spores | *Clostridium botulinum* in any honey | Worldwide | Infant botulism | No honey for babies under 12 months |
| Wasp honey | *Brachygastra lecheguana* nests | Brazil | Poisoning reported after eating stored wasp honey | Wasp makers rather than bees |

### 16.3 Mad honey in history
- **401 BC:** Xenophon's Greek army of the Ten Thousand ate local honey near Trabzon on the Black Sea. Soldiers collapsed, vomited and lay delirious for a day before recovering.
- **60s BC:** During the Third Mithridatic War, Roman troops under Pompey ate mad honey left along their route by local allies of King Mithridates. The incapacitated soldiers were slaughtered.
- **Today:** Mad honey is still sold in Turkey and Nepal as a folk medicine, aphrodisiac and intoxicant. Gurung honey hunters in Nepal harvest it from giant cliff combs of *Apis laboriosa*, hanging from rope ladders.

### 16.4 Honey and nectar toxic to bees
- **Rhododendron nectar:** *R. ponticum* nectar kills honey bees and some solitary bees but does not harm bumblebees.
- **California buckeye:** Nectar and pollen kill honey bee brood.
- **Death camas, some *Astragalus* and yellow jessamine:** Poison foragers.
- **Caffeine and nicotine:** At natural nectar doses they improve bee memory rather than harm it.

### 16.5 Poison honey risks on the island
| Plant | Toxin type | Where | Risk |

## Part 17: Taming and Working with Bees

### 17.1 Can bees be tamed?
- Honey bees are **semi-domesticated**. Colonies can always swarm away and live wild.
- Until instrumental insemination was developed in the 1920s to 1940s, queens mated in open air with any drones nearby, so humans could not fully control breeding.
- Individual bees can't be tamed. Workers live only weeks. The **colony** is what humans manage.
- Temperament comes from genetics (mostly the queen's line and the drones she mated with) plus conditions inside and outside the hive.

### 17.2 History of human and bee relations
| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| About 9,000 years ago | Beeswax residue in Neolithic pottery in Anatolia shows early farmers used bee products |
| About 8,000 years ago | Rock painting at Cuevas de la Arana, Spain, shows a honey hunter climbing ropes to a wild nest |
| About 2400 BC | Egyptian sun temple of Nyuserre shows clay tube hives, smoke and honey processing |
| 10th to 9th century BC | Tel Rehov, Israel: oldest excavated apiary, rows of clay cylinder hives (an estimated 100 to 200), stocked with bees imported from Anatolia |
| Pre-Columbian era | Maya keep stingless bees (*Melipona beecheii*) in hollow logs. Honey is sacred |
| Middle Ages | Straw skeps. Colonies often killed with sulfur smoke to harvest honey |
| 1851 to 1852 | Lorenzo Langstroth defines "bee space" and patents the movable-frame hive. Modern beekeeping begins |
| 1857 | Wax comb foundation invented in Germany. Honey bees reach Hawaii |
| 1865 | Centrifugal honey extractor invented |
| 1870s | Bellows smoker developed by Moses Quinby |
| Early to late 1900s | Brother Adam breeds the gentle, disease-resistant Buckfast bee |
| 1920s to 1940s | Instrumental insemination of queens developed |
| 1956 to 1957 | African bees brought to Brazil. Twenty-six swarms escape and spread the Africanized bee across the Americas |
| 2006 | Los Alamos researchers train bees to detect explosives by smell |

### 17.3 How beekeepers calm bees
- **Smoke:**
  - Masks alarm pheromone so the alarm can't spread.
  - Triggers a fire response. Bees gorge on honey to prepare to flee. Full bees bend their abdomens less easily and sting less.
  - Must be cool and white. Hot smoke singes bees. Burlap, pine needles and dry grass make good fuel. Kiawe wood burns long but hot.
  - Too much smoke drives bees running across the combs and stresses the colony.
- **Timing:** Best on warm sunny days in late morning to midafternoon during a nectar flow, when foragers are out. Worst during dearth, before storms, at night, early morning or after robbing.
- **Position:** Stand beside the hive, never in front of the entrance flight path.
- **Movement:** Slow and smooth. Never swat or jerk frames. Never crush bees, because crushed bees release alarm pheromone.
- **Clothing:** Light colors and smooth cotton. Dark fuzzy clothing resembles bears and skunks. Bees react to wool, leather and animal odors.
- **Scent:** Avoid perfume, banana and heavy sweat. Banana smells like alarm pheromone (isopentyl acetate). Fear sweat seems to make bees more defensive.
- **Breath:** Bees react to exhaled CO2. Beekeepers avoid breathing into an open hive.
- **Sugar syrup spray:** Light mist keeps bees busy cleaning and feeding.
- **Light touch:** Many experienced keepers work bare-handed during strong flows because gloves make them clumsy.

### 17.4 Why colonies turn defensive
- Queenlessness
- Nectar dearth and robbing
- Thunderstorms and falling air pressure
- Mite and disease stress
- Night predators (skunks, toads, raccoons in other regions)
- Vibration from mowers or machinery
- Heat
- Genetics, especially Africanized or "hot" lines

### 17.5 Breeding for gentleness
- **Requeening:** Replacing a mean colony's queen changes the whole colony's temper in about 8 weeks as old workers die off.
- **Selection:** Breeders choose mother colonies for gentleness, honey production, low swarming, disease resistance, comb building and thrift with stores. Robbing tendency is bred out.
- **Race temperaments:** Carniolans are gentlest but swarm most. Italians are gentle and bright yellow, easy to find the queen. Caucasians are gentle but persistent stingers once roused.
- **First-generation crosses** between pure races are often productive. Later crosses can turn defensive.
- **Disease resistance:** In the 1950s some Molokai colonies showed natural resistance to American foulbrood. Modern breeders select for Varroa-sensitive hygiene.

### 17.6 Signs of trust between bees and keeper
- **Swarms are gentle:** Bees full of honey with no home to defend rarely sting. Swarms can be shaken into a box by hand.
- **Bee bearding:** Performers cage a queen under their chin. Thousands of workers cluster on the face following her pheromone. This shows how completely the queen's scent controls the colony.
- **Recognition:** Bees can learn individual human faces in laboratory tests. In practice bees mostly know a keeper by smell, routine and behavior. Many beekeepers report certain hives accepting them and rejecting strangers.

### 17.7 Other human and bee partnerships
- **Honeyguide birds:** In Africa the greater honeyguide leads human honey hunters to wild nests. Yao hunters in Mozambique call it with a special trill-grunt. The bird responds to that call more than to other sounds. Humans open the nest and the bird eats leftover wax.
- **Honey hunting:** Gurung (Nepal), Hadza (Tanzania) and Mbuti (Congo) peoples harvest wild nests.
- **Stingless bee keeping:** Maya Melipona keeping, Australian "sugarbag" bees, Brazilian meliponiculture.
- **Detection bees:** Trained to smell explosives, drugs and disease.
- **Bee venom therapy:** Folk and experimental use of stings.
- **"Telling the bees":** European folk custom of informing hives of births, deaths and marriages so the bees would not leave.

### 17.8 Beekeeping on the island
- Raise hives on stands with legs banded or soaked against ants.
- Lift hives high enough that cane toads can't sit at the entrance and eat bees.
- Turn entrances away from trade winds, roads and paths.
- Give partial afternoon shade and a clean water source.
- Move hives by elevation to follow flows.
- Leave honey until capped because humidity keeps it wet.
- Harvest early in the morning before fresh nectar comes in.
- Extract kiawe honey every 3 to 4 weeks before it sets solid in the comb.
- Follow interisland movement rules to protect Varroa-free islands.

### 17.9 Magical taming system
Real beekeeping already has every element of a bond system: scent, routine, queen control and earned trust. Magic formalizes it.

| Bond tier | Requirement | Hive response | Beekeeper ability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stranger | None | Guards challenge on approach | None |
| Tolerated | Calm visits with smoke rites over several weeks | Bees ignore the keeper | Open the hive safely |
| Accepted | Keeper's scent marked on the comb through wax offerings | Guards let the keeper pass without smoke | Harvest surplus honey without stings |
| Hive-friend | Survives a full generation of workers (6+ weeks) without harming the colony | Bees escort the keeper and warn of danger | Read basic dances, borrow guard bees |
| Hive-kin | Rears the colony's queen by hand from a grafted larva | Colony treats the keeper as part of its scent | Command a swarm cloud, share senses with scouts |
| Queen-bound | Swears a blood or honey oath to the queen | Queen pheromone extends to the keeper | Bee bearding at will, entire colony defends the keeper |
| Swarm-lord | Bonded to many related colonies across an island | Linked hives act as one network | Island-wide scouting, mass swarm attacks, honey economy control |

**Taming methods**
- **Smoke rites:** Incense smoke mixed with native woods (sandalwood, kiawe) masks the keeper as fire masks alarm pheromone.
- **Pheromone sigils:** Synthetic queen pheromone lures already exist and are sold to attract bees. Magical versions carved in wax act as command seals.
- **Honey offerings:** Returning part of a harvest as honey water builds trust.
- **Queen midwifery:** Grafting the larva that becomes a queen ties the keeper to every bee she produces.
- **Island custom:** Offering ohelo berries to Pele before taking lehua honey from volcanic forests.

**What breaks a bond**
- Crushing bees, especially the queen
- Taking more honey than surplus
- Bringing disease or mites into the hive
- Abandoning the hive during a raid
- Sweating fear or rage near the comb

## Part 18: Bee Intelligence in Depth

### 18.1 Brain hardware
- Weighs under 1 mg, about the size of a sesame seed.
- Fewer than 1 million neurons, about 0.01% of a human brain.
- **Optic lobes:** The largest share of neurons. Process color, motion and pattern.
- **Antennal lobes:** About 160 processing units (glomeruli) for scent.
- **Mushroom bodies:** Hundreds of thousands of densely packed neurons for learning, memory and combining senses. They grow larger as bees begin foraging.
- **Central complex:** Sky compass and steering.
- **Reward chemistry:** Octopamine signals reward. Dopamine signals punishment.
- Modeling shows advanced abilities can emerge from very small neural circuits.

### 18.2 Learning and memory
- **One-trial learning:** A bee can learn a scent paired with sugar in one exposure.
- **Memory stages:** Short-term (seconds to minutes), mid-term (hours), long-term (days to lifetime).
- **Sleep:** Bees sleep and consolidate memories during sleep. Sleep-deprived bees dance less precisely.
- **Time-place learning:** Bees learn which flowers yield nectar at which hour and show up on schedule.
- **Latent learning:** Orientation flights map the landscape before foraging begins.
- **Landmark counting:** Bees count up to about four landmarks along a route to find a feeder.
- **Individual differences:** Learning ability varies widely from bee to bee.

### 18.3 Abstract concepts
- **Same and different:** Bees learned to choose the matching or non-matching color, then applied the rule to scents and patterns they had never been trained on (Giurfa, 2001).
- **Above and below, left and right:** Learned as relationships, not specific images.
- **Numbers:** Discriminate quantities up to about 4 or 5.
- **Zero:** Placed an empty set below one in a number ordering task (Howard, 2018).
- **Arithmetic:** Learned to add or subtract one using color as the instruction (Howard, 2019).
- **Symbols:** Learned to match symbols to quantities.
- **Odd and even:** Learned to sort quantities into categories.

### 18.4 Face recognition
- Bees trained with sugar learned to pick a target human face from similar faces at over 80% accuracy (Dyer, 2005).
- One bee scored about 94% on the first day and 79% two days later.
- Bees recognized a face from a new angle after learning two other views, by blending the stored images.
- Bees and wasps use holistic face processing, combining features into a whole as humans do.
- To bees, faces are probably strange flower-like patterns. The ability shows a tiny brain can master tasks it never evolved for.

### 18.5 Self-monitoring
Bees given the option to skip a choice avoided difficult tasks more than easy ones, improving their success rate. This may reflect a simple form of uncertainty monitoring, though its interpretation is debated.

### 18.6 Emotion-like states
- **Pessimism:** Honey bees shaken violently to simulate a predator attack judged ambiguous scents more negatively afterward.
- **Optimism:** Bumblebees given an unexpected sugar treat approached ambiguous signals faster and recovered faster from a simulated attack.
- **Play:** Bumblebees roll small balls repeatedly with no reward. Young bees do it more.

### 18.7 Social learning and culture
- **Learning the dance:** Young honey bees that never watched experienced dancers made more direction errors and coded distance poorly. Bees learn part of the dance from elders.
- **Dialects:** Different honey bee populations code distance differently in the waggle dance.
- **Puzzle solving:** Bumblebees learned to open a two-step puzzle box only by watching trained demonstrators. They could not solve it alone. This is a form of culture.
- **String pulling:** A string-pulling technique spread through bumblebee groups by observation.

### 18.8 Tool use
Asian honey bees collect animal dung and smear spots of it around their hive entrances. This repels giant hornets that try to chew into the nest. It is the first documented tool use in honey bees.

### 18.9 Navigation
- **Path integration:** Bees track direction and distance flown to calculate a straight route home.
- **Compasses:** Sun, polarized sky light and magnetic fields.
- **Odometer:** Distance measured by image motion across the eyes.
- **Map-like memory:** Radar-tracked bees released in unexpected places flew novel shortcuts home, suggesting a mental map.

### 18.10 Personality
- Some bees are novelty seekers that scout new food or nest sites. Their brains show different gene activity tied to reward chemistry.
- A minority of "elite" foragers bring in a large share of a colony's food.

### 18.11 Collective intelligence
- **Nest choice:** Scouts compete with dances and head-butt rival dancers with stop signals. This works like competing neuron groups inhibiting each other in a brain.
- **Speed versus accuracy:** Colonies adjust quorum thresholds to decide fast or carefully.
- **Foraging allocation:** The colony shifts workers to the best flower patches faster than any single bee could judge.
- **Superorganism brain:** Each bee acts like a neuron. The colony thinks.

### 18.12 Limits
- Working memory is short and small.
- Complex rules take dozens of trials to learn.
- Easily fooled by visual tricks.
- Strongly driven by scent and reward.
- Individual bees don't understand the purpose of what they learn in experiments.

### 18.13 Magical intelligence scale
| Tier | Description | Abilities |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Instinct | Ordinary bees | Dances, scent memory, face learning with training |
| 2. Clever | Mana-fed bees with enlarged mushroom bodies | Learn human gestures, recognize individuals instantly, count to dozens |
| 3. Aware | Colony-level awareness | Understand simple speech, trade honey for protection, hold grudges and alliances |
| 4. Sapient hive | Colony mind with personality | Negotiates, plans raids and defenses, teaches dances to other species |
| 5. Ascended hive | Island-spanning network of linked colonies | Remembers across generations through scent-encoded "memory comb," predicts volcanic eruptions and storms, acts as a spirit of the land |

**Island-specific minds**
- **Lehua hives** carry long memories of lava flows and forest regrowth.
- **Kiawe hives** are practical traders tied to coastal villages.
- **Spirit Hylaeus** are ancient, solitary and wise, speaking for native plants rather than any colony.
- **Feral black bee lines** descended from the 1857 German bees are proud, defensive and old-blooded.
