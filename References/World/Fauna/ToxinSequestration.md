# How Animals Become Poisonous From Their Diet

Earth research on **sequestration**: an animal eats a toxic plant, animal or microbe and keeps the toxin in its own body for defense. Different from animals that make their own toxins (most snakes, bees, ladybirds) and from accidental buildup (mercury in tuna).

Story use: monster / companion toxin feel, Poison Resistance training, why diet changes toxicity (captive dart frogs, farmed puffer). Live poison skill: `../../../Progression/SkillsDesign.md`. Ned / Needle Worm toxins: `Species/NeedleWorm.md`, `../../People/Ned.md`. Food spoil / never-eat list: `../../Food/Food.md`. Not a lock on Terra species.

---

## Six problems in order

1. Survive the toxin
2. Absorb it intact instead of destroying it
3. Move it through the body without self-poisoning
4. Optionally modify it into a safer or stronger form
5. Store it somewhere useful
6. Deploy it against predators (and sometimes pass it to offspring)

---

## 1. Resistance: surviving the toxin

Most toxins work by jamming a specific protein. Sequestering animals usually carry mutations in that exact protein so the toxin can no longer bind. This is called **target-site insensitivity**.

| Animal | Toxin | Target | Resistance mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monarch butterfly | Cardenolides (milkweed) | Sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) | Three amino acid swaps (positions 111, 119 and 122) block binding. Fruit flies gene-edited with the same three swaps became resistant to milkweed toxins. |
| Large milkweed bug | Cardenolides | Na+/K+-ATPase | Multiple copies of the pump gene, some resistant versions used in specific tissues |
| Oleander aphid | Cardenolides | Na+/K+-ATPase | Altered pump |
| Pufferfish | Tetrodotoxin (TTX) | Voltage-gated sodium channels | Channel mutations that TTX cannot plug |
| Common garter snake | TTX (from newts) | Sodium channels in muscle | Same type of channel mutations, evolved independently in populations that eat toxic newts |
| Phantasmal poison frog | Epibatidine | Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors | Receptor mutation that blocks the toxin while keeping the receptor working |
| Poison dart frogs (many species) | Various alkaloids | Nerve channels and receptors | Assorted channel and receptor changes |
| Japanese keelback snake | Bufadienolides (from toads) | Na+/K+-ATPase | Resistant pump |

Not every species relies on mutations alone. Batrachotoxin carriers (golden poison frogs, pitohui birds) appear to rely heavily on toxin-binding proteins that soak up the toxin before it reaches nerves.

Without resistance the diet would kill the animal before any defense could develop. This is why sequestration usually evolves in animals that were already eating the plant and had evolved tolerance first.

---

## 2. Absorption: getting the toxin in intact

Most animals destroy plant toxins in the gut and liver using detox enzymes (cytochrome P450s, glutathione transferases and others). A sequestering species has to do the opposite for its chosen compound.

**Strategies:**

- **Reduced breakdown:** detox enzymes that would degrade the target toxin are less active or absent.
- **Dedicated transporters:** proteins in the gut wall actively pull the toxin into the blood.
- **Selective uptake:** animals often absorb some compounds from a plant while excreting others.

**Examples:**

- **Willow leaf beetle larvae (*Chrysomela*):** a specific ABC transporter protein moves plant glucosides out of the blood and into defensive glands on the back. Knocking out that transporter leaves the glands empty.
- **Monarch caterpillars:** absorb certain cardenolides from milkweed preferentially, favoring the ones that are more stable in storage.
- **Cinnabar moth caterpillars:** absorb pyrrolizidine alkaloids from ragwort while discarding much of the plant's other chemistry.
- **Poison dart frogs:** absorb alkaloids from ants, mites and beetles through the gut with remarkably little breakdown.

---

## 3. Transport: moving it safely

Once the toxin is in the blood, it can still poison the animal's own tissues. Several solutions exist.

### Binding proteins ("toxin sponges")

- **Poison frogs (*Oophaga sylvatica*):** an alkaloid-binding globulin in blood plasma grabs alkaloids and carries them.
- **Pufferfish:** a saxitoxin and tetrodotoxin binding protein (PSTBP) carries TTX in the blood.
- **American bullfrog:** carries saxiphilin, a protein that binds saxitoxin. The bullfrog is not toxic; the protein appears to function purely as protection.

### Barriers around sensitive tissue

- **Monarchs and milkweed insects:** a sheath around the nerve cord contains pumps that keep cardenolides away from nerve cells.
- Many insects use ABC transporter pumps to expel toxins from sensitive organs.

### Inactive transport forms

- **Rattlebox moth (*Utetheisa ornatrix*) and cinnabar moth:** convert pyrrolizidine alkaloids into **N-oxides**, a water-soluble, nontoxic form. The N-oxide circulates and gets stored safely. When a predator eats the moth, the predator's gut converts it back into the toxic form.

---

## 4. Modification: changing the toxin

Many animals chemically alter what they eat.

| Animal | Ingested compound | Change | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Some poison frogs (e.g. green-and-black poison frog) | Pumiliotoxin 251D | Enzyme adds an oxygen group, producing allopumiliotoxin 267A | About five times more toxic |
| Willow leaf beetle larvae | Salicin (willow) | Converted to salicylaldehyde | Volatile, foul-smelling repellent |
| Rattlebox moth | Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Converted to N-oxides for storage; males also convert some into a courtship pheromone (hydroxydanaidal) | Safe storage plus a signal advertising how well-defended the male is |
| Cinnabar moth | Ragwort alkaloids | N-oxide conversion | Safe storage |
| Monarch butterfly | Polar cardenolides | Modified into forms suited for storage | More stable reserves |
| Burnet moths (*Zygaena*) | Cyanogenic glucosides from bird's-foot trefoil | Sequester them and also synthesize the same compounds themselves | Can top up from diet or make them when the diet is poor |
| Heliconius butterflies | Cyanogenic compounds from passionflower | Sequester some, synthesize others | Flexible defense |

## 5. Storage: where the poison lives

### Skin glands

- **Poison dart frogs (Dendrobatidae, Central and South America):** granular glands in the skin hold alkaloids. Muscles around the glands squeeze them out when the frog is grabbed or stressed. Over 800 alkaloids have been identified across the family.
- **Golden poison frog (*Phyllobates terribilis*):** batrachotoxin in the skin, likely from small beetles. Wild individuals rank among the most toxic animals known. Captive-bred frogs fed crickets and fruit flies are essentially harmless.
- **Mantella frogs (Madagascar):** evolved the same strategy independently, eating ants and mites.
- **Red-bellied toads (*Melanophryniscus*, South America) and Australian corroboree frogs (*Pseudophryne*):** also sequester dietary alkaloids. Corroboree frogs additionally make some of their own.
- **Cuban frogs (*Eleutherodactylus iberia*):** a tiny frog that independently evolved alkaloid sequestration from mites.

### Specialized neck glands

- **Japanese keelback snake (*Rhabdophis tigrinus*):** eats toads and moves their bufadienolides into **nuchal glands** along the back of the neck. When threatened, it arches its neck toward the attacker so a bite ruptures the glands. Island populations with no toads have empty glands.
- **Firefly-eating keelbacks:** some related *Rhabdophis* species in China switched diets and fill their nuchal glands with bufadienolide-like compounds from fireflies instead.

### Liver and internal organs

- **Pufferfish:** TTX concentrates in the liver and ovaries, with lower amounts in skin and intestines. Muscle is usually low, which is why trained fugu chefs can serve the fish. The toxin originates with bacteria and moves up the food chain through small invertebrates. Farm-raised pufferfish on clean feed are nontoxic.
- **Common garter snake:** after eating rough-skinned newts, it holds TTX in its liver for weeks, making the snake itself toxic to birds and mammals that eat it.
- **Butter clam:** stores saxitoxin from toxic algae in its siphon and can remain dangerous for up to two years after a bloom.

### Wings, cuticle and blood (insects)

- **Monarch butterfly:** cardenolides concentrate in the wings, exoskeleton and hemolymph (insect blood). A bird that eats one often vomits and learns to avoid the orange-and-black pattern.
- **Queen butterfly:** related to the monarch with a similar system.
- **Pipevine swallowtail:** stores aristolochic acids from pipevine plants through caterpillar, pupa and adult stages.
- **Large milkweed bug:** stores cardenolide-rich fluid in special spaces along the sides of the thorax and abdomen. When squeezed, the fluid seeps out at weak points in the cuticle.
- **Cinnabar moth:** caterpillars and adults both retain ragwort alkaloids.

### Defensive glands and pouches (insects)

- **Willow leaf beetle larvae:** paired glands on the back fill with salicylaldehyde and get everted when the larva is disturbed.
- **Milkweed grasshopper (*Poekilocerus bufonius*, Africa and Middle East):** stores cardenolides from Calotropis plants in a poison gland and sprays the secretion at attackers.
- **Pine sawfly larvae (Diprionidae):** store pine resin in pouches off the foregut. When disturbed, a group of larvae rears up together and regurgitates sticky resin onto ants and spiders.

### Feathers and skin (birds)

- **Hooded pitohui and variable pitohui (New Guinea):** carry batrachotoxin, the same toxin as golden poison frogs. The source appears to be *Choresine* beetles. The toxin concentrates in skin and feathers. Locals call the hooded pitohui a "rubbish bird" because handling it causes numbness and burning.
- **Blue-capped ifrita (New Guinea):** a separate bird lineage that also carries batrachotoxin from the same beetle group.

### Flesh (birds, accidental-adjacent)

- **Spur-winged goose (Africa):** eats blister beetles and accumulates cantharidin in its flesh. Eating the goose can poison people.
- **Common bronzewing pigeon (Western Australia):** eats seeds of native *Gastrolobium* plants that contain fluoroacetate (the compound in Compound 1080). Native animals there evolved resistance; introduced cats and foxes that eat the pigeons can die.
- **Common quail:** people who eat migrating quail sometimes develop coturnism (muscle breakdown), linked to plant seeds the birds eat along migration routes.

### Stolen weapons (sea slugs)

- **Aeolid nudibranchs (e.g. *Flabellina*, *Glaucus atlanticus*):** eat hydroids, anemones or Portuguese man o' war. Unfired stinging cells (nematocysts) travel through branches of the gut into sacs (**cnidosacs**) at the tips of the finger-like cerata on the back. The slug sorts them, often keeping the most powerful types. When attacked, the cnidosacs release the stingers. These are called **kleptocnidae** ("stolen nettles"). *Glaucus atlanticus* (the blue dragon) can deliver a sting worse than the man o' war it ate.
- **Dorid nudibranchs (e.g. *Chromodoris*):** eat toxic sponges and store the sponge chemicals in **mantle dermal formations**, glandular pockets around the edge of the body.
- **Sea hares (*Aplysia*):** store halogenated compounds from red algae in the digestive gland and skin.
- **Sap-sucking sea slugs (*Elysia*, *Oxynoe*):** eat *Caulerpa* algae and keep caulerpenyne, which some convert into a stronger deterrent. Some *Elysia* species also keep the algae's chloroplasts working inside their own cells.

### Applied externally (mammals)

These animals do not store the toxin internally; they coat themselves with it.

- **African crested rat (*Lophiomys imhausi*):** chews bark of the *Acokanthera* tree, which contains ouabain (a cardiac toxin used on traditional arrow tips). It smears the toxic saliva onto specialized hairs along its flanks. Those hairs have a lattice structure that soaks up the liquid like a wick. Dogs that bite the rat can die.
- **European hedgehog:** chews on toad skin or other strong-tasting substances, foams at the mouth and spreads the froth over its spines (self-anointing). Toad toxins on the spines may add a defensive sting.

## 6. Deployment

| Method | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Passive | Predator tastes or eats the animal and gets sick | Monarchs, pufferfish, garter snakes, pitohuis |
| Gland secretion | Muscles squeeze toxin out on contact | Poison frogs, keelback neck glands |
| Spraying | Toxin ejected at a distance | Milkweed grasshopper |
| Regurgitation | Toxic gut contents expelled onto attacker | Pine sawfly larvae |
| Seepage | Fluid oozes from weak points in cuticle | Large milkweed bug |
| Stolen weapons | Captured stingers fire on contact | Aeolid nudibranchs, blue dragon |
| Coated surfaces | Toxin smeared on fur or spines | Crested rat, hedgehog |

Most sequestering animals pair the toxin with **warning coloration** (aposematism): bright reds, oranges, yellows or blues that predators learn to associate with a bad experience.

---

## 7. Passing toxins to offspring

- **Strawberry poison frog (*Oophaga pumilio*):** the mother carries tadpoles to small pools of water in plants, then returns to lay unfertilized "food eggs" loaded with alkaloids. Tadpoles raised on these eggs become toxic before they can eat anything on their own.
- **Japanese keelback snake:** toxic mothers put bufadienolides into their eggs. Hatchlings start life with filled neck glands before eating a single toad.
- **Rattlebox moth:** males transfer pyrrolizidine alkaloids to females inside the sperm package during mating. The female combines his supply with her own and coats her eggs with it, protecting them from ants and beetles. Females prefer males whose pheromone signals larger alkaloid reserves.
- **Monarch butterfly:** adults carry over cardenolides from the caterpillar stage. Eggs receive some as well.
- **Pufferfish:** females load TTX into their ovaries and eggs, which protects the eggs and may also act as a chemical attractant for males.

---

## 8. Costs and maintenance

Sequestration is not free.

- **Toxins leak and degrade.** Poison frogs slowly lose toxicity in captivity. Toxicity in the wild tracks local diet, so the same species can vary from deadly to nearly harmless across its range.
- **Diet dependence.** Monarchs raised on low-cardenolide milkweed species are much less toxic. Some wild monarchs are barely toxic at all yet still look identical, so they ride on the reputation of the toxic ones (**automimicry**).
- **Metabolic cost.** Transporters, binding proteins and storage tissue all cost energy to build and run.
- **Resistance trade-offs.** Mutations that block a toxin can make the target protein work less efficiently. Garter snakes with high TTX resistance crawl more slowly.
- **Storage limits.** Animals hit a ceiling on how much they can hold before self-harm or physiological strain sets in.

---

## 9. Accidental accumulation vs real sequestration

Some animals become toxic from diet without any defensive benefit. This is **bioaccumulation**, not adaptive sequestration.

- **Ciguatera reef fish (barracuda, grouper, snapper):** accumulate ciguatoxins from the dinoflagellate *Gambierdiscus* moving up the food chain. The fish gain nothing; people who eat them get sick.
- **Shellfish poisoning (mussels, clams, oysters):** filter-feeders concentrate saxitoxin and other algal toxins during red tides.
- **Mercury in tuna and swordfish:** builds up with age and food chain position.

The line between the two categories is not always sharp. The butter clam's two-year saxitoxin retention may deter some predators, for instance.

---

## 10. Convergent evolution

The same solutions keep evolving independently:

- **Batrachotoxin** shows up in Colombian frogs and New Guinean birds, likely from related beetle groups on opposite sides of the world.
- **Alkaloid-sequestering frogs** evolved separately in Central and South America, Madagascar, Australia and Cuba.
- **Cardenolide resistance** evolved separately in monarchs, milkweed bugs, milkweed beetles, aphids, grasshoppers, keelback snakes and some toad-eating lizards and mammals, often through mutations at the same few positions in the same protein.
- **TTX resistance** evolved separately in pufferfish, garter snakes and newts through matching sodium channel changes.

The recurring pattern: the cheapest way to become poisonous is to borrow chemistry from something else, as long as the animal can first evolve the resistance to survive it.

# The Monarch Caterpillar: How a Leaf-Eater Turns Poison Into Armor

The monarch butterfly (*Danaus plexippus*) is the best-studied example of an animal that becomes poisonous by eating poisonous leaves. Almost all of the work happens in the caterpillar stage. The adult butterfly barely eats anything toxic at all; it carries the poison it collected as a larva.

## 1. The food: milkweed

Monarch caterpillars eat only milkweed (genus *Asclepias* plus a few close relatives). Milkweed is one of the most heavily defended plants in North America. It fights herbivores on three fronts.

### Cardenolides (the poison)

- Steroid compounds also called **cardiac glycosides**, the same chemical family as digoxin from foxglove.
- They block the **sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase)**, a protein in the membrane of nearly every animal cell. The pump keeps sodium out and potassium in, which nerves and heart muscle depend on.
- In vertebrates, blocked pumps cause vomiting, irregular heartbeat and at high doses cardiac arrest.
- Concentrations vary enormously between milkweed species:

| Milkweed species | Common name | Cardenolide level |
|---|---|---|
| *Asclepias curassavica* | Tropical milkweed | Very high |
| *Asclepias syriaca* | Common milkweed | Moderate |
| *Asclepias tuberosa* | Butterfly weed | Low |
| *Asclepias incarnata* | Swamp milkweed | Low |

### Latex (the glue)

- Milkweed leaves are threaded with pressurized canals full of sticky white latex.
- When a caterpillar bites in, latex floods the wound. It contains cardenolides and gums up the caterpillar's mouthparts.
- Newly hatched caterpillars are tiny enough to get stuck and drown in it. A large share of first-stage caterpillars die this way.

### Trichomes (the fuzz)

- Fine hairs on the leaf surface that slow down small feeders.

## 2. Behavioral defenses: disarming the leaf

Before any biochemistry comes into play, the caterpillar physically defuses the plant.

**Newly hatched caterpillar (1st instar):**
1. Eats its own eggshell first (a nutrient-rich, non-toxic first meal).
2. Shaves off the leaf hairs in a small patch.
3. Chews a **circular trench** into the leaf surface. This severs the latex canals around the patch so pressure drains.
4. Eats the isolated center of the circle, which now has little latex.

**Larger caterpillars (later instars):**
1. Bite partway through the **midrib or leaf stalk**, cutting the main latex supply line.
2. Wait while the latex bleeds out at the cut.
3. Move past the cut and eat the depressurized leaf tip.

This is called **vein cutting** or **petiole notching**. It lowers the latex dose without blocking cardenolide intake from the leaf tissue itself.

## 3. Genetic resistance: a pump the poison can't grip

Monarchs don't avoid cardenolides. They let them in. The trick is a modified sodium-potassium pump.

### The three key mutations

Monarchs carry three amino acid substitutions in the alpha subunit of the pump, at the exact spot where cardenolides bind:

| Position | Change | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 111 | Glutamine → Valine (Q111V) | Partial resistance |
| 119 | Alanine → Serine (A119S) | Partial resistance; also stabilizes the protein |
| 122 | Asparagine → Histidine (N122H) | Strong resistance |

### How they evolved

- The substitutions appeared **step by step** across the milkweed butterfly family tree. Relatives of the monarch carry only one or two of them.
- Position 122 alone is harmful. On its own it disrupts nerve function. The earlier changes at 111 and 119 cushion that damage, which allowed 122 to arise later. The order mattered.

### The "monarch fly" experiment

In 2019, researchers used CRISPR to put the same three mutations into fruit flies:
- Normal fruit flies die on cardenolide-laced food.
- Flies with all three mutations survived like monarchs.
- These flies also **retained cardenolides into adulthood**, a basic form of sequestration.
- Flies with only 122 suffered seizures and paralysis, confirming the need for the earlier mutations.

Three amino acid changes were enough to turn a fly into a milkweed-tolerant insect.

## 4. Absorption: pulling the poison in

Most insects break cardenolides down or excrete them. The monarch caterpillar does the reverse for certain types.

- **Selective uptake:** monarchs preferentially absorb **polar** cardenolides (more water-soluble). These stay stable in the blood and are harder for the insect's own tissues to absorb accidentally.
- **Conversion:** less polar cardenolides get partly converted into more polar forms during or after absorption.
- **Discarding:** compounds that aren't worth keeping pass out in the frass (caterpillar droppings).

### Example: tropical milkweed chemistry

- *Asclepias curassavica* contains **uscharidin** and **voruscharin**, two extremely potent cardenolides.
- Monarch caterpillars convert them into **calotropin** and **calactin**, which they then store.
- Voruscharin is so toxic that caterpillars on high-voruscharin plants grow more slowly even with their resistant pump. Conversion appears to be partly a detox step.

## 5. Protecting the nervous system

Even a resistant pump has limits. Insect nerves get extra protection:
- The nerve cord sits inside a **sheath** (perineurium) that acts as a barrier between hemolymph and nerve tissue.
- In caterpillars generally, efflux pumps in that barrier push toxins back out.
- In monarchs, the resistant pump does much of the work, so the barrier is a second line of defense.

## 6. Storage: where the poison goes

### In the caterpillar

- **Hemolymph (insect blood):** the main circulating reservoir.
- **Integument (skin and cuticle):** cardenolides concentrate in the body wall, so a predator's first bite delivers a dose.

### Through metamorphosis

- Before pupating, the caterpillar empties its gut. Anything unabsorbed leaves with the frass.
- Stored cardenolides carry through the pupa into the adult. Some loss occurs during the transformation.

### In the adult butterfly

- **Wings:** among the highest concentrations by weight in the body.
- **Exoskeleton and abdomen wall:** high concentrations.
- **Thoracic flight muscle:** comparatively low.

This uneven distribution matters for predators (see section 8).

### How much gets stored

- Caterpillars on low-cardenolide milkweed store proportionally more of what's available.
- Caterpillars on very high-cardenolide milkweed hit a **ceiling**. Storage levels off while growth costs keep climbing.
- The result is a range of toxicity among wild monarchs, from heavily loaded to nearly empty.

## 7. Costs of being poisonous

| Cost | Detail |
|---|---|
| Slower growth | Caterpillars on the most toxic milkweeds grow slower and smaller |
| Latex mortality | Large losses among newly hatched caterpillars |
| Diet lock-in | Monarchs can't develop on anything but milkweed |
| Energy | Transport, conversion and storage all cost resources |
| Pump efficiency | Resistance mutations can reduce how well the pump works, requiring compensating changes |

## 8. Does it work? The predator side

### The blue jay experiments

Lincoln Brower's classic studies from the 1960s onward:
- Monarchs raised on **cabbage** (via a special diet strain) were eaten by blue jays with no problem.
- Monarchs raised on **milkweed** made jays vomit, often within minutes.
- After one bad experience, many jays refused to touch monarchs again. Some gagged just from seeing one.
- Brower measured toxicity in "emetic doses," the amount needed to make one jay vomit. Some wild monarchs carried enough to sicken several birds.

### The palatability spectrum

- Because toxicity depends on which milkweed a caterpillar ate, wild monarchs range from very toxic to nearly harmless.
- The harmless ones look identical, so they benefit from predators' learned avoidance. This is **automimicry**.

### Predators that cheat

At overwintering colonies in the mountains of central Mexico, millions of monarchs cluster in fir forests. Two birds eat them anyway:
- **Black-backed orioles** pick monarchs apart, eating the thoracic muscle and abdominal contents while avoiding the cuticle where cardenolides concentrate. They also favor monarchs that have lost toxicity over the winter.
- **Black-headed grosbeaks** eat whole abdomens and appear to tolerate the toxins better.
- Black-eared mice eat dying monarchs on the forest floor and seem to have some tolerance too.

The storage pattern (toxins in wings and cuticle, less in muscle) is exactly what the orioles exploit.

## 9. Warning colors

- **Caterpillar:** bold yellow, black and white bands with black filaments at each end.
- **Adult:** orange with black veins and white-spotted black borders.
- Both advertise the poison to predators that have learned the lesson.

### Mimics

- **Viceroy butterfly:** looks very similar to the monarch. It was long assumed to be a harmless mimic. Taste tests showed viceroys are distasteful too, from salicylic compounds in the willow leaves its caterpillars eat. Both species reinforce each other's warning (**Müllerian mimicry**).
- **Queen butterfly:** a close relative with similar coloring and the same milkweed-based defense.

## 10. Milkweed as medicine

Monarchs carry a protozoan parasite, *Ophryocystis elektroscirrha* (OE), which damages wings and shortens lifespan.
- Caterpillars that eat high-cardenolide milkweed end up with lower parasite loads as adults.
- **Infected females preferentially lay eggs on high-cardenolide milkweed**, effectively medicating their offspring before they hatch.

### The tropical milkweed problem

- *Asclepias curassavica* is popular in gardens in the southern US.
- Unlike native milkweeds, it doesn't die back in winter in warm areas.
- Monarchs that find it can keep breeding through winter instead of migrating, which builds up OE parasite levels in those local populations.
- Many conservation groups recommend cutting it back in fall or planting native species instead.

## 11. Following one cardenolide molecule

1. **In the leaf:** a cardenolide sits in milkweed tissue and latex.
2. **The bite:** the caterpillar cuts the leaf vein first, reducing latex flow, then chews the tissue.
3. **The gut:** the molecule reaches the midgut. If it's a polar type, it gets absorbed across the gut wall. If not, it may be converted or passed in frass.
4. **The blood:** it enters the hemolymph. Cells it touches carry resistant pumps, so it can't shut them down.
5. **The nerve barrier:** the sheath around the nerve cord keeps most of it away from nerve tissue.
6. **Storage:** it settles into hemolymph and the body wall.
7. **Metamorphosis:** it survives the pupal rebuild.
8. **The adult:** it ends up concentrated in the wings or exoskeleton.
9. **The payoff:** a bird bites the butterfly, the cardenolide hits the bird's unprotected sodium pumps, the bird vomits and never eats an orange-and-black butterfly again.

## 12. Other caterpillars using poison leaves

| Caterpillar | Plant | Toxin | Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Queen butterfly | Milkweed | Cardenolides | Same system as monarch |
| Cinnabar moth | Ragwort | Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Stores in nontoxic N-oxide form; predator's gut reactivates it |
| Rattlebox moth | Rattlebox (*Crotalaria*) | Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Stores alkaloids; adult males pass them to females during mating to protect eggs |
| Pipevine swallowtail | Pipevine (*Aristolochia*) | Aristolochic acids | Stores through all life stages |
| Heliconius butterflies | Passionflower | Cyanogenic compounds | Stores some, synthesizes others |
| Burnet moths | Bird's-foot trefoil | Cyanogenic glucosides | Stores and synthesizes the same compounds |
| Tobacco hornworm | Tobacco | Nicotine | Doesn't store it. Pumps nicotine into the blood and exhales it through breathing holes, repelling wolf spiders ("toxic halitosis") |
