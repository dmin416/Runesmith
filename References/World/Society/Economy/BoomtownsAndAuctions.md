## Part 1. Boomtowns

### The cycle

| Phase | Earth timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Strike** | Day 0 | A find. Locals rush first |
| **Rumor** | Weeks to months | Word spreads by mail, ships and travelers. Early birds get the best claims |
| **Rush** | First 6 to 18 months | Thousands arrive with too little. Prices spike. Tents and shacks. Crime and disease |
| **Peak** | Year 1 to 3 | Supply lines catch up. Real buildings. Banks, assay offices, courts, police |
| **Consolidation** | Year 2 to 5 | Easy finds run out. Capital-heavy companies take over. Wages replace prospecting |
| **Bust or settle** | Year 3 on | The town empties or becomes a lasting city on trade and services |

A new strike elsewhere drains a boom fast. Klondike crowds left for Nome in 1899.

### Earth booms by era

| Era | Boom | Find | Scale and notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medieval | **Freiberg**, Saxony (about 1168) | Silver | The first German silver rush. The margrave granted free mining: any newcomer could dig after paying a fee. Silver had to be sold to his mint. The town grew within about two decades. Output fell from the mid-1300s |
| Medieval | **Kutná Hora**, Bohemia (late 1200s) | Silver | Funded the Prague groschen coin. Royal mining code around 1300 |
| Medieval | **Jihlava**, Moravia (1200s) | Silver | Its mining law was copied across Central Europe |
| Renaissance | **Joachimsthal**, Bohemia (1516) | Silver | Its coin, the thaler, gave the word "dollar." A mountain village became a large town within about two decades |
| Renaissance | **Schwaz**, Tyrol (1400s to 1500s) | Silver and copper | Financed by the Fugger banking house. Mines as investments for great banks |
| Renaissance | **Potosí**, Andes (1545) | Silver | About 14,000 people in 1547, about 40,000 in 1571, at least 150,000 by 1600. A 1611 count gives about 160,000. Forced labor draft (mita) from 1572. Mercury refining revived output. The Spanish crown took a fifth |
| Victorian | **California** (1848 to 1855) | Gold | About 90,000 arrived in 1849 alone. San Francisco grew from about 200 people in 1846 to over 35,000 by 1852 |
| Victorian | **Victoria, Australia** (1851) | Gold | Ballarat and Bendigo. Licence fees led to the Eureka Stockade revolt in 1854 |
| Victorian | **Comstock Lode**, Nevada (1859) | Silver | Deep hard-rock mines. Capital and engineering won |
| Victorian | **Witwatersrand** (1886) | Gold | Founded Johannesburg. Deep reef needed companies from the start |
| Victorian | **Klondike**, Yukon (1896 to 1899) | Gold | About 100,000 set out. About 30,000 to 40,000 arrived. Dawson grew from about 500 people in 1896 to roughly 17,000 to 30,000 by summer 1898. Fires, high prices and epidemics |

### Who gets rich

Most miners do not. In California about half the gold seekers made a profit, mostly modest. The steady fortunes go to:

| Earner | Why it pays |
|---|---|
| **Suppliers** | Tools, food, clothing and gear sell at many times normal price. Sam Brannan bought up every pick, shovel and pan before announcing the strike. He became the rush's first millionaire. Levi Strauss arrived in 1853 to sell dry goods and work clothes |
| **Transport** | Ferries, ships, packers, mule trains, porters. Every pound has to come in |
| **Food and lodging** | Inns, boarding houses, bakeries, laundries |
| **Land and rent** | Early lots near the center multiply in value |
| **Money handlers** | Assayers, bankers, express couriers, money changers |
| **Lawyers and clerks** | Claim disputes and contracts |
| **Entertainment** | Saloons, gambling halls, theaters |
| **Crown and lord** | Royal share of the metal (a tenth in many medieval mining codes, a fifth at Potosí), mint monopolies, licence fees and taxes |
| **Big investors** | Banks and companies that fund deep work once the surface is gone (Fuggers, Comstock firms) |

### Prices

- **Spike.** Goods that have to be hauled in jump to many times normal. California 1849 accounts give eggs at $1 to $3 each, boots at $6 and a pan at $8 (normally about 20 cents). Sources disagree on the exact figures. Treat them as rough.
- **Wages rise too.** Labor is scarce while everyone digs. Cooks, carpenters and laundry workers earn far above normal.
- **Glut.** When supply lines catch up, prices crash. Goods get dumped. In 1849 hundreds of ships sat abandoned in San Francisco harbor after their crews ran for the hills.
- **Local money.** Gold dust passes as coin at a set rate. Scales, cheating and brass filings follow. An honest assayer is worth a fortune.

### Problems that always come

| Problem | Earth example | Fix that emerged |
|---|---|---|
| Starvation | Klondike threat of famine | Canadian police required a year of food, about a ton of goods, per person at the passes |
| Claim jumping | Every gold field | Camp mining codes: claim size limits, a claim must be worked or it lapses |
| Crime and lynch law | San Francisco vigilance committees (1851, 1856). Skagway under a con man's gang | A strong neutral police. The Mounties kept Dawson far calmer than Skagway |
| Fire | San Francisco burned several times in 1849 to 1851. Dawson burned too | Brick, wide streets, fire companies |
| Disease | Cholera, typhoid, scurvy | Clean water, latrines, fresh food |
| Tax revolt | Eureka Stockade | Fair, visible fees |

## Part 2. Auctions

### Types

| Type | How it runs | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **English (open outcry)** | Price rises until one bidder remains | Excitement, bidding wars | Rings can hold prices down |
| **Dutch (descending)** | Price falls until someone takes it | Very fast. Good for perishables (flowers, fish) | Less excitement |
| **Candle** | Bids run until a short candle burns out | Nobody knows exactly when it ends. Stops last-second sniping | Slow |
| **Sealed first-price** | Secret bids. Highest pays its bid | Private. Good for contracts | Bidders shade bids down |
| **Sealed second-price** | Highest wins, pays the second-highest bid | Bidders bid what it is worth to them | Hard to explain |
| **Silent** | Written bids on a sheet over a set time | Charity sales | Easy to watch and outbid |

### History

#### Roman and late antique

- **Sub hasta** ("under the spear"). A spear stood beside the sale, from selling war booty. A crier called the bids. A magistrate presided. The sale passed full ownership to the buyer.
- Estates, debts, war booty and slaves were sold this way.
- In AD 193 the Praetorian Guard auctioned the empire itself to Didius Julianus. He lasted about two months.

#### Medieval

- Public sales by a town crier: debtors' goods, dead men's estates, seized property, leases and tolls.
- Many local names for it: outcry, vendue, roup (Scotland), almoneda (Spain).
- Fish, wool, livestock and grain sold at markets by open bidding.
- Records are thinner than for later eras. Most medieval trade was fixed-price bargaining.

#### Renaissance and early modern

- **Candle auctions** in England. First recorded in House of Lords records in 1641. In 1660 Samuel Pepys recorded the Admiralty selling surplus ships "by an inch of candle."
- **Book auctions.** Catalogued sales in the Dutch Republic from about 1599. England's first book auction was in 1676.
- **Tulip mania.** On 5 February 1637 an orphans' auction at Alkmaar raised about 90,000 guilders for bulbs. The market collapsed within days.
- **Auction houses.** Stockholms Auktionsverk (1674), Vienna's Dorotheum (1707, from a pawnshop), Sotheby's (1744, books), Christie's (1766), Bonhams (1793).

#### Victorian

- Auctions sell nearly everything: tea, wool, tobacco, fish, horses, land, bankrupt stock and art.
- **Dutch clock auctions** for produce and flowers from the late 1800s.
- **Licences.** Britain's Auctioneers Act of 1845 put a duty on auctioneers' licences.
- **Rings ("knock-out").** Dealers agree not to bid against each other. One buys cheap. The ring then holds its own private auction and splits the difference. Sellers lose.
- **Puffing.** Fake bids for the seller to push the price up. British land-sale law of 1867 required sales to state whether the seller could bid.
- **Slave auctions** were a central horror of the Atlantic slave trade into the 1800s.

#### Modern note

The buyer's premium (a fee on the buyer as well as the seller) only began in London in 1975 at 10%. Before that the seller paid. Older sales charged the seller a commission on the hammer price.

### How a sale runs

1. **Consignment.** The seller hands goods over. The house checks them and agrees a reserve (lowest price) and fees.
2. **Appraisal.** Experts estimate a low and high value. Provenance (where it came from) raises trust.
3. **Catalog and viewing.** A printed list by lot number. Viewing days let buyers inspect.
4. **Order of lots.** Strong lots go in the middle once the room is warm. Weak lots go early or late.
5. **The sale.** The auctioneer sets the pace and the bid steps. A good auctioneer reads the room and keeps it moving.
6. **Hammer.** The lot is sold. Deposit at once. Full payment within days. Goods released on payment.
7. **Settlement.** The house takes its fees and pays the seller.

### Tricks and traps

| Trick | Who uses it | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| Ring or knock-out | Dealers | A reserve price. Bring in outside buyers |
| Puffing or fake bids | Seller or house | Rules that ban it or require disclosure |
| Bids "off the chandelier" | Auctioneer reaching a reserve | Same |
| Winner's curse | Every bidder | The winner is often the one who overestimated. Decide a limit in advance |
| Bidding fever | Rivals and prestige buyers | The seller's friend. Keep supply scarce |
| Flooding the market | Sellers | Release a few lots per sale |

### Why auctions suit new goods

When nothing like an item has ever sold, nobody knows its price. An auction finds the price by letting buyers compete. Fixed-price shops guess and usually guess low. This is Roland's case with comet-brand scrolls.

### Seller strategy for Roland

- **Scarcity.** A few lots per sale keep prices high.
- **Brand and provenance.** The comet mark is a provenance mark. Consistent quality makes it worth more each sale.
- **Reserve, never puff.** Set a reserve. Never bid on his own lots. Fake bidding is fraud and ruins trust.
- **Lot timing.** Put his items mid-sale.
- **Anonymity.** The house shields the seller's name. Useful for the Wayland cover.
- **Watch for rings.** A run of low hammers from the same few bidders is a sign.
- **Read the boom.** Sell into peak demand. Hold stock through a glut.
