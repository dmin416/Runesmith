# Paid Services of the Medieval and Early Modern Western Church (c. 1100–1600)

> **Earth design loot.** Not Caldris law. Use when pricing Solaria / church services against historical feel. Live prices: `Economy.md`. Fat economy: `EconomyDesign.md`.

Figures are mostly English, since English parish, probate and monastic records survive best.

**Currency and wage key (England, 15th century)**
- 12 pence (d) = 1 shilling (s); 20s = £1; 1 mark = 13s 4d
- Unskilled laborer: about 4d per day
- Skilled craftsman (mason, carpenter): about 6d per day
- A priest's living: about £5–£10 per year for an ordinary parish or chantry priest

## 1. Life-cycle sacraments and rites

Most sacraments were officially free since charging for them was simony. In practice "customary offerings" were expected and often enforced in church courts.

| Service | Typical payment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Baptism | Customary offering of a few pence plus the chrisom cloth or candle | Officially free |
| Churching of women (purification after childbirth) | About 1d–4d plus candle | Required before a mother returned to church |
| Marriage | Fee for banns plus offering at the nuptial mass, often a few pence to a shilling | Wealthier couples paid more |
| Marriage dispensation (cousins, affinity) | Shillings at bishop's level; several pounds through Rome once agents' fees were added | Papal Penitentiary handled thousands per year |
| Confession and Easter communion | "Easter dues" of about 2d per adult communicant | One of the most widespread fixed payments |
| Four offering days (Christmas, Easter, Whitsun, patronal feast) | ½d–1d per household or adult | Customary and enforceable |

## 2. Death and the afterlife (the largest service market)

| Service | Typical price | What was bought |
|---|---|---|
| Mortuary | The deceased's second-best beast (the lord took the best as heriot) or best garment | Compensation for tithes the dead person "forgot" to pay |
| Burial in churchyard | Often free or a few pence | Grave-digging and bell fees extra |
| Burial inside the church | Commonly 6s 8d (half a mark); more in the chancel | Prestige plus proximity to the altar |
| Knell or passing bell | Pence to a few shillings | Charged by bell and duration |
| Single requiem mass | About 4d–6d | Paid to the celebrating priest |
| Trental (30 masses) | Standard rate about 10s | Popular bequest in wills |
| Obit or anniversary | A few shillings per year, usually from an endowed rent | Annual mass with candles and alms |
| Perpetual chantry | Endowment producing £5–£7 per year for a priest's stipend | Required land or rents worth roughly £100–£200 capital plus a royal licence to alienate land |

By the 1540s England had roughly 2,000–3,000 chantries. Their seizure under Edward VI (1547) was a major Crown windfall.

## 3. Indulgences

The most notorious example is the St Peter's indulgence preached in Germany in 1517 by Johann Tetzel. Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz's instructions set a sliding scale in Rhenish gold gulden:

| Rank | Price |
|---|---|
| Kings, queens, princes, archbishops, bishops | 25 gulden |
| Abbots, counts, barons, great nobles | 10 gulden |
| Lesser prelates and nobles | 6 gulden |
| Burghers and merchants | 3 gulden |
| Artisans | 1 gulden |
| Poor | ½ gulden or free through prayer and fasting |

One gulden was roughly one to two weeks' pay for a German craftsman. Half the proceeds went to Rome for rebuilding St Peter's Basilica. The other half repaid the Fugger bank loans Albrecht had taken to cover papal fees for his archbishopric and a dispensation to hold multiple sees.

## 4. Pilgrimage and shrine offerings

| Shrine | Income |
|---|---|
| Thomas Becket, Canterbury | Over £1,000 in the 1220 translation year at its peak; about £36 a year by 1535 |
| Other major shrines (Walsingham, Hailes, Westminster) | Hundreds of pounds per year at peak |

Revenue also came from the sale of pilgrim badges, ampullae of holy water and candles. Jubilee years in Rome (1300, 1350 and later) brought pilgrims who left offerings at the basilicas in quantities chroniclers described as staggering.

## 5. Courts and legal services

Church courts held jurisdiction over wills, marriage, defamation and morals.

| Service | Fee |
|---|---|
| Probate of wills (England, capped by statute in 1529) | Free under £5; up to 3s 6d for estates of £5–£40; up to 5s above £40 |
| Pre-1529 probate | Often higher, which was a major lay grievance |
| Penance commutation | Public penance could be converted to a cash payment |
| Licences (to marry without banns, to eat meat in Lent, to preach) | Shillings to pounds |

English church courts kept probate jurisdiction until 1858.

## 6. Obligatory dues (not tied to a specific service)

| Due | Rate |
|---|---|
| Tithe | 10% of produce: great tithes (grain, hay, wood) and small tithes (livestock, wool, milk, eggs, garden produce) plus "personal tithes" on wages and trade profits |
| Peter's Pence (England to Rome) | Fixed at £199 6s 8d per year from the 13th century |
| Annates | First year's income of a newly filled benefice, paid to the papacy |
| Servitia | About one third of a year's revenue, paid to Rome by new bishops and abbots |
| Pallium fee | Thousands of ducats for an archbishop's confirmation |

## 7. Monastic services

| Service | Price |
|---|---|
| Corrody (lifetime board, lodging and clothing at a monastery, a medieval retirement plan) | Lump sum often £20–£50 or a land grant |
| Entry dowry for a nun or child oblate | Land or cash, often several pounds for gentry families |
| Confraternity membership (shared in the monks' prayers) | Gift or annual payment |
| Burial in a monastic church | Usually secured through an endowment |

**Scale:** the 1535 Valor Ecclesiasticus valued English monastic net income at about £136,000 a year. Crown ordinary revenue at the time was roughly £100,000.

## 8. Later developments (post-Reformation to modern)

| Practice | Details |
|---|---|
| Surplice fees (Church of England) | Fixed tables for weddings, burials and churchings continued into the 19th century |
| Pew rents | Common 17th–19th centuries; best pews rented for pounds per year |
| Catholic Mass stipends | Still exist; most U.S. dioceses set roughly $10–$25 per Mass |
| Indulgence sales | Banned by Pius V in 1567 |
