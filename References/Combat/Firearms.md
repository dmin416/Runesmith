## .50 Caliber Weapons for a Super-Strong User

Assumes gorilla strength and the durability of three or four men. A .50 caliber is period-appropriate, since the US Army's .50-70 Government cartridge dates to 1866.

### Copper vs Brass Bullets

- Both are lighter than lead for the same length, roughly 75 to 80 percent of the weight. They fly flatter but lose a little energy at long range.
- **Solid copper:** The modern standard for lead-free hunting bullets. Expands well and holds together.
- **Solid brass:** Harder and barely deforms. Penetrates deeper with less tissue damage. Harsh on rifling, which is irrelevant with an indestructible barrel.

### Rifle (.50 BMG Class)

| Stat | Value |
|---|---|
| Bullet | 650 grain solid copper |
| Velocity | 2,900 to 3,000 fps (3,200+ fps possible with indestructible barrel) |
| Energy | 12,000 to 13,000 ft-lbs (about 17,000 J) |
| Comparison | 4 to 5 times a .30-06; punches engine blocks and light armor plate |
| Recoil (15 lb rifle) | About 190 ft-lbs, roughly 9 times a .30-06 |

A normal man gets hurt by that recoil. The super-strong user handles it like a heavy hunting rifle.

### Pistol (.500 S&W Class)

| Stat | Value |
|---|---|
| Bullet | 350 grain solid copper |
| Velocity | About 1,900 fps |
| Energy | About 2,800 ft-lbs (about 3,800 J), beating many rifle cartridges |
| Recoil | Brutal for normal shooters, mild for the super-strong user |

### .50 BMG Pistol

Possible with indestructible metal. The short barrel drops velocity to roughly 2,000 fps with an enormous fireball and blast. Energy still lands around 5,000 to 6,000 ft-lbs.

### Victorian Comparison

The black powder .50-70 fired a 450 grain lead bullet at about 1,250 fps for roughly 1,500 ft-lbs. The smokeless .50 rifle has about 8 times that energy.

## .50 Caliber by Bullet Material

Same bullet length and powder charge in an indestructible-barrel rifle with double-base powder.

| Material | Weight | Velocity | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aluminum | 196 gr | ~4,600 fps | ~9,300 ft-lbs |
| Titanium | 326 gr | ~3,800 fps | ~10,600 ft-lbs |
| Steel | 570 gr | ~3,100 fps | ~12,200 ft-lbs |
| Brass | 617 gr | ~3,000 fps | ~12,400 ft-lbs |
| Copper | 650 gr | ~2,950 fps | ~12,600 ft-lbs |
| Silver | 762 gr | ~2,780 fps | ~13,100 ft-lbs |
| Lead | 823 gr | ~2,700 fps | ~13,300 ft-lbs |
| Tungsten carbide | 1,130 gr | ~2,400 fps | ~14,400 ft-lbs |
| Tungsten alloy | 1,306 gr | ~2,270 fps | ~15,000 ft-lbs |
| Gold | 1,400 gr | ~2,210 fps | ~15,200 ft-lbs |

Light bullets leave the barrel fastest but shed speed quickly. Heavy bullets keep their energy at range and penetrate deepest.

- **Lead:** Pure lead strips in the rifling above roughly 2,000 fps, so it needs a copper jacket or hardening with antimony or tin. It fragments on impact. Both eras can make it.
- **Steel:** Hard, armor-piercing and prone to ricochet and sparking. Bare steel seals gas poorly, so it usually gets a copper or brass driving band.
- **Silver:** Behaves like copper. Classic anti-monster material for fantasy settings.
- **Gold:** Soft like lead and heavy. A rich man's bullet with no real advantage over lead.
- **Tungsten:** The best practical penetrator. Tungsten was isolated in 1783. Victorians with full knowledge could produce alloy bullets. Medieval smiths cannot.

## Explosive Bullets

A .50 bullet can hold a few grams of explosive. Two grams of TNT releases about 8,000 to 9,000 J, roughly half the bullet's kinetic energy. That energy comes out as a small blast rather than penetration, similar to a firecracker going off inside the target. It shreds a fist-sized area and fragments the bullet.

Russia developed exploding rifle bullets in the 1860s. The St. Petersburg Declaration of 1868 banned explosive or incendiary projectiles under 400 grams, which fits a Victorian setting exactly. The modern Raufoss Mk 211 combines a small explosive charge, an incendiary and a tungsten penetrator in one .50 round.

## Copper Bullet Tiers: 10,000 to 50,000 ft-lbs

Assumptions: monolithic copper bullets, close range, flat armor plate. The adamantium needle is tungsten-dense, about one third of the caliber in diameter, four calibers long and about a quarter of the bullet's weight. If adamantium is only as dense as steel, cut the needle penetration figures by about 60 percent.

### The Bullets

| Tier | Caliber | Bullet | Velocity | Energy | Recoil (30 lb gun) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 ft-lbs | .50 (12.7mm) | 600 gr | 2,740 fps | 13,600 J | ~80 ft-lbs |
| 20,000 ft-lbs | 14.5mm | 900 gr | 3,170 fps | 27,100 J | ~220 ft-lbs |
| 30,000 ft-lbs | 20mm | 1,300 gr | 3,220 fps | 40,700 J | ~460 ft-lbs |
| 40,000 ft-lbs | 23mm | 1,700 gr | 3,260 fps | 54,200 J | ~800 ft-lbs |
| 50,000 ft-lbs | 25mm | 2,100 gr | 3,280 fps | 67,800 J | ~1,220 ft-lbs |

For scale, a .30-06 kicks about 20 ft-lbs. A muzzle brake cuts these recoil figures by 30 to 60 percent.

### Brass and Steel Penetrators

Brass and steel cores are full-size cores like real armor-piercing bullets, about 0.8 of the caliber wide and about 60 percent of the bullet's weight, inside a copper jacket. A thin needle of either metal bends or mushrooms on impact, so neither works in the adamantium needle shape.

**Brass core**
- Brass is only slightly harder than copper and nearly the same density. It gains about 10 to 15 percent penetration over solid copper.
- It holds its shape better against brick, stone and bone, so it penetrates deeper into unarmored targets and structures.
- It barely changes armor performance. Its value is cost and availability, since brass is easy to cast and machine in both eras.

**Hardened steel core**
- Roughly doubles copper's armor penetration at every tier. This is how real armor-piercing bullets like the .50 M2 AP and the 14.5mm B-32 work.
- Above roughly 3,000 fps, hardened steel can shatter against hard plate instead of punching through. The 30,000 to 50,000 tiers sit right at that edge, so steel cores lose some consistency against the best armor.
- Medieval smiths can harden steel cores. Victorian industry can mass-produce them.

**Steel core effects by tier**
- **10,000:** Defeats Renault FT front armor and A7V side armor. Punches through any WWI armored car.
- **20,000:** Defeats the 30 mm A7V front. Matches the WWII anti-tank rifles that killed early light tanks.
- **30,000:** Defeats early WWII Panzer III and T-26 front armor.
- **40,000:** Defeats WWII medium tank side armor at most angles.
- **50,000:** Defeats Sherman hull sides and flat 50 mm plate such as Panzer IV front armor.

### What Each Tier Does

**10,000 ft-lbs**
- **Copper:** Kills any land animal, including elephants. Goes through brick walls, car engine blocks and several men standing in a line. Defeats medieval plate armor, the knight inside and the horse behind him. Penetrates the side armor of a WWI British Mark IV tank (6 to 12 mm).
- **Needle:** Defeats the front armor of any WWI tank. Nearly defeats the wrought iron belt of HMS Warrior, the first British ironclad of 1860.

**20,000 ft-lbs**
- **Copper:** Defeats most WWI tank armor, armored cars and locomotive boilers. Similar in class to the WWII 14.5mm anti-tank rifles that killed early light tanks.
- **Needle:** Defeats a Victorian ironclad belt. Matches the 100 mm front of a WWII Tiger I.

**30,000 ft-lbs**
- **Copper:** Wrecks aircraft engines, small boat hulls and field gun shields. Borderline against the 30 mm front of the German A7V tank.
- **Needle:** Defeats most WWII medium and heavy tank front armor on flat plate.

**40,000 ft-lbs**
- **Copper:** Defeats the A7V front at close range. Breaches masonry walls and light bunker positions.
- **Needle:** Defeats nearly any WWII tank armor on flat plate.

**50,000 ft-lbs**
- **Copper:** Defeats WWII light tank fronts and the side armor of modern light armored vehicles.
- **Needle:** Approaches heavy WWII tank front armor even at moderate angles.

### How the Needle Changes Things

- On impact against armor, the copper flattens and strips away. The needle keeps going with its share of the energy focused on a tiny point. It never blunts, bends or shatters, so it outperforms steel and tungsten cores at the same weight.
- The hole it leaves is narrow, roughly 4 to 8 mm. Behind the armor it kills what it hits directly and throws some spall, but it does far less wide damage than a copper bullet that gets through.
- Against flesh, the copper still does the wounding. The needle separates and overpenetrates through anything behind the target.
- It ricochets much less than copper on angled plate, but steep angles can still deflect it.
- Since the needle is indestructible, it can be dug out of a wreck and reloaded into a fresh copper bullet.
